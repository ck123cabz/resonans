# Block 03: visuals that keep it digestible

Sources: Jason Murray's Art Direction Menu and matching-luggage rule, Nate Herk's retention practice (`knowledge/corpus/nate-herk/analysis/playbook.md`), and the HyperFrames kit in `content-studio/`.

## Rules

- **One anchor element.** One object stays on screen through segments 2 to 4 and changes state as the story moves. For one client it was a row of four boxes that fill during the problem and turn violet during the product.
- **One idea per beat.** Each piece of the problem gets a beat of 8 to 13 seconds, and several visuals play inside it.
- **A visual for every word that names something.** Each spoken noun, number, list item or step gets its own visual, landing on the word, and the visual shows the mechanism at work, never just its name. Christian asked for this at least six times over three rounds of review, and a blind reader dropped out at a static circle.
- **One focal point.** Park or clear the last element before the next one takes the frame. The anchor element gets parked, never cleared. Christian's note: "We park them to the side, so the attention is now on the TAM."
- **Nothing holds still past about 1.5 seconds** under the voice. When a beat shows a tool's output, the camera scrolls, steps or reveals through it. The old rule of a change every 4 to 6 seconds left frames frozen for whole sentences.
- **Labels only.** Words on screen are labels of four words or fewer; the voice carries the sentences.
- **Two palettes.** The problem is drawn in neutral ink and paper. The brand colour first appears when the product is named.
- **The problem in the product's own UI, drained.** Christian's call on 8 Oct 2026 for VSL 1: the problem scenes use the same app cards as the product, in white and grey with no brand colour, and the product scenes bring the colour back. Simple drawn shapes are the fallback when there is no product UI to borrow.
- **One visual world.** Every shot comes from the brand file's palette and type, with no stock photos.
- **The picture tells the story with the sound off.** Every line needs a picture that tells its part of the story without the voice: b-roll, a highlighted source, an explainer, an event on screen that shows the line, never a state that only sits there. A blind reader watching with the sound off checks it. On 13 Oct 2026 Christian called Why Nations Fail Ep 1 too narration-heavy: the voice carried the story and the picture only illustrated it.
- **An abstract visual earns its seconds.** It either previews something the viewer sees for real later, or it goes.

- **Show scale to scale.** People misjudge proportions; Christian's example is a wealth video where viewers guessed
  the top 1% held 50% more and the truth was 1,000%. Draw the real ratio: a map lit region by region for "most were
  farmers" and "most were tenants", a field of crops with half carted to the landlord, 3 vs 300 hectares as land
  you can see. The gap on screen is the feeling.
- **Guide attention with checkpoints.** Every new segment opens with a headline that names the question it answers,
  and a chapter marker stays on screen through the whole video. A recap scene parks into that marker so the viewer
  can see where they are at any time (Christian, 13 Oct: "the moment they come here, they get lost").
- **The hook frame is the thumbnail.** Its headline is the hookiest sentence in the video ("Mas mayaman pa ang
  Pilipinas kaysa South Korea nung 1960"), and it holds long enough to read. A chart that moves on before the
  viewer has read it loses them.
- **The picture says what the neutral line won't.** A script that reports plainly ("puwedeng pilitin magbenta
  kung lampas 300 hectares") leaves the verdict to the picture: 3 ha against 300 ha drawn so the unfairness is felt.
  Christian, 14 Oct: "the script is just simply explaining ... the visuals will invoke the emotions."
- **Let a dense frame breathe.** When a frame carries several facts, hold it until a slow reader is done; a fast
  transition after a busy frame was Christian's first note on WNF Ep 1 v6.

## Asset list

A table in the script file: shot, segment, source, status (exists, to build, check). Name the file or page each existing asset comes from.

## Sound

Name the sound for every script, from Jason Murray's three types:

- Mismatch: music that runs against the picture, such as calm piano over a hectic scene.
- Amplifier: music that pushes the picture's mood further.
- Sonic Signature: a short motif the brand repeats in every video.

Check the music licence before naming a track.
