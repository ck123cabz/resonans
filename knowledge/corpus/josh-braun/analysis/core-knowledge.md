## 1. Core Philosophy

The entire Braun corpus traces to one axiom: **illuminate, don't persuade.** Every framework, every technique, every anti-pattern connects back to this. `[D]` (5+ explicit statements, 80+ implicit reinforcements)

**The axiom, in Braun's words:**
"Your job isn't to talk people into buying. It's to illuminate a potential problem so prospects can determine if they'd like to continue the conversation. Some will. Some won't. Your role is to be genuinely curious and listen without having expectations." `[D]` `[D:vis]`

Four principles follow from this axiom:

**1. Buyers have the answers. Sellers have the questions.** `[D]` (35+ signals, the most-repeated single phrase in the corpus) The seller's role is to ask questions that let prospects convince themselves. "You're evoking what's already present, not installing what's missing." People believe what they hear themselves say, not what they're told.

**2. Detach from the outcome.** `[D]` (60+ signals across multiple formulations) Attached sellers push, steer, and tighten. Prospects feel it and pull away. Detached sellers ask neutral questions and listen without agenda. Braun calls the buyer-side sensation of being sold to by someone who needs the deal **"commission breath."** Nobody buys from someone with commission breath.

The pre-call mantra: "It's not my job to talk people into booking a meeting. My job is to illuminate a potential problem so prospects can determine if they'd like to continue the conversation. Some will. Some won't." `[D:vis]`

**3. You're not for everyone.** `[D]` (6+ signals) "Your grass-fed beef is for some people, but it's not for everyone. It's not your job to talk vegans into being meat eaters. Find meat eaters." Trying to convert everyone breeds anxiety and commission breath. The fix: find people who already have the problem you solve.

**4. Create conditions for self-persuasion.** `[D]` (8+ signals) People self-sell through a sequence: justify, rationalize, explain away objections, then inform you the decision is already made. "You didn't close them. They closed themselves." The seller's job: spark the idea, illuminate the cost of inaction, make space for it to grow, then step aside. "When people talk themselves into something, they don't return it."

## 2. Cold Call Methodology

### 2A. The Poke the Bear Call Structure

This is Braun's most documented methodology (25+ reinforcing signals). The six-step flow: `[D]` `[D:vis]`

**Step 1 — Mini Invitation (permission opener):**
"Hi, my name is [X]. I was wondering if you could help me out for a moment?" Then PAUSE. Alternative: "We've never spoken, and I know you weren't expecting my call, but I was hoping you could help me out for a moment." The mechanism: asking for help reframes the caller from salesperson to someone asking for assistance. Harder to refuse. `[D]` (5+ signals)

**Step 2 — Confirm right person:**
"Do you still handle [X]?" or "Are you still the one managing [Y]?" This is a System 1 question — easy, fast, doesn't trigger resistance. `[D]` `[D:vis]`

**Step 3 — Current-way question:**
"How are you getting [Y] done today — spreadsheets, automated, or something else?" This surfaces the status quo without judging it. The three-option structure (current way 1 / current way 2 / what's possible) invites honest disclosure. `[D]` (10+ signals)

**Step 4 — Poke the Bear:**
Ask a neutral question that highlights a potential problem the prospect didn't know they had. Be "crispy" (specific). Let them draw their own conclusions.

Examples across industries `[D]` `[D:vis]`:
- "How are you using that data to make sure you're not overpaying?" (fleet telematics)
- "Coffee machines have lots of little spots where bacteria like E. coli, staph, and strep can grow. How do you usually keep yours clean?"
- "Most annual physicals skip key heart disease markers like LDL particle size. How do you know if yours are in a healthy range?"
- "I often hear that once teams scale past 15 reps, call consistency drops. How are you keeping everyone on the same page?"

The question structure: industry-specific observation (often with a number or fact) + neutral question. The question never mentions the seller's product. `[D]` (40+ signals — the single most-referenced technique in the corpus)

**The psychology behind it:** George Loewenstein's curiosity gap — people feel compelled to resolve the gap between what they know and what they could know. Combined with autonomy: people resist when controlled, lean in when choosing freely. "The best way to persuade is to let others persuade themselves." `[D]` (October 2025 — the most complete theoretical explanation in the corpus)

**Step 5 — Presuppose Familiarity:**
"You've probably looked into automating it." Then shut up. If they have, peel the onion: "Sounds like the value wasn't there for you." If they haven't, you've planted curiosity. This move uses the correction instinct — prospects correct wrong assumptions, revealing information without interrogation. `[D]` (10+ signals)

**Step 6 — Meeting ask or graceful exit:**
If interest: "Seems like this might be worth exploring. Would it make sense to set aside 30 minutes?" If no interest: detach gracefully. "That's not a problem. If anything changes, I'm easy to find."

**The notecard test:** If your talk track doesn't fit on a notecard, it's too complicated. `[D]`

### 2B. Cold Call Openers

Eight named opener archetypes `[D]` `[D:vis]` (8+ signals):

| Opener | Example | Mechanism |
|--------|---------|-----------|
| The Helper | "Hoping you could help me out for a moment" | Lowers status frame, harder to refuse |
| The Transparency | "Full transparency, this is a cold call. Want to hang up or roll the dice?" | Disarms by naming the awkward thing |
| The Relevance | "I'm calling about the Inside Selling podcast. Do you still produce it?" | Answers "what's this about?" immediately |
| The Connection | Name-drop a mutual contact | Social proof shortcut |
| The Shortie | "Do you have a brief moment?" | Minimal footprint |
| The Bounce | "I was hoping to bounce an idea off you" | Frames as collaboration, not pitch |
| The Preemptive Empathy | "I know you weren't expecting my call but..." | Validates their likely feeling |
| The Gambler | "Want to roll the dice or hang up?" | Pattern interrupt through humor |

Core rule: tone matters more than words. Pick whichever feels natural. `[D]`

**Preemptive empathy** is a named sub-concept: acknowledging the interruption before the prospect raises it. "We've never met but..." / "I know you weren't expecting my call..." / "Full transparency, this is a cold call, but..." `[D]`

### 2C. Objection Handling (Join the Resistance)

The meta-principle: "Replace the word 'objection' with 'information.' Don't handle objections — join the resistance. Verbal aikido." `[D]` (21+ signals)

**The three-step response sequence:**
1. Pause two beats ("One Mississippi, Two Mississippi")
2. Acknowledge ("That's helpful to know" / "That's not a problem")
3. Label the real concern ("Sounds like budgets are reserved for existing vendors")
Then listen. Never fight, never rebut, never convince. `[D]`

**Specific objection responses** `[D]`:

**"I'm not interested"** — This is resistance, not an objection. "That's okay. I know you didn't ask for the call. Before we hang up, if I'm not overstepping, is it my timing, or is this not relevant?" Each clause serves a function: "That's okay" = no penalty; "I know you didn't ask" = restores autonomy; "timing or not relevant" = reframes rejection as neutral classification. `[D]` (word-by-word psychological breakdown available)

**"We don't have budget"** — Pause. "Seems like budgets are reserved for existing vendors." Mute. Then: "Not sure if this is for you, but some folks like to review options ahead of time, so when budget opens up, they already have a picture. Is that something you'd be open to?" This is temporal distancing — removing the pressure of "now." `[D]`

**"Send me an email"** — "Sure. So I don't do you a disservice by sending irrelevant information, mind if I ask you a couple of quick questions?" Turns a deflection into a discovery opportunity. `[D]`

**"Your price is too high"** — Don't defend or justify. Ask knowledge-gap questions that expose what cheaper quotes leave out: "Are they using hollow core or solid doors?" / "Are they putting down RedGard for moisture protection?" Each question reveals something the prospect hadn't considered. "Price is the last refuge when people don't see a meaningful difference." `[D]`

**"Is this a sales call?"** — Don't beg for time. Label their feeling: "Sounds like you get a lot of these calls." / "You probably think I'm just another salesperson trying to sell you something you don't need." `[D]`

**"How are you different?"** — Don't list differentiators. Empathize: "I'm sure you've seen a lot of similar options, all promising the same thing." Then redirect: "What does 'different' look like for you, given what you're trying to achieve but haven't yet?" `[D]`

### 2D. Elicitation (Late 2025 Addition)

Elicitation uses statements instead of questions to bypass the sales guard. The brain relaxes with statements but braces for questions. `[D]` (11+ signals)

**Mechanism:** Make an educated guess about the prospect's situation. If correct, they confirm and elaborate. If wrong, they correct — revealing information without interrogation. "People like correcting but don't like being corrected." `[D]`

**Examples:**
- "Sounds like that's your forever home" — prospect corrects ("well, I wouldn't say forever") and a conversation opens.
- "Your reps are probably crushing quota" — if wrong, they explain why.
- "So you've got layers of exceptions, different rates for roles, territories, and special spiffs stacked on top" — prospect leans in: "Exactly. That's our mess." `[D]`

The principle: "Statements open doors. Questions close them." Elicitation emerged as a named technique in August 2025 and runs parallel to Poke the Bear — not a replacement. Poke the Bear uses questions; elicitation uses statements that trigger corrections. Both bypass the sales filter. `[D]`

### 2E. Cold Call Craft

**Tone:** Smooth, calm, relaxed. Casual and conversational. 6/10 speed, 7/10 volume when the prospect says "What?" `[D]`

**Pacing:** Two-beat pause before responding to anything. Silence signals confidence and gives the prospect space. `[D]`

**Names:** First name only on cold calls. Omit last name and company name in the opener. `[D]`

**Encouragers:** Short verbal continuation signals ("Mm-hm," "Got it," "Right") paired with reflective fragments ("timing slipped?" / "sounds political?") followed by silence. Anti-patterns: don't stack them ("yeah yeah yeah") and don't grade the answer ("awesome!"). `[D]`

**Voicemail:** Pique curiosity with an open loop, don't pitch. The voicemail is a "movie trailer" for the follow-up email. Subject line of the follow-up email: "Voicemail." `[D]`

**"Burn the Popcorn"** anti-pattern: the conversation is going well but you keep asking questions instead of asking for the meeting. Recognize when to transition. `[D]`

## 3. Cold Email Methodology

### 3A. Email Anatomy (5-7 Element Framework)

Braun's cold email anatomy evolved from a flat checklist (2023) to a psychologically-sequenced framework (2025). The current canonical version has 5-7 elements depending on the email. All variants are compatible — different lenses on the same structure. `[D]` `[D:vis]` (15+ signals)

**The consolidated framework:**

1. **Trigger / Relevance** — "Why are you emailing me?" A specific observation about the prospect answering this question. Must be "crispy" (specific enough to be believable). Not decorative personalization. `[D]`

2. **Poke-the-bear question** — Illuminates a potential problem. Doesn't push, invites. Opens a curiosity loop. "How are you making sure reps don't get thrown off balance when a CHRO picks up?" `[D]`

3. **Knowledge gap / What's meaningfully different** — Introduces a blind spot the prospect isn't thinking about. Described as "crispy" (specific enough to be relatable). `[D]`

4. **Social proof / Third-party credibility** — Specific and not overhyped. "2,000+ fleets, $10M result." "4.8 rating. 209 reviews." `[D]`
   - **Cross-corpus note:** Braun uses named case studies in examples ("Kevin Trosian, CFO at Extensiv"). Eric's 500K-email dataset shows named proof underperforms at scale. **Reconciliation rule:** use named proof when TAM < 500 and the name is a genuine peer. Use generalized proof for at-scale campaigns.

5. **Preemptive objection handling** — Validates the prospect's current setup and gently introduces doubt. "You probably have a system in place that seems to be working, but even well-run ops can have hidden overpayments." Also expressed as "Not [objection]" (Brooklyn Best lemonade: "Not too sweet") or "No [friction]" ("No long-term contracts or tech"). `[D]`

6. **Low-pressure CTA** — Gauges interest rather than asking for time. "Think this might help?" / "Worth exploring?" / "Open to learning more?" / "Feel free to say no if you don't see a fit." `[D]` (30+ signals)

7. **(Optional) P.S.** — Personalization or humor callback. "P.S. I'll give you a call as well, but only when you least expect it." `[D]`

**Named framework variants:**
- **4-T Framework:** Trigger, Think, Third-party credibility, Talk? Subject under 3 words. Email under 75 words. (5+ signals, March 2024 onward)
- **5-Part Psychology:** Maps each section to the buyer's internal question: "Why me?" / "What's different?" / "Am I alone?" / "I trust my current vendor" / "No pressure." (April 2025)
- **10-Point Systems Check:** Priority-ordered checklist where ranking is the insight: subject line and problem specificity outrank social proof and personalization. `[D:vis]` (July 2023, earliest framework)
- **4-P Framework:** Problem, Premise, Payoff, Prompt. Simpler mnemonic for verbal delivery. (December 2025, latest variant)

### 3B. Sequence Architecture

**Max 4 emails per sequence** (evolved from 6-8 in 2023; 15+ signals confirm the shift to a hard cap of 4 by 2025). `[D]`

**One problem per email.** "Don't make all your chess moves at once." Each email in a sequence isolates a single, relevant problem. The anti-pattern: cramming multiple ideas into one email causes cognitive overload. `[D]` (20+ signals)

**Each email must be interesting on its own.** "If your message isn't interesting on touch #1, it won't magically become interesting on touch #14." High-touch cadences (8-14 touches across 7-28 days) are a symptom of bad messaging, not a strategy. Multi-touch sequences are "volume taxes you pay for being generic." `[D]` (5+ signals)

**No response is a response.** Stop when they don't engage rather than escalating touches. `[D]`

**Cadence:** 3-4 emails over a few weeks. Not 8+ touches in 7 days. `[D]`

### 3C. Subject Lines

**Under 3 words.** The subject line's only job is to get the email opened, not describe the product. `[D]` (8 signals)

**Patterns that work:**
- Two-word problem reference: "Walmart invoices," "Ironman Cozumel," "SDR job ad"
- "Via [Name]": leverages a mutual connection's first name. Ask permission first. `[D]`
- "Voicemail": bridges a voicemail to the follow-up email
- The Triplet (comedy Rule of 3): expected + expected + unexpected. "Abu Sayyaf + podcast + grandma." Must close the loop in the body or it's clickbait. `[D]`
- Problem-focused beats product-focused: "Miami project delay" over "Streamline project management"

### 3D. CTAs (Zone of Acceptance vs. Zone of Resistance)

**Zone of Resistance CTAs (avoid in email):** "Are you available for a brief call at {time}?" / "Can we sync up?" / "Do you have 30 minutes?" These ask for a commitment before interest is established. `[D]`

**Zone of Acceptance CTAs (use in email):**
- "Think this might help?"
- "Worth exploring?"
- "Open to taking a peek?"
- "Feel free to say no if you don't see a fit but... might this help?"
- "Is this something you've explored?"
- "Worth a chat?"
- "Open to learning more?"
- "Either way, rock the run." `[D]`

**Unconventional CTAs:** "Send me your best objection :)" / "Would it be a crime against humanity for you to see how they're doing it?" `[D]`

**Channel distinction:** On cold email, gauge interest (never ask for time). On cold calls, once the prospect is engaged via poke-the-bear and showing interest, ask for the meeting. Failing to ask on a call is the "burn the popcorn" mistake. `[D]` (This reconciles an apparent contradiction in the corpus.)

### 3E. Copy Mechanics and Editing Rules

**Word count:** Under 75 words per email. Earlier corpus (July 2023) said under 60. The 75-word benchmark is later and more frequent. `[D]`

**Tension note on email length:** Braun's default is brevity. But he acknowledges exceptions: "People don't have short attention spans. They have short interest spans." A longer email that demonstrates deep understanding of the prospect's world can outperform a short-but-generic one. The governing principle: relevance density matters more than word count. `[D]`

**"Crispy"** = Braun's signature term for hyper-specificity. When your observation, problem statement, or social proof is generic, it's dismissible. When it's specific ("unsupported version of Spotify API," "server timeouts requiring manual intervention," "35+ migrations"), it's credible without needing to prove anything else. Specificity IS the proof. Also described as "high resolution" vs "low resolution" copy. `[D]` (5+ signals)

**The "you/we" ratio:** 4:1. Four references to the prospect for every reference to yourself. `[D]`

**"Write with an eraser."** Strip to outcome only. Over-explaining kills. Specific edits: "as per" -> "per"; "ACME is able to" -> "ACME can"; "in order to" -> "to"; cut "I wanted to reach out"; replace round numbers with specific ones ("60X faster" -> "in 5 minutes instead of 5 hours"); delete self-serving claims ("we're the best / cutting edge"). `[D]`

**Casual tone beats professional tone.** If it sounds like a corporate memo, rewrite it. Abbreviations, shorter sentences, the way a person talks. `[D]`

**"Without" and "No" as power words.** Promises relief, not gain. "Train your dog to focus without yelling, punishing, or spending hundreds on obedience school." Speaks to pain avoidance. Addresses objections before the prospect voices them. "People don't want more. They want less." `[D]`

**The "You shouldn't have to..." formula.** Cold email first-sentence opener focusing on what prospects can stop doing. "Mark — You shouldn't have to sit through commercials when you watch TV." Each variant becomes a separate email in a sequence. `[D]`

**Before/after contrast framing.** "Most finance teams spend 20+ hours/month. Our clients slash that to 5 hours." Prospect doesn't know if 5 is good unless contrasted against 20. `[D]`

**The Waiting Tax.** Reframe gain as loss: "Earn 3.5% more" (gain frame) vs "Are you overpaying 3.5%?" (loss frame). Loss framing triggers action per Kahneman/Tversky prospect theory. `[D]`

**Simplicity Anchor.** Simple language = perceived as smarter and more trustworthy. Formula: "With A, you do B, so C happens, without D." `[D]`

**Email opener differentiation.** Avoid "I noticed / I saw / I'm reaching out because" (overused first-person openers). Use "It looks like you're... / It seems like you're... / Your job ad for X suggests..." to shift focus to the prospect. Relate the trigger to the problem you solve. `[D]`

### 3F. Copy Sourcing: "Good Cold Email Copy Isn't Written, It's Found"

This is Braun's methodology for where cold email language should come from. (20+ signals)

**JTBD Interviews:** Interview customers who recently switched — within 45 days. Reconstruct their decision timeline through seven phases: (a) Purchase/Switch, (b) First Thought, (c) Passive Looking, (d) Trigger to Act, (e) Considering Options, (f) Decision, (g) Using the Solution. Frame yourself as a journalist, not a seller. "Create space for your customer's story to unfold." `[D]` `[D:vis]`

**The Lingo Library:** A document where sellers collect exact customer phrases about what they did before switching. The sourcing method: JTBD interviews, testimonial mining, searching for the word "before" in case studies. "Write how your customers talk, not how you talk." Use customer words verbatim in cold emails. `[D]` (5+ signals)

**The Before Story:** Mine customer success stories for what sucked before switching. What follows "before" in any testimonial is the problem statement for your cold email opener. `[D]`

**Pain Mirror:** Lift copy directly from customer quotes rather than writing it. The customer's words describing their old pain become the email's opening lines. `[D]`

**"Personal but not personalized."** A prospecting message can be segment-specific (same email to all small business owners in a vertical) without being individually personalized. Research problems shared by a group, send the same problem-focused email to all of them. That's personal, not personalized. Researching individual hobbies and tying them to a pitch is "cosplay." `[D]` (3+ signals)

**Personalization tension note:** Braun advises segment-level relevance over individual personalization. But he also praises emails sent to him that reference his specific content. The reconciliation: personalization must connect to a problem or opportunity the prospect faces. Segment-level problem-awareness beats individual surface-level trivia. When personalization is "crispy" (tied to a real problem), it works. When it's decorative (iguanas in Boca Raton, the prospect's wine hobby), it backfires.

## 4. Discovery and Qualification

### 4A. Discovery Call Process

**The opening frame:** "Mind if I share what I was thinking? I'll ask questions, you might have some for me, and at the end we decide if there's reason to talk further. If not, that's okay too." `[D]` (18+ signals on discovery methodology)

**"Stay in the question."** Don't skip to the next question after a surface answer. Unpack each response before moving on. `[D]`
- "How are you handling X?" > "Spreadsheets" > "How's that been working?" > "Gets messy" > "Messy how?" > "Collaborating is a nightmare."
- Key moves: one-word mirrors ("Messy?"), follow-up questions ("How so?"), opinion-seeking questions ("What's your theory on why that is?")
- The anti-pattern is interrogation: asking unrelated questions in sequence.

**Three Levels Deep.** Go three levels deep on every answer instead of jumping to the next question. Level 1: "How long has this been going on?" Level 2: "What's your theory on why that is?" Level 3: "What's motivating you to look at this now?" `[D]`

**"What's your theory on why that is?"** Discovery question that boosts the prospect's competence and autonomy. Enlists them to diagnose their own problem rather than having the seller diagnose it. `[D]` (5+ signals)

**Twist the Knife.** After a prospect names a problem, don't solve it yet. Go deeper: "How's that affecting the team? Morale? Burnout?" Elevates a tech problem to a people/leadership/retention problem. "Big expensive problems are more urgent to solve." `[D]`

**Future pacing:** "Imagine we're having this conversation six months after the workshop; what has to have happened for you to feel you've made progress?" Creates productive tension between desired future state and current reality. `[D]`

**Summarize in their words:** "Based on my current understanding... [summary]. Did I miss anything?" Quote the prospect. Everyone likes to be quoted. `[D]`

**Surrender control at the end:** "Where would you like to go from here?" `[D]`

**Ten phrases that make prospects feel heard** `[D]`:
1. "Sounds like you have a reason for saying that."
2. "When you say X, can you give an example?"
3. "Seems like X is important to you."
4. "Could you go into more detail?"
5. "Could you elaborate on that?"
6. "What do you mean?"
7. "I'm not tracking."
8. "Tell me more."
9. "In what way?"
10. "How so?"

### 4B. Qualification and Disqualification

**"Selling to sorting."** Shift from trying to sell everyone to sorting for fit. "Selling is not helping" because the helping mindset assumes people need your help. Your actual role: illuminate a meaningfully different idea. It's a "potential" problem because it only becomes one if it matters to the prospect. `[D]` (3+ signals)

**Velvet Rope / Bring Up the Uglies First.** Vocalize your own disqualifiers before the prospect raises objections. "Before we go further, here are reasons I might not be a fit: No NDAs, payment upfront, $25-35k, can't guarantee results." 90% close rate on positive responders. `[D]`

**Traffic Light Rule (Three-Strike Rule).** Green = first cancellation, things happen. Yellow = second, proceed with caution. Red = third, stop. Send breakup email: "I might be misreading, but it seems like the timing is off." Then add to nurture and move on. `[D]` (3 signals)

**Urgency Scale (1-10).** "On a scale of 1-10, with 10 being mission critical and 1 being irritating but livable, where are you?" 7+ = keep talking. 4-6 = proceed with caution. 1-3 = red light. Combined with reverse-selling: if you can easily talk people out of switching, they're not ready to switch. `[D]`

**Fast-Forward Label.** When prospects ask for a proposal: "It sounds like if the proposal has what you want for the price we discussed, you're ready to move forward." Surfaces hidden blockers before investing effort in a proposal. `[D]`

**Four Essential Discovery Questions** `[D]`:
1. "Looking back 6 months, what has to have happened for you to feel really happy?"
2. "How do you measure success?"
3. "What has stopped you from resolving this?"
4. Scale 1-10, mission critical vs. irritating.

**Five Diagnostic Questions (before talking solutions)** `[D]`:
1. What are you measuring?
2. What is now?
3. What do you want it to be?
4. What's the value of the difference?
5. What have you tried?

### 4C. Price Transparency and Negotiation

**Tell price early, not after you "show value."** Withholding price destroys trust. `[D]` (15+ signals)

**Proactive script:** "I don't want to waste your time doing a demo only for us to realize we're too expensive. Would it be okay if I ran some numbers by you?" `[D]`

**After stating price, be silent.** Don't qualify ("but we can break that up into payments..."). They fill the space. `[D]`

**Silence as negotiation tool.** After hearing a price quote, say nothing. Braun's landscaper dropped from $25k to $21k without being asked, purely because Braun stayed quiet. `[D]`

**Calibrated question for discounting (Chris Voss):** "How am I supposed to do that when the assessment is the most time-intensive part of the project?" Turns a standoff into a shared problem. `[D]`

**"Your price is too high" decoded as three possible meanings** `[D]`:
1. They don't have the money — should have disqualified early.
2. They think they can get the same thing for less — you didn't illuminate what's meaningfully different.
3. They think the solution isn't worth the money — you didn't illuminate the cost of inaction.

## 5. Buyer Psychology

### 5A. Zone of Resistance (ZOR)

A named psychological state prospects enter when they detect sales pressure. `[D]` (40+ signals, including implicit references)

**The department store analogy:** A customer says "No thanks, I'm just looking" even when they came in to buy. That reflexive response to sales pressure is the ZOR. `[D:vis]`

**Hard resistance vs. soft resistance** `[D]`:
- Hard resistance (budget mismatch, wrong fit) = join the resistance, don't push against it.
- Soft resistance (uncertainty, info-seeking) = seek understanding through labeling and questions.

**ZOR-triggering phrases and replacements** `[D]` `[D:vis]`:
- "Who's the decision-maker?" -> "Aside from you, who else needs to weigh in on this?"
- "I haven't heard back from you" -> "Have you decided to defer the project?"
- "Does that make sense?" -> "How does that sound to you?"
- "What's your budget?" -> "Is there a ballpark range you're trying to stay within?"
- "Can I be honest?" -> "Mind if I'm direct?"

**ZOR-lowering vocabulary:** Softeners ("potentially," "perhaps," "might," "I'm not sure if this applies, but..."), labels ("sounds like..."), easy outs ("either way"). `[D]`

### 5B. Self-Determination Theory and Autonomy

Autonomy is a basic human need. "When salespeople bring swords, prospects raise their shields." Traditional rebuttal scripts strip prospects of agency. "People don't resist change — they resist being changed." `[D]` (8+ signals)

The fix: shift from controlling to understanding. Surrender control rather than seize it. Every technique in the Braun system — softeners, labels, permission-based CTAs, "where would you like to go from here?" — preserves the prospect's sense of choice. `[D]`

### 5C. Curiosity Gap and Zeigarnik Effect

**Curiosity gap (George Loewenstein):** People feel compelled to resolve the gap between what they know and what they could know. This is why Poke the Bear questions work — they create an open loop the brain wants to close. `[D]` (6 signals)

**Zeigarnik Effect:** People remember unfinished tasks better than completed ones. This is why the "deferred" follow-up email works — "Have you deferred the cold call workshop?" leverages the brain's need for closure. `[D]`

### 5D. JTBD Four Forces of Buying

Every switching decision involves four forces `[D]` (9 signals):
- **Push** = current way might not cut it anymore
- **Pull** = new way looks attractive
- **Inertia** = "I'd rather dance with the devil I know"
- **Anxiety** = "Will people use it? Will it work? Will I lose my job?"

Change happens when push + pull > inertia + anxiety. Until then, nurture without pressure. "You can't make fruit grow faster because it's the end of Q4." `[D]`

Buying readiness is driven by changing circumstances, not seller messaging. A broken grill side doesn't create buying intent (still cooking for 2). Hosting a party for 10 creates the gap. The seller's role in the interim: stay top of mind through value, not through pressure. `[D]`

**"There's no such thing as 'no decision.'" The prospect decided to wait.** `[D]`

### 5E. Loss Aversion and Cost of Inaction

"You cannot create urgency, but you can illuminate the cost of inaction." The plumber example: today's $160 fix vs. future $500+ fix. "It's up to you." `[D]` (6+ signals)

Loss framing outperforms gain framing (Kahneman/Tversky). "Earn 3.5% more" vs. "Are you overpaying 3.5%?" The question "What does it cost your prospect to wait?" reframes inaction as an ongoing cost. `[D]`

Urgency is discovered, not manufactured. "Price becomes the last refuge when the prospect sees no meaningful difference." `[D]`

### 5F. Pratfall Effect and Transparency

When a competent person shows a small flaw, they become more likable and trustworthy. "Flaunt your flaws." `[D]` (7 signals)

"Perfect feels like an ad. People are skeptical of ads. Imperfect feels relatable." `[D]`

Buckley's cough syrup outsold competitors spending 4x more on ads with the tagline "It tastes awful. And it works." Outsold Benylin and Robitussin ($2M budgets) with $522K budget. `[D:vis]`

"Telling what your product doesn't do builds more trust than what it does." This connects to the "tell the whole story" principle. `[D]`

Proactive negatives in copy: "Not [objection]" formula. Brooklyn Best lemonade: "Not too sweet." If the main objection your buyers have is known, address it before they raise it. `[D]`

### 5G. Elicitation and the Correction Instinct

"People like correcting but don't like being corrected." Mislabeling triggers correction, revealing information without interrogation. `[D]` (11 signals)

When you make a deliberate statement (even a wrong one), the prospect's correction instinct kicks in. Labeling negatives defuses negatives (Chris Voss). "Seems like..." / "Sounds like..." / "Feels like..." stems verbalize an educated guess about what the prospect is thinking. If you mislabel, they correct you — and the correction contains the real information. `[D]` (17+ signals on labeling, mirroring, calibrated questions)

**Emotional Echoing (distinct from Voss labeling):** Says what's NOT being said — names the feeling underneath the words. "Seems like you're unsure this will actually get used by the team." / "Sounds like you tried something like this before and it didn't go well." Parroting ("Sounds like you want to think about it") is the anti-pattern. `[D]`

### 5H. Status Dynamics

Meeting requests trigger two defenses: loss of status ("show you" puts the prospect in a student chair they didn't ask for) and loss of autonomy (calendar commitment corners them into yes/no). `[D]`

**Equal vs. Low-Status Communication** `[D]` (4+ signals):
- Low: "Thank you for picking up my call, I really appreciate it." Equal: "I'm calling about the Inside Selling podcast. Do you still produce it?"
- Low: "I'll ask my boss if we can lower the price." Equal: "It sounds like you're ready to move forward if the price is X."
- Low: "Yes of course, I'll send you a proposal right away." Equal: "Sounds like if the proposal has what you want for the price we discussed, you're ready to move forward."

"Confidence is contagious. Equal stature creates honest discussion." `[D]`

### 5I. Additional Psychological Principles

**Choice of Two Tokens** (Jean-Louis Gassee via Jason Fried): When dealing with someone upset, whichever token you pick ("it's not a big deal" or "it's the end of the world"), they choose the other. If you minimize their problem, they escalate. If you take it more seriously than they do, they relax. `[D]`

**Social Loafing:** When prospects feel they have no unique contribution to make, they feel little responsibility to respond. Good cold emails overcome this by highlighting what drew you to THIS person rather than someone else. `[D]`

**Cognitive Dissonance (Sour Grapes):** When prospects say "We don't need another tool," it's often the mind rewriting the story to avoid the discomfort of change. Lower the tension with elicitation, normalize the problem, assume competence, leave an open loop. `[D]`

**Altercasting:** When you give someone a positive label, you cue them to live up to it. "You're always really responsive" makes them stay responsive. Must be genuine. `[D]`

**Four Types of False "Yes"** `[D]`:
1. "Yes to keep the peace" — polite yes from rapport
2. "Yes to make it stop" — relief yes from a busy person
3. "Yes under pressure" — eager tone framed yes as only option
4. "Yes without tension" — no real curiosity or problem worth solving
Fix: better tension (poke the bear), not better persuasion.

## 6. Anti-Patterns

### 6A. Phrases to Avoid

Master list of phrases that trigger the Zone of Resistance, compiled from 22+ signals across the corpus `[D]`:

| Phrase | Why it fails | Replace with |
|--------|-------------|-------------|
| "I haven't heard back from you" | Feels like blame | "Have you deferred the project?" |
| "What keeps you up at night?" | Lazy, overused, condescending | Poke the bear with a specific problem |
| "Does that make sense?" | Condescending — implies they might not understand | "How does that sound?" / "What's your take?" |
| "Are you the decision maker?" | Diminishes the prospect | "In addition to yourself, who else gets involved?" |
| "I'd love to..." | Centers seller's desire = commission breath | Drop entirely or rephrase as prospect-centric |
| "Just out of curiosity..." | Fake-casual filler | Cut the preamble |
| "Just checking in" / "Bumping this" | Withdrawal with no value | Send a deposit (article, calculator, insight) |
| "That's a great question!" | Stalling, condescending | Answer the question |
| "I understand how you feel. Many felt the same way. But what they found was..." | Feel-Felt-Found — explicitly called manipulative by Braun | "It seems like you have some concerns." |
| "That's exactly why I'm calling" | Dismissive, universal plug-in | Address the specific concern |
| "Can I have 27/37 seconds?" | Begging for time | Ask a question worth their time |
| "How are you today?" (on cold calls) | False familiarity — you're strangers | State purpose: "I'm calling about X" |
| "If I could 10X your revenue, would you be interested?" | Leading the witness | Ask a neutral poke-the-bear question |
| "To be honest" / "Honestly" / "Frankly" | Implies prior dishonesty | "Mind if I'm direct?" |
| "We will increase revenue / save you time / reduce cost" | Generic, unfalsifiable claims | Specific, crispy results |
| "Trust me" | Says you can't | Demonstrate trustworthiness through transparency |
| "Sound fair?" | Manipulative contract language | "How does that sound?" |

### 6B. Structural Anti-Patterns

**Aggressive cadences / "Siege cadence."** 8-14 touches across 7-28 days. "If your message isn't interesting on touch #1, it won't magically become interesting on touch #14." High-touch cadences are a symptom of bad messaging, not a strategy. `[D]`

**Sandler-style upfront contracts on first calls.** "If you have no further interest we can part ways, but if you do, let's have another meeting. Does that sound fair?" Braun: sounds like a date saying "if this goes well, we can discuss next steps." `[D]`

**Connect-and-pitch on LinkedIn.** Withdrawal without deposit. The message asks for time without having earned any social capital. `[D]`

**AI-generated cold email that checks all the boxes but has no soul.** The FlashIntel example: generic flattery ("I saw your post about using ChatGPT"), vague value prop ("automating personalized outreach"), corporate language ("streamline your efforts"). Structurally correct. Zero personality. `[D]`

**Decorative personalization.** "Saw you're based in Boca Raton — seen the iguanas?" Braun distinguishes this from relevant personalization: "Saying something nice to cash it in later is called manipulation." `[D]` (3 signals)

**Triple Tap Method.** Call once, let it ring 3 times, hang up, call back, hang up, call back a third time. Then lie about why. "This is what happens when your intent is to push for a conversation at all costs." `[D]`

**Disney-Character Sales Voice.** The over-bright, over-eager, over-scripted tone. "People don't reject your pitch first. They reject your energy." `[D]`

**Premature Pitching Syndrome (PPS).** Pitching your solution before establishing a problem. "Your solution has no value without a problem, so it's madness to pitch at the jump." Cure: "Move off the product. Move into the problem." `[D]`

**Parroting disguised as labeling.** Prospect says "I want to think about it." Seller says "Sounds like you want to think about it." That's parroting. Real labeling: "You don't want to rush into a decision you might regret." `[D]`

**Misleading subject lines.** "Re: contract" on a first-time cold email. Local presence dialing to fake caller ID. Pre-checked opt-in boxes. Negative-option billing. `[D]`

**Leading questions.** "If I could 10X your revenue would you be interested?" / "Is breathing air a priority for you?" These steer prospects toward a desired answer and trigger the Zone of Resistance. `[D]`

**Shame-based follow-ups.** "I haven't heard back from you. Do you have any questions? I think you're wasting your potential." Shame triggers defensiveness and resentment, not engagement. `[D]`

**Treating intent signals as inbound leads.** These are NOT inbound: attended a webinar, downloaded an ebook, visited your website, has a lead score above X, liked/commented on social. An actual inbound lead: filled out a demo request form. `[D]`

### 6C. Mindset Anti-Patterns

**"Wanting people to buy" leads to salesy behavior.** The intent to book meetings with everyone produces pushy behavior. Neurons that fire together wire together. `[D]`

**"Selling is helping" mindset.** Leads to presuming people need your help, which is presumptuous. The fix: selling is sorting. `[D]`

## 7. Follow-Up and Prospecting Strategy

### 7A. Deposits Before Withdrawals

Withdrawals: "Just checking in" / "Bumping this to the top of your inbox" / "Any thoughts?" Subtext: "I have nothing valuable but I need something from you." `[D]` (12+ signals)

Deposits: Send articles, calculators, relevant tools, personal callbacks to prior conversations, intros. "Withdrawals say 'notice me.' Deposits say 'I noticed you.'" `[D]`

**"Checking in" vs. "Checking back":** Checking back references something personal from the last conversation. `[D]`

**Ghost Recovery / "Deferred" Email:** One-sentence follow-up: "Have you deferred the cold call workshop?" Leverages the Zeigarnik Effect. Keep to 8-10 words. `[D]` (5 signals)

**Longer ghost recovery template:** "Hi James — It seems like you're putting [product] on hold, which is not a problem. Perhaps somewhere along the way, I dropped the ball. Here's my ask, and I know it's a big ask because you don't owe me a response. Would you be open to sharing some feedback so we can better serve you should a need arise in the future? Either way, thanks for considering [company]." `[D]`

**Stay-top-of-mind (TOM) sequence:** After a non-response, add the prospect to educational emails about topics they care about. No chasing, no needy emails. `[D]`

### 7B. Prospecting Strategy

**Bottom-up prospecting.** Contact direct reports first, gather intel, then reference names and insights when reaching out to the decision-maker. Braun cracked GEICO and Comcast this way. `[D]`

**Physical mail as channel arbitrage.** "Most prospects get 100+ emails/day. How many get a letter in a green envelope? Zero." No spam filters, no swipe-to-delete. "Prospecting is about showing up differently, not shouting louder." `[D]`

**The "Costco sample" approach.** Do the work first, then show proof. Send an unsolicited sample of your work instead of pitching. "The best pitch isn't a pitch at all. It's a sample." `[D]`

**22 Revenue Plays ranked by proximity to revenue.** Cold outreach is dead last (#22). The first seven are warm: re-engage existing customers, upsell, improve demo-to-win rate, re-engage churned, closed-lost, referrals, network intros. **Important philosophical difference with Eric/Nick:** Braun views cold outreach as the last resort in a full sales system. Eric and Nick build businesses entirely on cold outreach as the primary engine. Both are valid for their contexts. For pipeline strategy, Braun's warm-first hierarchy informs prioritization. For execution once cold is the chosen channel, Eric/Nick methodology governs. `[D]`

**Mise en place.** From the culinary world: prep work before cooking. Prepare 40 people to call the night before, so morning hours are spent dialing not researching. `[D]`

**List Quality Levels (1-8).** Progressive narrowing from Level 1 ("Contact everyone") to Level 8 (stacked intent signals: role + team size + hiring + tool usage + recent content + timing). "At Level 8, you're not pushing an idea. You're joining an existing conversation." `[D]`

**Trigger Events** (seven categories) `[D]`:
1. New Technology
2. Area of Interest
3. Open Positions / hiring
4. Regulation / Legal
5. Uncommon Commonality
6. Former Customer who Switched Companies
7. Events / Conferences

### 7C. Winning the Day

Baseline routine of 5-10 non-negotiable daily activities you control. Score yourself: 8+/day = won the day, 40+/week = won the week. Activities include: call 35 people, send 20 cold emails, listen to recorded calls, 10-minute walk. "A good day isn't about what you can't control." `[D]`

## 9. Labeling, Mirroring, and Calibrated Questions (Chris Voss Techniques)

These appear throughout the corpus as the primary toolkit for objection handling, discovery, and conversation deepening. Braun attributes them to Chris Voss and applies them extensively with his own modifications. `[D]` (17+ signals — the third most-referenced technique cluster)

**Labeling:** Verbalize an educated guess about what the prospect is thinking. Stems: "Seems like..." / "Sounds like..." / "Feels like..." If you mislabel, they correct you — revealing the real information. "People like correcting but don't like being corrected." `[D]`

**Mirroring:** Repeat the last 1-3 words with a questioning inflection. "Spreadsheets?" / "No sponsors?" Opens space for elaboration. `[D]`

**Calibrated questions:** "How am I supposed to do that when...?" Forces the other party to solve your problem. Used primarily in negotiation. `[D]`

**Braun's modifications to Voss:**
- Emotional echoing (distinct from labeling): names the feeling underneath the words, not the words themselves.
- Elicitation: makes statements (educated guesses) to trigger corrections, rather than asking questions.
- Braun explicitly rejects no-oriented questions on cold calls (P2-B5-32), despite Voss recommending them. He uses them only in negotiation contexts.

**"Why" questions replaced with labels:** "Why isn't the team prospecting more?" triggers defensiveness. "It seems like the team's been focusing more on closing deals than prospecting" invites collaboration. "Why is like shining a spotlight in their eyes. 'It seems like' is like holding a flashlight, gently guiding the way." `[D]`

## 11. Cross-Corpus Integration Notes

**Where Braun adds to Eric/Nick:**
- Cold call methodology (Eric has zero, Nick has validation but no tactics)
- Psychology of buyer resistance (ZOR, self-determination theory, curiosity gaps)
- Discovery and consultative selling (post-reply conversations)
- Negotiation (Voss techniques with worked transcripts)
- Physical mail as outreach channel
- Copy sourcing through JTBD interviews and Lingo Libraries

**Where Braun's frameworks assume conditions Eric/Nick do not:**
- Braun assumes offer-market fit exists. Eric puts it at 30% agency / 70% client offer. Nick says 80% of results depend on the client's offer. The GTM Agent's offer-evaluation logic comes from Eric and Nick, not Braun.
- Braun's email examples sometimes include links, named case studies, and emails over 75 words. These may violate Eric's operational rules (no links in E1, generalized proof, under 75 words). When the Copywriter Agent generates at-scale campaigns, Eric's constraints govern. When generating 1:1 or small-TAM outreach, Braun's approach applies.
- Braun provides no infrastructure guidance (deliverability, domain management, warm-up). That comes entirely from Nick and Eric.

**Reconciled contradictions:**
- **Touch count:** Braun says max 4. Eric says 2-3. Nick says 2. All agree: short sequences. Use Eric/Nick for at-scale campaigns. Braun's 4-email max applies to 1:1 or small-TAM outreach.
- **Named vs. generalized proof:** Braun uses named case studies. Eric's data shows named proof underperforms at scale. Rule: named proof for TAM < 500 with genuine peers. Generalized proof for everything else.
- **Assertive vs. non-assumptive framing:** Braun defaults to "might/potentially" (30+ signals) but deliberately used "is losing sales" in his Challenger email. Rule: default to softened language. Switch to assertive only when strong evidence supports the claim.
