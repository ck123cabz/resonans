## Pricing ladder

**When to use:** Quoting design work or advising on rate progression.

Three models, in order of maturity. Hourly is the entry model he tells you to graduate off, since incentives oppose: the designer wants more hours billed, the client wants fewer. US-market hourly bands: Junior (1-2 years) $20-30/hr, Intermediate (after successful client projects) $35-50/hr, Senior/Expert $60-100/hr. Rates are location-bound; an average designer in India earns ~$12/hr against ~$50/hr US.

Project-based pricing starts from break-even: estimated hours times hourly rate. Then gross up by client type. High-value or big-name client, charge break-even flat, because portfolio prestige and referrals are the real margin. Middle-of-the-road client, break-even x 1.5. Boring or low-value client, break-even x 2.

Value-based pricing is his "Holy Grail": charge 5-10% of the additional profit or revenue the redesign generates. Worked example: a redesign that lifts a $2M company's revenue 10% ($200K) justifies a $10,000-$20,000 fee regardless of hours spent.

## The 4 Levels as productized tiers

**When to use:** Scoping or pricing a SaaS landing page, or placing an existing site on the quality ladder before a redesign pitch.

Each tier carries a market price and observable craft markers, so you can price from a screenshot.

- **Level 1, The Template, $500** (he scores it 2/10): repetitive text-left/image-right alternation, generic stock photos, vague copy, flat menus, few active design decisions.
- **Level 2, The Visual Identity, $2,000** (6/10): stacked hero, real product screenshots instead of stock, shorter headlines, outlined CTAs, simple load animations, cohesive brand.
- **Level 3, The Crafted Experience, $6,000-$10,000** (8/10): the shift from UI to product design. Bento grids, mega menus, clickable multisections, zoomed-in product shots curating what the user sees.
- **Level 4, The Masterpiece, above $10,000** (10/10): immaculate page flow, high-end motion (blur transitions, mega menus that bump out sideways), hover-only CTAs, outcome-focused copy: "turn data into decisions," not "analyzing data."

## Tension: artifact price vs outcome price

**When to use:** Any time both frameworks apply to the same quote.

Note the unresolved tension in the corpus. Tier pricing prices the artifact (what craft went into the page); value-based pricing prices the outcome (what the page earns the client). Kole never reconciles the two. Practical resolution for quoting: treat the craft tier as the floor and the value calculation as the ceiling. A Level 3 build for a $2M company starts at $6-10K on craft and can defensibly run to $20K if the ROI math supports it.

## Portfolio path with no clients

**When to use:** Building a portfolio from zero, no client work to show.

Build realistic fake projects. Generate a unique prompt from Good Brief or Sharpen Design, pick a passion niche (never "websites for everyone"), then have ChatGPT expand the prompt into a full project brief. Pull inspiration from Dribbble, Pinterest, Instagram, Behance. Never use Lorem Ipsum anywhere; real or AI-generated corporate text makes the project feel production-ready. You only need 2-3 case studies, or 3-4 polished interface results, to land a job.

Platform call: a personal website beats Dribbble/Behance for professionalism, with Notion sites as the quick fallback that still feels personal. Present final UI in mockups (Apple device frames, AI-generated lifestyle scenes) and push past displaying a website toward proof of concept with numerical evidence the design solved the problem.

Timing rule: don't start the portfolio in the first two months. Taste changes so fast early on that an immediate portfolio gets scrapped later.

**Provenance:** [S] (S95, S96, S109)

## The 8-step UX case study

**When to use:** Writing or reviewing a case study; each step has a known failure mode to check against.

1. **Problem + solution snapshot up top.** Failure: a wrong or non-existent problem, no summary.
2. **High-end visual presentation** with images, GIFs, graphs carrying titles, axis labels, annotations, legends. Failure: great design wrapped in a garbage-looking case study nobody reads.
3. **Data-backed personas**, goals and frustrations only. Failure: backstory bloat, obsessing over photos and names.
4. **User flows in FigJam**, distinct shapes and colors per page, decision, and input. Failure: scoping too big; small projects tell better stories.
5. **Low-fi wireframes** tested on real people. Failure: skipping the stage entirely; imperfect drawings add value.
6. **Final design**, large high-detail images plus written reasoning. Failure: squishing the images.
7. **Reflection** that admits difficulties and what you'd improve. Failure: omitting flaws, which reads less genuine.
8. **Storytelling style**: chapter-like titles, a light-bulb moment, 10-15 minute read cap, images over long paragraphs.

Cross-cutting tells that sink the whole thing: Lorem Ipsum, over-designed charts (curved data lines, rounded bar tops hide values), and inconsistency across screens, specifically mismatched icons and varying corner radii.

**Provenance:** [S] (S97, S98, S99, S100)

## Portfolio models

**When to use:** Picking a portfolio archetype or auditing site structure.

Two named models. Emily Shen is the gold-standard case study, readable without a single word: solution snapshot up top, GIF-heavy skimmable steps, labeled and annotated graphs, honest reflection section. Luca Schultz is "eat with your eyes first": full-width flashy imagery, bright color, text secondary, and heavy curation, zooming way in on one dashboard section to show craft instead of showing everything.

Site rules: headshot above the fold (a video intro is even stronger); concise intro stating name and what you do; 2-3 pages max (Home, Contact, sometimes About); a genuine About page with personal stories; a relatable quirk (Robert K's "fueled by IKEA plants"). Presentation hacks: explode the image so UI lines run off-screen, device mockups or AI-generated lived-in scenes over plain screenshots, working prototypes instead of describing interactions.

## Landing clients

**When to use:** Sales pitches, positioning, and upsell framing.

The one change that lands clients: stop displaying finished websites, start presenting proof of concept and case studies. "Presenting undeniable results of what you can achieve is the single best sales pitch." Selling structure: define the specific problem, list the services provided, present the solution with numerical evidence wherever possible.

Niche by problem, not industry. "Expert at reorganizing large product-collection sites to make them intuitive" beats "designer for dentists." Every pitch reduces to ROI, small fee in, large revenue out, and most business problems reduce to needing more customers. Build for the end user rather than the client's taste; fast loads and smooth checkout beat flashy graphics. Upsell SEO and CRO as outcome services, since on high-traffic sites a marginal CRO gain means a large revenue lift. Ship one main CTA, the way Google and Apple do.

## The 28-day learning plan

**When to use:** Structuring a beginner's first month, or advising someone starting from zero.

Week 1, train the eye, no Figma yet: build a swipe file of ~20 hero and feature sections from Mobin, Dribbble, or Pinterest, then annotate a few sites, marking the four zones (main text block, navbar, stats, image) plus spacing, hierarchy, groupings.

Weeks 2-3, cloning: clone one loved hero section exactly (forces noticing padding and font sizes), clone that same site's feature section to learn layout transitions, then clone a second site with a completely different layout for range.

Week 4 splits the craft. UI "flavor" exercise: restyle a cloned layout twice, once warm and friendly (rounded corners, bright colors), once modern and premium (darker tones, subtle noise). UX exercise: design the screens where an onboarding journey splits, an "invite your team" page for a manager against a setup page for a solo user.

Standing rules across the month: no portfolio yet, and quantity over perfection, since quality is bred from high-volume practice.

## Career mindset

**When to use:** Coaching tone, motivation content, or framing advice for early-career designers.

The "6 Things You Probably Need to Hear": 1) your designs suck at first, iteration and quantity breed quality; 2) there are more colors than the saturated hex-picker corners, so mine muted tones, nature palettes, artworks; 3) getting hired is harder than you think, so ask "if I was the one hiring me, would I?"; 4) less is more, one primary CTA like Apple and Google; 5) design doesn't need 3D effects or heavy animation if they slow the site; 6) good ideas don't exist in a vacuum, mood board before opening Figma.

Beyond the list: graduate from pretty UIs to product design, the whole house rather than decorating a room. Kill the happy-path fallacy by designing every state (empty, loading, success, error). Pattern recognition is the edge; the four-zone skeleton repeats everywhere, and cloning professional work internalizes it fastest.
