## 1. The Decision Tree: How Offer Level Determines Everything

The Offer Level framework is the first question the agent must answer before writing a single word of copy. The market position of the service determines the CTA strategy, copy aggressiveness, follow-up cadence, and offer positioning. Everything downstream flows from this diagnosis. `[D]`

### Offer Levels

**Level 1 (High Demand):** `[D]` Prospects are actively looking for this service. Lead gen, podcast placements, commission-only closers. The market wants what you sell. Copy can pitch a call directly. You can lead with the feature itself because the market wants that mechanism. The one-sentence email works well here. Personalization is optional when the offer is strong enough.

**Level 2 (Latent Demand / Saturated):** `[D]` Prospects know they need it but get pitched daily. Email marketing, social media management, paid ads. Copy must lead with guarantees, risk reversals, concrete case studies, specific revenue numbers, and proven track records. Lead with benefits, not features, because the feature is commoditized. The case study angle and direct pitch email types perform best here.

**Level 3 (No Perceived Demand):** `[D]` Prospects believe they don't need it. Web design, SEO, Facebook ads in certain markets. Copy must hide the pitch behind free upfront value or a lead magnet. The Trojan Horse strategy was designed for this level. You cannot pitch directly because there's no existing desire to activate.

**Level 4 (Emerging Market):** `[V]` Prospects don't understand the category yet. Lead with education. This was added in Christian's 2025-2026 LinkedIn content as an expansion of the original framework.

Christian on LinkedIn: "This is why copying 'winning templates' is killing your campaigns." The template that crushed it for a Level 1 service will flatline for a Level 3 service because the prospect's relationship to the offer is fundamentally different. `[V]`

### Features vs. Benefits: The Offer Level Override `[S]`

Standard marketing says "always lead with benefits." Christian's framework overrides this:
- Level 1 (trendy/high-demand): pitch the feature itself. TikTok ads services can just say "TikTok ads" because the market wants that mechanism.
- Level 2 (saturated): pitch tangible benefits. Facebook ads is commoditized, so "15 sales calls per month" beats "we run Facebook ads."
- Broad/full-service offers: break the service into granular sub-features on paper. A/B test pitching individual sub-features to find which angle the market responds to. Sell the full stack on the phone.

### Market Sophistication Layer `[V]`

Layered on top of the Offer Levels is a market sophistication assessment:
- **Saturated market** (Facebook ads) = lead with results, case studies, risk reversals
- **New market** (TikTok strategy) = mentioning the channel gets attention, lead with innovation
- **Mature market** = lead with differentiation from the status quo
- **Emerging market** = lead with education

## 2. Named Strategies

### The 1-2 Punch `[D]`

Boxing terminology. Two-phase outreach where Email 1 and Email 2 have completely different jobs.

**The Jab (Email 1):** Short, concise, deliberately vague feeler. Personalization line plus a one-sentence question or pitch. No case studies. No guarantees. No mechanism explanation. The shortest email in the sequence, often 7-12 words of body copy. Its only job is to get a reply.

**The Right Hook (Email 2):** All ammunition deployed. Hard CTA, quantifiable guarantees, case studies, deeper mechanism explanation. This is where you make the actual pitch. Still under 50 words, but substantively heavier than the Jab.

The insight is counterintuitive: withhold your best material from Email 1. Most people put their strongest pitch first. Christian inverts this. The Jab establishes contact; the Right Hook closes. Reported result: 1,000+ calls booked.

The 1-2 Punch can contain a Trojan Horse in Email 2. When the Jab gets a response, the Right Hook can be a free video audit rather than a written pitch.

**The four email types that map into the 1-2 Punch:** `[V]`
1. **The One-Sentence Email** (the Jab). Yes/no question, binary response trigger.
2. **The Loom Video Pitch** ("Mind if I share a quick video?"). Works as both a Jab and a follow-up CTA.
3. **The Case Study Angle** (lead with specific results). Best as a Right Hook.
4. **The Direct Pitch** (state offer + result, ask to talk). Best for Level 1 offers.

After 10M+ emails, Christian says only these four types work. Test 3-4 angles per type, giving you 12-16 email variations to run.

### The Trojan Horse `[D]`

Pose as a consumer browsing the prospect's front-facing assets (website, ads, newsletter). Point out a vague but alarming problem hurting their conversions. Offer a free video audit.

**Two-line structure:**
1. "I was checking out [Company]'s website/ads..."
2. "...noticed a few things hurting your conversions. Mind if I share a quick video explaining further?"

**Critical rule:** Do NOT record the video until the prospect replies yes. This is an efficiency optimization. You only invest production time on prospects who've already demonstrated interest.

**Applicability constraint:** Only works for front-facing services where you can observe the prospect's public assets. Web design, paid ads, email marketing, SEO, ecommerce conversion. Cannot work for backend services like CRM, IT infrastructure, or sales management.

**Evidence:** A Trojan Horse Loom pitch closed a $25K web design deal. Script (~39 words): "Hi [Name], I was looking at FFLs in Florida and found your company's website. I noticed a few things about your eCommerce store that might be hurting your conversion rate. Mind if I share a quick video explaining further?" `[S]`

### The Billboard Strategy `[D]`

The governing principle for follow-up emails: never change the core offer or messaging between follow-ups. The offer stays constant. The framing rotates.

Named after a billboard: the same ad, same message, repeated until it sticks. Changing your guarantee or introducing a new service mid-sequence confuses prospects and resets trust.

**Where this gets nuanced:** The NLM corpus describes follow-ups as "near copy-paste" of Email 1's hook. `[S]` But Christian's LinkedIn content (2025-2026) says each follow-up must "give a new reason to respond" and adds new information. `[V]` The reconciliation: the *offer* is constant, but the *angle on the offer* rotates. Email 2 might reframe the economics. Email 3 might add proof. The underlying service being pitched never changes.

**Anti-pattern:** "just bumping this up in your inbox." Christian flags this explicitly. Every follow-up must earn its existence by offering something the previous email didn't.

### The One-Sentence Cold Email `[D]`

A yes/no question that makes it trivially easy to respond. Five-step build process: (1) question must be answerable with yes/no, (2) question must have strategic purpose behind it, (3) workshop your offer by listing technologies, pain points, and desires, (4) turn those into yes/no questions, (5) test.

Example: "Do you use Klaviyo for email marketing?" Prospect says "yes," which transitions into the pitch.

Christian says this framework took his agency from $0 to $100k/mo. `[V]`

**Temporal note:** The one-sentence email was a 2023 innovation. By 2024, Christian declared it "watered down" because the market caught on. It remains one of four email types, but it is no longer the default strategy. It works best for Level 1 offers where the question itself qualifies the prospect.

## 3. Copy Rules

### The 50-Word Limit `[D]`

Cold emails must be 50 words or less, typically 1-4 sentences. The relationship between length and reply rate is inversely proportional. Christian states this as a hard rule, not a guideline.

The 50-word ceiling operates differently within the 1-2 Punch: the Jab should be the absolute shortest (7-12 words of body copy is common). The Right Hook can use more of the 50-word budget with case studies and guarantees, but still must cut all self-centered fluff.

LinkedIn puts it as "four sentences max." `[V]` The NLM says "50 words." Different measurements of the same constraint.

**Evidence:** The 47-word ListKit script booked 685+ calls and scaled ListKit from $0 to $108K MRR in 90 days. The 26-word e-commerce flow rewrite script generated "incredibly high response rates." `[S]`

"When you ramble for 200 words about your methodology, prospects tune out." `[V]`

### The 3-Second Rule `[D]`

Prospects decide to read or delete within the first 3 seconds. The opening must immediately show what's in it for them. Wasting the first line on self-introduction or storytelling fails the 3-second test. This is the attention economics principle that the 50-word limit implements.

### The 2:1 Rule `[D]`

For every time you refer to yourself ("I," "me," "we," "us"), mention the prospect ("you," "your") twice. Apply by literally tallying pronouns after writing.

**Fix pattern:** "I would love to show you a few ideas" (self-centered, needy) becomes "Would you like to hear a few ideas to improve your website?" (removes "I," adds "you").

The rule is mechanical and auditable. Count the pronouns. If the ratio is off, rewrite until it passes. Christian's CTA recommendations on LinkedIn demonstrate the 2:1 Rule in action without naming it: "Let me know if you're interested" (self-focused) becomes "Mind if I send over a quick video showing what this could look like for you?" (prospect-focused). `[V]`

### Offer Anchoring and Tangibility `[D]`

Strip away marketing jargon. Pitch tangible end-results that the prospect can visualize: a physical spreadsheet of 50 free leads, a specific case study, a hard guarantee with a dollar figure.

**Effective:** "15 sales calls per month guaranteed." "50 free leads based on the case studies on your website." "$1,500 one-time fee and you own the asset."

**Ineffective:** "improved ROAS." "brand awareness." "ROI." "marketing solutions."

The tangibility principle governs word choice across all scripts. It is the vocabulary constraint on the XYZ Framework.

### Clarity Over Creativity `[V]`

"In cold email, clarity will outperform creativity almost every time because prospects are scanning your email for relevance." `[V]`

If a prospect has to reread the message to figure out what you're pitching, you've introduced friction that kills the reply. Remove anything that makes the offer less clear. State the problem directly. Tie it to a measurable result. End with one simple CTA.

Christian tore down a "creative/funny" cold email that was clever but confusing: "I read this email multiple times and still couldn't clearly understand what the person was actually offering." `[V]`

### Specificity as Primary Differentiator `[D]`

"If you want them to let their guard down and read your email, you need to be SPECIFIC." `[V]`

"In cold email, clarity will outperform creativity almost every time because prospects are scanning your email for relevance." `[V]`

Generic openers signal mass outreach. Specific openers signal research and intent. Specificity is not a style preference; it is the mechanism that lowers prospect defenses.

Implementation: replace generic terms with industry-specific language. "Use their language in your email flows." `[V]`

## 4. Script Architecture

### The 5-Step Sequence `[S]`

**Email 1 (Day 1):** Core pitch with A/B/C/D variants. The Jab. Shortest email in the sequence.

**Email 2 (Day 4):** Near the same hook as Email 1, may add a case study or reframe the economics. The Right Hook.

**Email 3 (Day 7):** Same offer, transition the CTA to a Loom video pitch or alternative lead magnet.

**Email 4 (Day 12):** Single detective emoji. Nothing else. This is a signature tactic. It's weird enough to prompt a response from people who saw the earlier emails but didn't reply.

**Email 5 (Day 33+):** Breakup email. "Would now be a better time to discuss [Benefit]?" The 21-day gap before Email 5 resets the prospect's timeline.

### 4-Part Email Structure `[S]`

Every cold email follows this anatomy:
1. **Subject line:** Brief, curiosity-inducing. (See subject line rules below.)
2. **Opener / Personalization:** Bridge directly into the pitch. Never an orphaned compliment.
3. **Body / Pitch:** The offer, anchored to tangible deliverables.
4. **CTA:** Permission-request framing. (See CTA design below.)

### Subject Line Rules `[D]`

Keep extremely brief. High performers from Christian's corpus:

- "quick question" -- still works in 2026 despite being declared burned in 2022. Market saturation is cyclical. `[D]`
- "[Company] x [Your Company]" -- B2B collaboration framing `[V]`
- "Do you have available [specific asset]?" -- service businesses `[V]`
- "Acquisition target for [company name]" -- gets immediate attention `[V]`
- "thoughts" paired with name (e.g., "Christian?") `[S]`
- "[Prospect] / [Your Name] intro" -- fakes warm referral `[S]`
- Direct guarantee lines ("15 qualified calls per month guaranteed") `[S]`

**Never:** Just the company name with no context. Boring, overly long, or generic sales subject lines.

"The email subject line is just the door opener. Your email needs to deliver value immediately." `[V]`

### CTA Design `[D]`

**Never say "I would love to [show you/discuss]."** Needy, breaks the 2:1 Rule.

**Never pitch a sales call in Email 1.** For 99% of modern B2B offers, this is a fatal mistake. The only goal of Email 1 is to get a "yes" that establishes trust. "Sell the response, not the meeting." `[D]`

**Never send a link unprompted.** Triggers spam filters and signals mass outreach.

**Never shove a calendar link.** "NEVER send your calendar link to a prospect... Always ask what times work for them and book manually." `[V]` "I'd argue that sending them your Calendly link is showing bad etiquette." `[V]` Controlled test: removed Calendly links, replaced with specific time suggestions. Booking rate jumped immediately. Higher show-up rates because prospects personally committed to a specific time. `[V]`

**Four CTA archetypes:** `[S]`
1. **Contextual Loom Video Pitch:** Give the video specific context. "Mind if I share a video going over a cold email strategy I drew up for your business?" Not "Want to watch a video explaining how our outreach works?"
2. **Free Value / Lead Magnet:** Figma file, rewritten sequence, spreadsheet of leads, sample cold email script.
3. **Portfolio / Deliverable:** For creative services. "Mind if I send over a portfolio?"
4. **Local "Coffee" Pitch:** For brick-and-mortar. "Connecting for a quick coffee at [Local Cafe]?"

**Highest-converting CTA ever:** "I recorded a quick video explaining further. Mind if I share it with you here?" `[S]`

**Matching CTA to persona:** `[S]`
- Visual/creative services: drop the Loom video, send a portfolio instead
- Startups: angle around raising Series A
- Older demographics (lawyers): ask for cell phone number instead of Calendly/Zoom link
- Influencers: state payment rate upfront ($500) and offer to write the post for them

### Post-Reply Protocol `[D]`

**Response-type routing:** `[V]`
- "Interested, tell me more" = one-page case study PDF + book specific 20-min slot
- "Not right now" = ask "when should I follow up, Q3 or Q4?" + set calendar reminder
- Pricing question = qualify budget range and timeline first, then three-tier pricing breakdown

**Booking mechanics:** Propose two specific times. Calendar link as backup only. Book manually. `[D]`

**Objection handling:** Never answer an objection without pushing for a call. Answer the question AND immediately pitch the next step. Never just answer and leave the conversation open-ended. `[S]`

**Never state pricing in cold email.** It commoditizes you. Deflect to phone: "Our pricing is flexible based on your needs -- the best next step is to jump on a quick call so we can offer you the best package for your situation." `[V]` Exception: if your price IS your proprietary advantage (e.g., $1,500 one-time fee replacing a $2K/month retainer). `[S]`

### Follow-Up Cadence and Persistence `[D]`

Christian's stance on persistence is extreme. "Until you get a clear yes or a clear no, NEVER stop following up." `[V]`

Dallas case study: followed up 50-60 times on one prospect before booking. Closed two deals at $100K-$200K each. `[S]`

Persistence story: prospect replied with interest, disappeared, no-showed a call, hung up mid-meeting, email bounced. Christian found his phone number in the email signature, left an iMessage voice note. Prospect signed up the next day. `[V]`

The automated subsequence system: when a prospect replies positively, tag as "interested" in Smartlead. This triggers an automated drip proposing specific meeting times plus a fallback calendar link. Subject line left blank so replies thread into the existing conversation. The drip continues indefinitely if ghosted. `[S]`

**Never shut off campaigns.** Momentum is expensive to rebuild. If busy, turn volume down or pivot to PR/podcast outreach. `[D]`

## 5. The Loom Video Pipeline

The Loom Video Pipeline is Christian's most durable and most-documented technique. It spans his entire career (college apartment to present) and appears in every source type. `[D]`

### The Pipeline

**Step 1 -- Email pitches the idea of a video.** Does NOT send the video. Does NOT include any links. Just asks permission: "Mind if I share a quick video going over a cold email strategy I drew up for your business?" `[D]`

**Step 2 -- Prospect replies "yes."** This is the consent gate. It filters for intent automatically. You only invest production time on people who've already said they want it. `[D]`

**Step 3 -- Record the video.** Pull up their website in the first 5 seconds (proves personalization in the thumbnail). Show how your solution applies to their specific situation. Keep under 3 minutes (2 minutes ideal). Talk like you're explaining something to a friend, not pitching to a stranger. `[V]`

**Step 4 -- Delivery email.** Embed a GIF thumbnail (not a plain link). Include two specific timestamps as hooks (like trailers for the key insights). Written CTA with two specific meeting times plus a backup calendar link. `[S]`

**Step 5 -- Follow-up.** Specific times plus backup calendar link. Continue the drip until booked or declined. `[S]`

### Video Pitch Framing `[V]`

The copy that pitches the video matters as much as the video itself. Wrong approach: "Interested in watching a video explaining how our outreach works?" (generic explainer). Right approach: turn the video into a lead magnet with specific context.

Good pitch lines:
- "Mind if I share a video going over a cold email strategy I drew up for your business?"
- "Would you be open to checking out a video going over the exact script that generated $5K in revenue?"
- "Mind if I share a video going over our portfolio of top performing creatives?"

The video must contain insights they can't get from a competitor's pitch. `[V]`

### Evidence Chain

- $25K web design deal from single Trojan Horse Loom pitch `[S]`
- $250K in retainers from a student using Loom audits `[S]`
- 600+ positive replies in one year for a custom merchandise client pitching video of their catalog `[S]`
- 75 unique positive replies from 19K leads for ListKit cold email setup offer `[S]`
- 2-week campaign, 1,200 prospects, 9 positive replies. Every single replier asked for a video. `[V]`
- 11 leads in 30 days using case study + video CTA progression `[V]`

## 6. Offer Construction

### The XYZ Framework `[D]`

"We help [X: Audience] accomplish [Y: Quantifiable Benefit] with [Z: Unique Mechanism]."

X is the audience. Y is the outcome anchored to something tangible. Z is the mechanism that makes you different.

The Offer Anchoring constraint governs how Y and Z get expressed: anchor to physical deliverables or hard numerical outcomes. "15 sales calls" or "50 free leads," not "marketing" or "ROAS."

### Intangible + Concrete + Proof Layering `[V]`

Layer 1: Intangible benefit that creates emotion ("make you go viral on TikTok").
Layer 2: Concrete guarantee that removes risk ("1 million views in 90 days or we work for free").
Layer 3: Proof to back up claims ("Fragile Collective got 50k in sales from organic content").

"Only emotional benefit = hype. Only guarantee = transactional. All three together = believable promise." `[V]`

This is a more granular version of the XYZ Framework. Y maps to Layer 2. Z maps to Layer 1. Layer 3 adds proof as an explicit component.

### Offer Angle Invention `[D]`

Break down every component of your service. Identify which components have never been pitched over cold email. That becomes your new angle.

Example: an ecom email marketing agency breaks down into customer interviews, Reddit research, copywriting, flow optimization, deliverability, design. Instead of pitching "15% revenue boost" (what everyone says), pitch "we interview 100 of your customers and use their language in your email flows." `[D]`

"You don't need to have fulfilled this before. If the market wants to buy it, pitch it and figure out the fulfillment once you have leads coming in." `[V]`

### Pricing Rules `[S]`

**The Setup/Asset Model:** Charge a one-time setup fee (e.g., $1,500) where the client owns the built asset outright. Bypasses the standard $2K/month retainer objection.

**The Paid Scoping Sprint:** For $100K-$200K deals, sell a $5K-$10K scoping sprint first. You get paid to investigate their pain points for a few weeks. Effectively getting paid to sell the six-figure backend. The Dallas case study confirms this pattern.

### Selling Against the Industry Quo `[S]`

Reframe the offer to contrast with what the prospect's industry actively hates:
- Lawyers: "without spending a cent on TV ads or billboards"
- Bookkeeping: sell against slow CPAs by pitching "Gen Z accounting firm" with 24/7 Slack/text access
- Lead gen: pitch multi-channel "LinkedIn, cold email, and warm calling" because everyone else only offers cold email

This positioning creates differentiation without changing the actual service.

## 7. The Industry Slang Translation Table

One of the most concrete, immediately automatable patterns in the corpus. Given an industry, replace generic "leads" or "clients" with the specific deliverable that industry values. `[D]`

| Industry | Generic Term | Industry Slang |
|----------|-------------|----------------|
| Lawyers | "leads" or "clients" | "contested divorce cases," "child custody disputes" |
| Restaurants | "customers" | "weeknight dine-ins," "beachgoer tourists" |
| Pest control | "jobs" | "bed bug and termite jobs" (pain points: "seasonal demand," winter slowdowns) |
| Trucking | "business" | "loads," "shipments" (avoid "dead head miles," "keeping your board full") |
| Real estate | "leads" | "listings," "buyer consultations" |
| E-commerce | "revenue" | "abandoned cart recovery," "welcome flow conversions" |
| Web design | "projects" | "site builds," "landing page redesigns" |
| Financial advisors | "clients" | "AUM growth," "retirement planning prospects" |

The governing principle: "If you want them to let their guard down, you need to be SPECIFIC." `[V]` AI tools (Deepseek specifically) can inject this slang into a generic script. Christian's prompt: "Do not make this script any longer or any shorter just change up the messaging to be hyper relevant for [Industry] companies using their lingo, pain points and desires." `[S]`

## 8. Anti-Patterns

Each anti-pattern includes the exact phrase Christian flags, why it fails, and what to do instead.

### Copy Anti-Patterns

**"I would love to show you" / "I would love to discuss"** `[S]`
Why it fails: Needy. Breaks the 2:1 Rule. Centers the sender, not the prospect.
Fix: "Would you be open to a quick call to learn more?" or "Mind if I share a quick video?"

**Self-disqualifying copy** `[D]`
Examples: "I know bookkeeping isn't the coolest topic." "I know this might seem random."
Why it fails: Immediately bores the reader and gives them permission to stop caring.
Fix: Lead with the value. If the topic seems boring, make the offer exciting through specific dollar amounts or outcomes.

**Assuming the prospect knows what you sell** `[V]`
Example from a teardown: "You know what's not fishy? Omnisend." Christian's response: "Is this a fish company?"
Why it fails: Cold email is cold. The prospect has zero context.
Fix: Answer upfront: What do you sell? What's in it for me? How easy is this?

**Pitching generic "leads" to specialized industries** `[D]`
Why it fails: Signals you don't understand their business. "Leads" means nothing to a trucking company. They want "loads."
Fix: Use the industry slang translation table.

**100+ word emails** `[D]`
Why it fails: Prospects aren't invested enough to read that much from a stranger. The 3-Second Rule means most of those words never get read.
Fix: Cut word count in half. Then cut again.

**Inauthentic compliments** `[D]`
Examples: "Big fan of your content" (when you don't follow them). "Love what you're doing at [Company]."
Why it fails: Prospects spot fake personalization instantly. Worse than no personalization because it actively demonstrates you did NOT research them.

**"Just bumping this up in your inbox"** `[V]`
Why it fails: Adds zero value. Signals laziness. The prospect already decided not to respond.
Fix: Each follow-up must offer new information about the same offer.

### Pitch Anti-Patterns

**Pitching a sales call in Email 1** `[D]`
Declared "completely dead" in 2025 for 99% of offers. Shows cards too early, triggers sales defenses. The only exception is Level 1 offers with overwhelming demand.

**Sending calendar links in cold email** `[D]`
"NEVER send your calendar link to a prospect." `[V]` Propose specific times, book manually.

**Stating pricing in cold email** `[D]`
Commoditizes you. Exception: price is your proprietary advantage.

### Technical Anti-Patterns

**Using primary domain for cold email** `[S]`
Why it fails: Spam complaints on your primary domain kill all your business email.
Fix: Burner domains only.

**Tracking open rates** `[S]`
Why it fails: Hidden tracking pixels trigger Google/Microsoft spam filters. The measurement tool damages the thing you're measuring.
Fix: Measure success by positive reply rate only.

**HTML, links, or images in cold emails** `[D]`
Why it fails: Signals "mass blast." Damages deliverability and trust simultaneously.
Fix: Plain text only for initial emails.

**AI writing cold email from scratch** `[D]`
ChatGPT is "the most brilliant research assistant in the world and the worst employee in the world." `[S]` AI produces fluffy, long-winded, vague copy. Christian's method: write the offer and structure yourself, then use AI to inject industry research, slang, and targeted pain points into that structure.

**AI-generated first lines** `[S]`
AI frequently hallucinates (congratulating a prospect on working at a company they never worked for). Deprecated as of 2024. Replaced by mass personalization (call out job title, or combine location + industry).

## 9. Personalization Framework

### Two Modes `[D]`

**Mass Personalization (for email):** Build a highly segmented list. Write one opening line that applies to everyone on it. Call out job title, or combine location + industry. The line must bridge directly into the pitch, not sit as an isolated compliment. This is the default mode for volume outreach.

**Sniper Personalization (for DMs / Dream 100):** Genuine 1-on-1 manual research. Reference a specific video, product, or podcast. 5-10 manually researched DMs per day, never automated. Aided by AI tools (Manis) for speed. Old manual process: 1 hour per prospect. AI-assisted: 30 seconds. `[D]`

**When to skip entirely:** If your offer is strong enough (a "no-brainer"), personalization is unnecessary. Jump straight to pitch. `[S]`

**The bridge rule:** `[S]` The personalization line must flow into the pitch. A compliment about their podcast that has no connection to your service is an orphaned line that wastes precious words. Every sentence must earn its place.

## 10. A/B Testing Methodology

### Angle Testing `[D]`

Create 3-4 variations of Email 1, each from a different angle. Test direct pitch vs. Trojan Horse vs. lead magnet vs. one-sentence. Scale the winner.

For a single service, list every benefit the prospect would care about. Split benefits into emotional ("clean your property without lifting a finger") and logical ("generate more income"). Write one offer angle per benefit. Test 3-4 head-to-head.

**Evidence:** For short-term rental cleaning, tested four angles. "Generate more income" won by 40% higher response rate than second place. "Your first guess is rarely the one that wins." `[V]`

### Volume-Based Testing Capacity `[V]`

- 50,000 leads/month = test 8 angles
- 2,000 leads/month = test 3-4 max

More volume enables more testing. Less volume demands focus on fewer, stronger angles.

### Baseline Conversion Benchmarks `[D]`

- Open rates: >50% (but no longer tracked)
- Positive reply rate: 1-2% (1-2 per 100 emails)
- Booking rate: 1 per 250 emails; 1,000 emails -> 10-20 booked calls
- Close rate: 1-2 deals per 1,000 emails
- North Star: 1 client per 1,000 emails sent
- Unit economics: 5,000 people monthly for <$500. 1 client per 1,000 = 5 clients from $500 spend. CAC = $100. `[V]`

## 11. Teardown Patterns

### Universal Teardown Protocol `[S]`

Four steps applied to every email regardless of industry, before layering on industry-specific fixes:

1. **Cut length in half.** Delete opening fluff paragraphs and solution-explanation paragraphs.
2. **Remove self-disqualifying and inauthentic copy.** Anything that undermines the sender's credibility or gives the prospect permission to dismiss.
3. **Swap "sales call" CTA for "free upfront value" CTA.** Loom video, Figma file, rewritten sequence, portfolio.
4. **Billboard follow-ups.** Repeat the same offer, rotate the framing, never introduce a new service.

### Diagnostic Framework `[V]`

When reviewing any cold email, answer upfront:
- What do you sell?
- What's in it for me?
- How easy is this?

If any of those are unclear after reading, the email fails.

### Sequencing Rule `[V]`

Certain CTAs require prerequisites. Sending a quote only makes sense after: (1) a clear problem has been identified, (2) impact explained in business terms, (3) prospect acknowledged solving it is a priority. Without that sequence, a quote feels presumptuous.

### Industry-Specific Teardown Templates `[S]`

**Web design (Level 3 offer):** The "Side Door" approach: pitch a specific desired outcome for the niche. For startups: "help you leave a great first impression on VCs." Subject line: "Looking to raise Capital." Case study: "[Client] raised $10M Series A, firm said website played major role." Alternative: Trojan Horse with a Figma file redesign as the CTA.

**Bookkeeping (unsexy offer):** Pitch specific tax loopholes with dollar amounts. "Augusta Rule" saves $20K in personal tax. S-Corp conversion saved a client $50K. Or: sell against the current accountant by positioning as a "Gen Z accounting firm" with 24/7 Slack/text access. Or: productize into tangible deliverables ($200 financial statement creation, LLC setup in Delaware, "perfect P&L").

**Email marketing (over-explained offer):** The "Rewrite" angle: "I rewrote your welcome emails to optimize them for more conversions. Mind if I share the three-step email sequence here?" (70+ positive replies). Or: invent a proprietary angle ("we interview 100 of their best customers and inject feedback into copy"). Or: the "Two Things" template combining guarantee + video: "Two things quick... One, we can bring you 10 extra sales calls per month guaranteed... Two, I recorded a video going over an email marketing strategy I came up with for you."

**Lead gen (heavily saturated):** Pitch multi-channel mechanism as a pattern interrupt ("mix of LinkedIn, cold email, and SMS appointment setting"). Differentiate the guarantee ("only charge for qualified sales calls where prospect shows up AND is a good fit"). Or pitch free upfront value: "Mind if I send over a sample cold email script I wrote up for you based on your website?"

### Unsexy Positioning Fix `[S]`

When the service name itself carries negative connotations, rename the mechanism. "Virtual assistants" scares prospects. "Virtual social media assistants" or "virtual podcast booking agent" is the same service, different framing.

## 13. AI Toolchain for Cold Email

### Role Separation `[D]`

Human writes the offer, the structure, and the rules. AI fills in vocabulary, research, and personalization data. The division is strict. AI never writes the complete email.

### Tool Roles `[S]`

**Deepseek** -- Best for cold email copy and slang injection. Christian's prompt: "Do not make this script any longer or any shorter just change up the messaging to be hyper relevant for [Industry] companies using their lingo, pain points and desires."

**ChatGPT** -- Research and pain point discovery. Prompt: "What are a few common pain points for [Target Audience] when it comes to marketing their [Business]?" Then drill deeper. Look for a single golden nugget.

**Claude Projects ("CB Writes Copy Bot")** -- Sequence generation. Fed with Christian's rules and best templates. Produces three-step sequences in 30 seconds.

**Manis** -- Dream 100 deep personalization. Scrapes website and LinkedIn to produce verified, accurate personalized outreach. Cuts 1 hour of manual research to 30 seconds.

### The AI Warning `[D]`

"The most brilliant research assistant in the world and the worst employee in the world." `[S]`

AI-generated first lines frequently hallucinate. Generic AI personalization performs worse than no personalization at all (Eric's data: 143% fewer replies). Christian's 2025 position is explicitly anti-AI-personalization: better copy beats better tooling.
