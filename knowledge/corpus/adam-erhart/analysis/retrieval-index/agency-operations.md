# Agency Operations

Running the business: onboarding, retention, delegation, the zero-to-first-client roadmap, and what fails. Source: `q04`, `q05`, `q43`. Provenance `[S]`.

---

## GT KT

Get Them, Keep Them. Credited to Ryan Lee, and he treats it as the master frame.

**Get Them** is acquisition: attracting, conversing, closing. Under 10 clients, 50 to 80% of the owner's time belongs here.

**Keep Them** is delivery plus overcommunication, against a stated baseline that up to 76% of traditional agency clients cancel within 90 days.

---

## RGA versus NRGA

**Revenue generating:** outreach, sales calls, booking, follow-up, direct offers, closing, delivering the core result.

**Non-revenue generating:** logos, website tweaking, brand colours, scrolling, and learning without executing. He names this last one **productive procrastination**, fear of rejection dressed as thorough preparation.

Below 10 clients: 50–90% of hours on RGAs, 10–30% on fulfilment.

This is his single most repeated constraint. It reappears in the mindset, zero-to-first-client and anti-pattern lanes under different names.

## Zero to first client

### Skip entirely at the start
- A complex website, a logo, business cards
- A portfolio, case studies or a pitch deck, your first 1 to 3 clients *are* your portfolio
- A menu of services
- Cold outreach to strangers on day one
- Hourly pricing or custom project work
- Spending weeks selecting a niche

### Days 1–2
Import the pre-built snapshot so fulfilment is ready before you contact anyone, 30 minutes. Build a 10 to 30 name warm list from businesses you already deal with: your barber, dentist, mechanic, gym, plus friends, family and past co-workers.

### Days 3–7
Informal outreach with no hard pitch and no price. Offer a zero-risk 7 to 14 day free trial, or send a 2-minute video audit naming a specific gap.

### Closing the first deal
$297–$500/mo, month to month, no contract, live within 48 hours to 7 days.

**Expect 2 to 4 weeks.** The first client always takes longest. Some land one in 48 hours; most do not.

### Weeks 2–8, to 3–5 clients
Target $1,000–$2,500/mo recurring. Over-deliver for the first 1 to 3, then ask for the testimonial once results show (often within 14–30 days): "I'm so glad this system is working for you, would you mind if I documented these results to show other businesses in similar situations?"

Once the warm list is exhausted, move to Google Maps, page 2 and 3 local service businesses with neglected reviews.

**Stay on one service and one niche for the first 3 to 5 clients, 60 to 90 days.** Do not add services early.

### Days 60–90+, to $10K
It is arithmetic, not a skill barrier. Nine to ten clients at $1,191. Or ten at $1,000, five at $2,000, four at $2,500, twenty-five at $400.

### The lifestyle rule
Do not raise personal living expenses until 20+ recurring clients are locked in. Churn is normal. This is the only place in the corpus where he plans for his own model failing.

## Onboarding

**The critical 30-day window.** The first 24 hours to 30 days must kill buyer's remorse.

**Engineer a quick win inside 7 days.** A reactivation campaign or a review sequence that produces actual bookings. If sales are not immediate, deliver a visible progress win, proof of citations built, campaigns set up.

**Overcommunicate.** Touch base on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 21 and 28 with 2-minute Loom screen-shares. Do not take the money and work in silence.

## Retention

Clients do not leave because of bad results. They leave because they cannot see the results.

**Service stacking is the flywheel.** One service reads as a disposable expense. Three interlocked systems cannot be cancelled without shutting down reviews, call coverage and the lead engine at once.

**Three psychological triggers:**
- **Progress amplifier.** A Friday "Weekly Wins" report with progress bars, reviews added, missed calls saved. Goal-gradient effect.
- **Future focus.** On the 25th of each month, send next month's roadmap so they are looking forward rather than at the invoice.
- **Switching costs.** Once their numbers, chat widgets and review software live inside HighLevel, leaving creates real operational friction.

**Reporting is the retention product**, not an admin task.

### The four causes of churn, in his order
1. **No quantifiable ROI.** Selling likes, comments and views instead of calls, appointments and revenue.
2. **Under-communication.** Taking the money and working in silence, so the client assumes nothing is happening. *The one that costs nothing to fix.*
3. **Single-service dependence.**
4. **Toxic bottom-10% clients** who chew 80% of the time for 10% of the revenue, produce email phobia and burn the owner out.

### Churn numbers
76% cancel a traditional agency inside 90 days. The average agency client lasts 2 months. Online clients retain 6 to 8 months; local face-to-face clients average 38.

**The $2,000/month beginner trap.** Four clients at $2K is $8K/mo that drops to $2K two months later, because a small business cannot carry $2K plus ad spend while results are still arriving.

## SOPs

Anything done more than once gets one, even if it is a checklist in a Google Doc.

Three categories:
- **Client acquisition**, scripts and steps for DMs, cold and warm email, calls, Loom audits, referral asks.
- **Client onboarding**, automated triggers for contracts, invoices, brand guidelines, access collection, kickoff.
- **Service delivery**, templated steps for campaign launches, local SEO and citations, review automation, reporting.

## Delegation

**The buyback rate:** (annual income ÷ 2,000 hours) ÷ 4. At $100K/year that is $50/hr, giving a $12.50/hr threshold. Anything outsourceable below it goes immediately.

**VAs** on Upwork or Fiverr at $5–$10/hr for repetitive admin, report compiling and technical setup.

**The Eisenhower matrix** for what to delegate or delete:

| | Urgent | Not urgent |
|---|---|---|
| **Important** | DO, client fires, crises | DECIDE, planning, content, SOPs. *Where the money is actually made.* Time-block it before others take the calendar. |
| **Not important** | DELEGATE, admin, bookkeeping | DELETE, compulsive email, scrolling |

**The 3-day time audit.** Track every activity in 30-minute blocks for three working days, then sort each task:
- Good at and enjoy → keep. Your zone.
- Good at and dislike → delegate to a VA, contractor or software.
- Not good at and enjoy → train, or delegate over time.
- Not good at and dislike → get help or eliminate today.

## White-label fulfilment

**Hand off** analytical, structured, intent-based work: Google Ads, Maps rankings, citation builds, link building, local SEO. The dividing line is whether a deliverable can be judged right or wrong without the client's opinion.

**Keep or avoid** subjective creative work, custom web design, social content, ad copy, because revisions and taste disputes break the model.

## The nine never-dos

1. **Never charge hourly.** It punishes efficiency, caps income and turns a business into a job.
2. **Never do custom work or custom proposals.** Arts-and-crafts projects kill scalability.
3. **Never offer a Baskin-Robbins menu.** One niche, one offer, one channel until real scale.
4. **Never sell low-ROI or untrackable services.** No social posting, no likes management, no pure branding.
5. **Never do manual execution software can automate.**
6. **Never prioritise NRGAs.**
7. **Never keep toxic clients.** Rank them, trim the bottom 10%, fire bad fits.
8. **Never rely on long sales calls or high-pressure pitches.** A 2-minute audit converts faster and cleaner than a 90-minute strategy call.
9. **Never build the solution before selling it.**

And the tenth, from the HighLevel warnings: **only promise what you control.** Never a #1 ranking, never a revenue figure. Promise profile optimisation, requests sent, 100% of missed calls answered.

## What he says fails

He prices his own failures, which is rare in this category.

- **$25,000 print campaign.** An industrial drilling client's entire annual budget on trade magazine ads and print directories. Zero traceable calls or sales.
- **$100 for a full campaign.** His first client. After the drive-outs and meetings, under $5 an hour.
- **5,000 dental leads wasted.** Facebook lead ads for a Vancouver dentist delivered 5,000 verified leads at $1–$2 each. The dentist called, emailed and texted none of them. All 5,000 went cold. *The strongest argument for speed-to-lead anywhere in the corpus, and it is an argument against his own delivery.*
- **The $1M agency at 20% margin.** ~$500K payroll plus rent, contractors and software left 20 cents on the dollar, and he felt like an overworked HR mediator.
- **The $1M course business.** Shut down after doctor-diagnosed stress-related burnout from the launch treadmill.
- **The AI poetry business.** A 48-hour ChatGPT and Facebook ads experiment: negative comments, 141 sales page views, 1 sale.
- **Nine hours on a sample website** for a roofing company, sent over, complete silence, the owner thought web design was handled.
- **The 11-service offer page.** 2017, $0 in 60 days. Deleted ten services, kept Google reviews, signed 7 clients in 30 days.
- **Losing half the agency overnight.** His largest client, nearly 50% of revenue, retired and closed.
- **12 to 18 hour days, seven days a week.** "A really expensive prison cell with Wi-Fi."

### Client campaigns that failed
- **Sarah, interior designer.** Weeks tweaking a landing page, copy rewritten 14 times, keyword match types changed until Google was confused. Launched: $97 spent, 1 lead, silence. After sharpening targeting and copy, the second attempt brought 4 leads in 3 days.
- **A resort.** $2,000/month on Google Ads, 1,000+ clicks, dozens of reservation forms, zero bookings, the forms sat unread in an inbox for hours or days.
- **Alex, coaching business.** 19 leads in week one, one follow-up email sent, then gave up.
- **The $40,000 campaign that looked dead.** Low CTR, low engagement, presumed tanked. Checking the CRM showed 2 leads had converted into a $25,000 client and a $15,000 retainer.

### Offers and models that fail
- **The Baskin-Robbins menu.** 11+ services triggers the paradox of choice. "Let me think about it", then never.
- **$97/month SaaS as a beginner.** 50 to 100 clients needed for $5K–$10K/month. High volume, zero differentiation, heavy support, rapid churn.
- **Offer-niche mismatch.** Lead generation to veterinarians who already run waitlists. Facebook ads for emergency plumbers when the homeowner is searching Google with a burst pipe.

## Timelines, honestly

Beginners expect linear progress or a 90-day exit. Reality is flat for a long stretch and then parabolic, and most people quit during the flat part and conclude the model does not work.

| Milestone | Reality |
|---|---|
| First client | 1 to 3 weeks of daily action |
| First agency to $100K/mo | 3 years |
| Second agency to $100K/mo | 30 days, on systems and a productized offer |
| Niche mastery | Staying 18 to 24 months |
| A best-fit high-ticket client deciding | 2 to 3 weeks, often 30 to 60 days, even when they say they are ready on day one |

**Buyer timing splits 3 / 10 / 87.** Three percent buy now (0–30 days). Ten percent soon (30–90). Eighty-seven percent later (90 days to 18 months).

Nearly nine in ten of the market is not buying in the window most outreach is measured over. That split is the justification for everything he does with nurture and reactivation.

## Survivorship bias

Taught through Abraham Wald and the WWII bombers. The military analysed returning planes, saw bullet holes on wings, tail and fuselage, and planned to armour there. Wald pointed out they were only studying the survivors, the planes hit in the cockpit or engines never came back.

Applied: beginners copy a guru's current behaviour, post daily, follow your passion, without seeing that the guru already has cash flow, an established offer and an audience.

Alongside it, business as a war of attrition. Surviving while competitors quit hands you market share by default.

A marketing educator naming the bias that makes marketing education unreliable, including his own.
