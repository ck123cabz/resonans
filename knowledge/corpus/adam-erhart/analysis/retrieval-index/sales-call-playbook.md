# Sales Call Playbook

The five named closing frameworks, the positioning stance behind them, the pain-escalation sequence, and the ethical lines Erhart draws around all of it. Source: `q12`–`q18`, `q42`. Provenance `[S]`, with `[G]` where a named study backs the finding.

Scripts live in `scripts-library.md`. This file is the structure, the timing and the psychology.

---

## Which framework, when

| Framework | Use it | Pause |
|---|---|---|
| Black Sand | High-ticket live call, warm lead, quantifiable pain, closing in one 30-minute conversation | 3 seconds after they confirm monthly losses |
| Freedom Close | End of any offer presentation, the moment resistance shows | 5 to 7 seconds after delivering it |
| Mirror Close | Minutes 5–10 of a discovery call, after the criteria reveal | none |
| Quiet Close | Local service owner, $497/mo recurring, where operational maths replaces a pitch | none |
| Tipping Point | Risk-averse prospects who default to delaying | 5 to 7 seconds after the fit question |

## Selective Authority: the stance underneath all of it

Four moves at four points in the funnel, all running on psychological reactance (Jack Brehm, 1960s) `[G]`. Restricting access triggers a drive to restore the freedom.

**Before contact, the velvet rope.** An application form and pre-call diagnostic content replace an open calendar link. Prospects arrive hoping to be accepted. Pre-call value delivery makes them think: if this is what they give away, what does the paid version look like.

**At first reply, authority reversal.** The frame of a diagnosing physician. The person asking the questions holds the leverage.

**During qualification, the doorman.** State explicit standards and evaluate whether they qualify.

**At the close, the red button.** Give explicit freedom to decline.

Together they swap the prospect's question from "do I want this?" to "am I good enough for this?"

### Where it breaks
- **Fake selectivity.** Prospects detect a seller who claims to be selective and takes anyone with a credit card.
- **Arrogance.** Selective professional, not a bouncer on a power trip.
- **Mediocre delivery.** Premium positioning on a mediocre service is expensive disappointment.
- **Incongruence.** An application funnel alongside needy cold DMs destroys the position.
- **Desperation.** Run from financial scarcity and the pullback cannot be executed. He tells beginners to build momentum and case studies first.

You lose transactional buyers who wanted a quick quote. He says that is the point: they churn.

## Black Sand Method

Named for the finality of an hourglass. Four steps.

**1. Diagnostic open.** "On a scale of 1 to 10, how painful is this problem for you right now?"

Below 7, stop: "Sounds like this isn't a critical priority right now, why don't we reconnect when it becomes more urgent." The walk-away is what makes the rest credible, and it is the piece most likely to be dropped in practice.

**2. Investment inquiry**, three parts:
- "If we could solve this problem completely, what would that be worth to your business annually?"
- "And what's it costing you right now to not have this solved?"
- "So every month that passes costs you roughly $X amount of dollars, is that math right?"

Then **3 seconds of silence**. Let the number land.

**3. Capability confirmation.** The three-story stack (see `scripts-library.md`).

**4. Choice architecture.** "Based on everything you've told me you have three paths to stop the bleeding." DIY Sprint $497 over 90 days, Partnership Track $5,000 over 30 days pre-selected, Done-For-You Rapid $12,000 over 14 days. Close with "but you are free to choose any option or none at all."

**Psychology:** consistency (a high pain score stated out loud creates internal pressure to act on it); the Zeigarnik effect (booking a call opens an unfinished mental tab); decision fatigue; the Goldilocks effect and default bias, with roughly 68% taking the pre-selected middle.

**Note:** these three tiers are an order of magnitude above the $297–$497 local retainers. This is his high-ticket consulting ladder, not the agency service ladder. Do not merge them.

## Freedom Close

Built on "but you are free" (BYAF). Christopher Carpenter's 2013 meta-analysis over 42 studies and 22,000+ subjects found the phrase roughly doubles compliance `[G]`.

Four steps: value confirmation, invite obstacles, freedom release, then the pause. Full wording in `scripts-library.md`.

The pause is 5 to 7 seconds and it is non-negotiable. Do not speak, do not fill the air, do not backpedal until they speak first.

**Why it works:** when people feel pushed, the primary urge is to escape. Opening the exit removes the threat to their freedom, and resistance goes with it.

## Mirror Close

Move 3 of the Gatekeeper sequence, delivered 5 to 10 minutes into a discovery call after the criteria reveal.

Declaring that they qualify removes the "am I good enough?" anxiety. Asking whether it fits what they are trying to accomplish forces them to commit to their own identity as someone who acts. Declining now means contradicting themselves.

Follow immediately with the final filter, then the pullback tag if they hesitate.

## Quiet Close

For local service owners on a $497/month retainer. Named for tides: they never beg or chase, they show up predictably.

Four parts:
1. **Build the shoreline.** Fix the listing, reviews, website, phone routing.
2. **Catch the first wave.** An AI receptionist answering missed calls within 10 seconds.
3. **Set the rhythm.** Automated review requests after job completion.
4. **Bring back the tide.** A 90-day database reactivation.

The pitch is compounding arithmetic. A local business converts about 10% of inbound. Each of the four parts is claimed to add a conservative 20% lift: 10% × 1.2⁴ = 20.7%.

Close: "$497 a month, no contract, about $16 a day. The first time the AI receptionist catches a missed call that turns into a customer, the month is paid for."

**Caveat:** the maths assumes the four lifts are independent and multiplicative. He does not defend that assumption. Treat 20.7% as illustrative.

## Tipping Point Method

For prospects paralysed by risk. Loss aversion does the work: losses weigh roughly twice as much as equivalent gains `[G]`.

**1. Weight audit.** Pre-call intake asking: biggest challenge, what you've tried, Plan B, where the business is in 6 months if we solve this. On the call: "Before we go any further I want to make sure I understand what's really at stake here. On a scale of 1 to 10, how much is this problem actually costing you, not just money, but time, energy, stress?"

**2. Desire stack.** Three story weights (Skeptic, Fast Mover, Transformation) through the pre-call sequence or live.

**3. Fear reframe.** For a partner objection: "If your partner says yes, would you do it?" then "What parts do you think they wouldn't like?"

**4. The tip.** "Does this feel like the right fit for what you're trying to solve?" then 5 to 7 seconds of silence. If they ask "are you still there?", answer "yep I'm here" and keep waiting.

## POWER Method

A five-step sequence that runs through messages or a call without pushing.

**P: Pattern interrupt.** "Just curious, are you the business owner who inquired about scaling past 50K per month?" The parts: "just curious" removes pressure, "are you" makes it binary, "the business owner" is an identity hook, and the past-tense assumption makes their brain fill the gap.

**O: Open loops.** A numbered before-and-after story ending in a 15-minute offer. Specific numbers and contrast build believability.

**W: Worthiness tests.** A multiple-choice bottleneck question, or a plain "we only work with [criteria]." Unqualified prospects eliminate themselves; serious ones justify why they qualify.

**E: Escalation ladder.** Small yeses compounding toward a booked time. Commitment and consistency bias.

**R: Reactance triggers.** "I just checked our calendar and I have two strategic consultation spots available this week. These usually book two to three weeks out… If you're serious about scaling I can hold Tuesday's spot for the next two hours while you check your calendar. Would that help?" Then two hours later: "Hey, that Tuesday spot is about to open back up, last chance to grab it, should I release it to the waitlist or lock it in for you?"

The R step frames scarcity as access, not as time. It also sits closest to the line he draws against fake scarcity: the whole weight rests on whether the spot is genuinely limited.

## Pain escalation

**Latent**, the pain exists but is intellectualised away. They say "yeah, I'm doing all right."
**Realized**, a question opens a crack and "all right" stops being acceptable.
**Extreme**, the emotional cost of inaction becomes the thing they need out of. This is where the sale happens.

### The sequence
1. "What's the single biggest problem you're struggling with in your business right now?"
2. "What happens if you're still doing the exact same thing in 2, 3, or 5 years?"
3. "How would you feel if nothing changed?"
4. "What made you reach out today instead of last month?"
5. "On a scale of 1 to 10, how painful is this problem for you right now?"
6. **The flip**, when they answer 7 or 8: "You said it's an 8. What would make it a 10?"

The flip is the sharpest move in the corpus. It makes them describe the full cost in their own words rather than hearing it from you.

Then the loss calculation, three questions and 3 seconds of silence.

### Future-pacing inaction
"Describe the frustration, the regret, the opportunities slipping through your fingers while your competition gets sharper and further ahead."

Closing line: "If you don't make a move now, you're going to get left behind."

## Story structures

### Pit of Darkness
"No one buys the perfect origin story. They buy the comeback."

1. **Find the pit.** The worst moment before you figured it out. His example: sitting on the floor after a client ghosted an invoice with the bank account at zero.
2. **Don't sanitise it.** Skipping the hard parts removes the tension that makes it relatable.
3. **Show the turning point and the rise.** The specific decision or system that pulled you out.

### Status Shift Framing
People buy to upgrade who they are, and they fight harder for identity than for a surface problem.

Name the before and after: overworked freelancer → respected authority; faceless business → trusted brand; guessing marketer → confident strategist.

Reframe from "here's what you get" to "here's what this makes you." Confirmation phrases: "this is what high-performing consultants do," "this is how leaders delegate without losing control."

This is why his offers are named for outcomes and identities, never for deliverables.

### Three-part transformation
Problem (the character and the specific frustration) → Journey (what was tried, the false starts, the turn) → Outcome (quantifiably better, with the lesson).

## The biases, with their moves

| Bias | Move |
|---|---|
| Halo effect | Audit the first touchpoint. It buffers later mistakes. |
| Mere exposure | 7 to 14 touchpoints, including physical proximity |
| Loss aversion `[G]` | Headline the cost of inaction; calculate monthly lost revenue live |
| Anchoring | The higher number first, always |
| Reciprocity | Give something fully usable before asking. The free audit. |
| FOMO duo | Genuine deadlines, genuine caps. Never a resetting timer. |
| Yes ladder | A one-click quiz or a one-word comment trigger before the real ask |
| Trust equation | Publish live audits and strategy sessions so competence is observed before contact |
| Dangerous simplicity | One sentence, one service. "Complexity feels like work, simplicity feels like momentum." |
| IKEA effect | Have clients approve ad copy or pick keywords. Up to 5x the perceived value. |
| Serial position | Put the scripting effort into the hook and the closing CTA |
| Decoy effect | A third inferior option makes the middle obvious |
| Zeigarnik | Open loops in content and subject lines |
| Prattfall | A competent expert admitting a relatable flaw reads as more trustworthy |
| Ben Franklin | A small favour asked makes people like you more |
| Priming | Pre-expose them to the words, images or stories that open the frame you need |

Iyengar's jam study `[G]`: 24 choices produced a 3% buy rate, six produced 30%. This is the argument against the menu.

Rackham's SPIN research over 35,000 calls `[G]`: pitching triggers resistance, diagnostic questions let prospects close themselves.

## The ethical lines he states

He calls this material dark psychology and draws the boundaries himself.

- "Psychology without empathy is manipulation, and people can smell it."
- Do not use the triggers if the offer does not genuinely solve the problem.
- Real selectivity, never fake countdown timers or manufactured spots.
- Do not fabricate experience. If you are new: "you'd be one of my first clients."
- Helping someone decide quickly is more ethical than leaving them stuck in a problem costing them money daily.

Any agent generating from this file needs these as constraints, not as a footnote.

## The Adventurer Frame

For positioning with no clients, credentials or authority. Do not fake expertise. Reframe as someone documenting the work live:

> "I'm not the world's leading expert on this yet, but I'm obsessed with testing AI systems for local businesses, and I'm documenting everything that works."

Prospects buy access to someone figuring it out so they don't have to.

Pairs with the One-Step Rule: you need to be one step ahead of the owner on one problem, not ten years ahead. And with the Prattfall effect, which is why admitting the gap strengthens rather than weakens the position.
