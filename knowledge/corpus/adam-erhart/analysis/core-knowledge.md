## 1. What he actually sells

A one-person marketing agency with no employees, serving local service businesses, fulfilled by software rather than labour.

The commercial thesis is one move he calls the **Bridge**. You are a mortgage broker, not a manufacturer. Find a quantifiable revenue leak in a local business, match it to a software feature that already exists, and charge a monthly fee to keep the connection running. You never build anything custom. Installing a client is importing a pre-built snapshot and swapping the business name: 10 to 20 minutes.

Three gaps, found from outside the business in under two minutes:

- **The missed-call gap.** Phone the business at 5:15pm or over lunch and hit voicemail.
- **The review gap.** They have 12 Google reviews; the competitor at the top has 180.
- **The speed-to-lead gap.** Submit a quote request on their website and wait.

Each maps to a HighLevel feature: missed-call text-back or an AI receptionist, an automated review engine, speed-to-lead workflows.

### The margin argument
A traditional $1M/year agency keeps $100K–$200K after payroll, rent and overhead, which is 10 to 20%. A one-person agency at $200K–$250K keeps $180K–$225K, over 80%. Same take-home, none of the management. He ran the first kind and walked away from it at exactly 20% margin.

The mechanism is the flat platform fee. HighLevel's Unlimited plan is $297/month regardless of how many client sub-accounts you create, so the first client covers the entire software stack and every client after runs at 90%+ net.

Cheap prices ($27, $47, $97) attract high-maintenance low-trust clients who churn. State the number plainly and stop talking.

### Justifying the price
Never defend the fee. Reframe the comparison against the client's own job value. A plumbing call is $250–$350. A roofing job is $5,000–$8,300. A dental patient is worth $4,000–$10,000 over their lifetime. "If this brings you one extra job a month it pays for itself many times over."

## 3. The sequence arguments

Three frameworks making one claim: order is the method.

### The 5 Ms: Model, Market, Message, Media, Machine
**Model** decides what you sell, how it's packaged, priced and delivered. Without a clear offer you have an expensive hobby. **Market** defines who gets maximum value fastest, across demographics, geographics and psychographics. **Message** bridges the two, built from their miseries and miracles. **Media** picks channels and formats, and only amplifies whatever the first three produced. **Machine** is the funnel and automation processing the attention Media generates.

Each step is unanswerable until the previous one is settled. Starting with Media, jumping on TikTok, launching ads, is the failure he calls the number one marketing sin, because a weak Model amplified is just louder confusion.

### The marketing hierarchy: Tricks, Tools, Tactics, Strategy, Psychology
Bottom to top: tricks and hacks (button colours, posting times, trending audio, "strapping rockets to a bicycle"), tools (CRMs, ad dashboards, infrastructure with no direction), tactics (a broadcast, a reel, a lead magnet, real moves that fail alone), strategy (which tactics to run and which to ignore; the 5 Ms live here), psychology (the peak).

Psychology sits on top because technology changes constantly and human wiring has not moved in 10,000 years. The brain still runs on seek pleasure, avoid pain, conserve energy.

His summary: *"Tactics without strategy is noise, and strategy without psychology is just smart guessing."*

Starting at the base is normal. Staying there is the failure.

### The Google build order
Map Pack first, no media budget, fastest calls. Then organic and a dedicated landing page, never the homepage. Then ads, once the page converts. The 5 Ms applied to one channel: Machine before Media.

## 4. The Rule of One

One person (ideal customer avatar), one problem, one promise, one path, one price, one platform. It applies to any business under $1M/year.

The execution version is the **One-Block Method**: one trade (plumbers, roofers, never "small businesses"), one urgent pain, one standardised offer, one channel run at volume.

He tested the alternative. In 2017 he built an agency site listing 11 services. It made $0 in 60 days. He deleted ten, left Google reviews, and signed 7 clients in the next 30.

### Choosing the niche
Five rules: one niche only; more specific is better; they must *need* what you sell (start from their pain, not your offer); they must afford it ($500–$15,000 per customer); the market must be growing, speedboats, not tugboats.

The Boring Gold Mine filters: boring beats popular; Maps over ads (target businesses chosen on Google Maps in 30 seconds); and a review gap under 50, or unreplied reviews.

**Runway versus destination.** Crowded niches, dentists, chiropractors, realtors, lawyers, are proven and profitable, which is why he teaches with them. They are destinations, not starting blocks. A beginner's first niche is a runway: garage doors, tree services, junk removal, the ugly set nobody pitches. Get 3 to 10 clients and real case studies there. Enter a crowded niche only with prior domain background, post-runway proof, a drilled-down sub-niche (orthodontics, not dentists; estate law, not lawyers), or warm local relationships.

## 5. Sales psychology

### Selective Authority: the stance
Four named moves at four points in the funnel, all running on psychological reactance (Brehm, 1960s) `[G]`: restricting access triggers a drive to restore it.

- **Velvet rope**, before contact. An application form and pre-call diagnostic content instead of an open calendar link.
- **Authority reversal**, at first reply. The frame of a diagnosing physician. The person asking the questions holds the leverage.
- **The doorman**, during qualification. State standards and evaluate whether they qualify.
- **The red button**, at the close. Give them explicit freedom to decline.

Together they swap the prospect's question. They stop asking "do I want this?" and start asking "am I good enough for this?"

**All four fail the same way: incongruence.** Fake selectivity is detected instantly. Arrogance reads as a bouncer on a power trip. Premium positioning on mediocre delivery is expensive disappointment. Running an application funnel while also sending needy cold DMs destroys the position. And the stance requires the ability to actually walk away, run from financial desperation, the detachment is fake and the pullback cannot be executed. He tells beginners to build momentum and case studies first.

### The Gatekeeper Method, in four moves
1. **Open (0–60s):** "Thanks for reaching out. Before we dive in, I want to make sure we're a good fit. I'd hate for either of us to waste time if this isn't the right approach for where you're at. I've learned my method works really well for certain situations, but not all. Does that make sense?"
2. **Criteria reveal (2–5 min):** name two paths. "This approach works best for people who are already generating revenue but feel stuck… people who need a few weeks to think it over usually end up not moving forward."
3. **Mirror close (5–10 min):** "From everything you've just told me, I think this would work really well for you. The real question though is, does this feel like the right fit for what you're trying to accomplish?"
4. **Final filter and freedom close:** "Just so I'm clear, you're ready to implement this week, right?" then "I think this could really work for you, but you are completely free to accept or refuse."

On hesitation, never push and never defend the price. Open the exit wider.

### Releasing pressure is what produces the yes
The single most repeated mechanism in the corpus. Christopher Carpenter's 2013 meta-analysis over 42 studies and 22,000+ subjects found that adding "but you are free to say no" roughly doubles compliance `[G]`.

It shows up everywhere: the Freedom Close, the Gatekeeper pullback, the graceful-exit follow-up message he says produces the highest response rate of the sequence, and the closing tag on choice architecture.

The silences are specified. Three seconds after the prospect confirms their monthly losses. Five to seven after the freedom close, do not fill it, do not backpedal. If they ask "are you still there?", say "yep I'm here" and keep waiting.

### Pain escalation
Latent (the pain is there but intellectualised away, "yeah, I'm doing all right") → Realized (a question opens a crack) → Extreme (the emotional cost of inaction becomes the thing they need out of, which is where the sale happens).

The sequence: "What's the single biggest problem you're struggling with right now?" → "What happens if you're still doing the exact same thing in 2, 3, or 5 years?" → "What made you reach out today instead of last month?" → "On a scale of 1 to 10, how painful is this problem for you right now?"

The sharpest move is the flip. When they answer 8: **"You said it's an 8. What would make it a 10?"** They describe the full cost in their own words.

Below 7, he stops. "Sounds like this isn't a critical priority right now, why don't we reconnect when it becomes more urgent."

### The ethics he states
He calls this material dark psychology and draws the lines himself. *"Psychology without empathy is manipulation, and people can smell it."* Do not use the triggers if the offer does not solve the problem. Real selectivity, never fake countdown timers or manufactured spots. Do not fabricate experience, if you are new, say "you'd be one of my first clients." And his defence of speed: helping someone decide quickly is more ethical than leaving them stuck in a problem costing them money daily.

### The named biases
Halo effect (audit the first touchpoint, it buffers later mistakes). Mere exposure (7 to 14 touchpoints). Loss aversion at roughly twice the weight of gain `[G]`, so headline the cost of inaction. Anchoring (high number first, always). Reciprocity (the free audit). Scarcity and urgency, genuine only. The yes ladder (a one-click quiz before the real ask). The trust equation (publish your work so competence is observed before contact). Dangerous simplicity, "complexity feels like work, simplicity feels like momentum."

Iyengar's jam study `[G]`: 24 choices produced a 3% buy rate, six produced 30%. This is why the menu is banned.

## 6. Acquisition

### The audit is the unit
Permission first, always. Never attach an unsolicited video. Send two or three sentences naming a specific visible gap and ask:

> "Hey [Name], I was searching for a [business type] in [City] and noticed a gap on your Google listing that's probably costing you calls. I recorded a quick 2-minute video breakdown of what's going on. Want me to send it over?"

The Stealth variant does not record anything until they say yes.

The video runs 2 to 3 minutes in Loom, four parts: show the problem on screen (their profile beside a competitor's), show the fix running live in your test account, state the price against the return, give one instruction. No discovery call, booking one reintroduces the friction the audit just removed.

Over five minutes and it gets closed halfway, because it is watched on a phone between jobs.

Cold email converts at 0.7%, about 140 messages per client. A personalised 2-minute audit converts at 10–15%. That gap is the entire argument.

### Warm before cold
**The Bullseye Method.** Set a 20-minute timer. List everyone across six categories: every job worked, every place lived, every school attended, every sport or club, every family member running a business, every friend whose parents or siblings run one. Do not filter. Expect 30 to 100+ names. Then run a 2-minute audit on each business, and message only the top 5 with the worst visible problems.

**Warm Water.** Location (be where they already are, the coffee shop, the gym, your barber, the contractor at your house), readiness (capture existing demand, don't create it, listen for complaints about software bloat and missed calls), systems (one consolidated platform replacing their scattered tools).

One local win is socially visible, so one client yields 3 to 5 referrals within weeks. He reports warm-water retention averaging 38 months against 6 to 8 for cold online clients.

**The referral ask**, credited to Phil Jones: *"You wouldn't happen to know just one person, someone who, just like you, would benefit from [specific benefit]?"* Asking for one avoids the choice paralysis that asking for three produces. Ask a few weeks in, right after a visible win, never during a problem.

### The Reply Trap
A prospect says "I'm interested" and the seller dumps process, pricing, case studies and services. In his image: they made a small bet and you shoved your whole stack in and flipped your cards over.

The escape, in order: **Acknowledge** ("Before I dive into details, I want to make sure this is actually the right fit for what you're dealing with. Mind if I ask you a couple questions?") → **Filter** (one or two diagnostic questions) → **Reveal** ("The reason I ask is because I found this approach works best for people who…") → **Controlled advance** ("Based on what you've told me, I think we could probably help with this. If you want, I can send over a link to grab 15 minutes this week…").

### Volume
The Rule of 100: 100 points of contact per workday before any non-revenue task. Outreach scales linearly where paid ads do not, 100 attempts yielding 15 replies, 4 calls and 1 client means 200 attempts yields 2 clients.

Sequence: day 1 the observation, day 2 a soft bump carrying something new, day 5 a value nudge, day 7–10 the breakup. Never a generic "just checking in."

80% of sales need an average of five follow-ups. 44% of sellers quit after one and 94% quit after four.

### Prospecting
Google Maps, "niche + city", skipping the top ads and ranked listings for pages 2 and 3, or smaller suburban towns. Page 1 has no gap to sell against; page 4 has no business to save.

**Ghost Town signals**, two of five confirms one: under 30–50 reviews while competitors sit at 100–300+; last review months or years ago; zero owner replies; unclaimed or incomplete profile; invisible to AI search.

**Qualify on:** real revenue and leads but poor conversion; ready to implement within 30 days; process curiosity, meaning they ask about methodology and their own duties rather than how fast they get rich; budget and decision authority.

**Disqualify on:** refuses to watch the material you sent; expects results with no participation; no authority or budget; unrealistic urgency and drama; zero revenue; and the over-optimised winner already in the top 3 with 500+ reviews and an agency.

## 7. Local and search

### The Anchor Method: three signals
**Words.** AI reads the text inside reviews, not just the count. Twelve customers writing "fixed my leak the same day" teaches it that capability. Reviews older than two years read as possibly-closed.

**Signals of life.** Real unpolished phone photos of jobs, trucks and team, 2 to 6 a month. AI-drafted replies to 4 and 5 star reviews on a 10–15 minute delay so they don't read as robotic, with anything under 4 stars flagged for a human inside 24 hours. A short weekly post. Target a 100% response rate.

**Profile completeness.** Every field: name, categories, description, address, phone, exact hours including holidays, itemised services, website and booking links. One to two hours once, then a 5–10 minute monthly check. He puts over half of local service profiles as missing critical fields.

**NAP consistency** underneath all of it: name, address and phone 100% character-for-character identical everywhere. "Suite 1" on Google against "ST 1" elsewhere splits the ranking authority.

### The AI search shift
Ask Maps answers conversational questions and returns one to four businesses. In a Seattle plumber test with 90+ companies it named four, leaving 86 invisible. Visibility goes from tiered to binary.

**Complete beats better.** A GatherUp study found Google's AI recommended a 3.3-star car wash over 4.8-star competitors because its listing carried clearance height and hours `[G]`. GatherUp also found 45% of consumers weigh review recency over the star rating `[G]`.

This is also the sales wedge. An owner whose phone went quiet does not know why. Pitching web design is hard because they think that's handled; showing them AI naming three competitors and not them creates urgency immediately.

### Review generation, and the compliance rule
Trigger on job completion. Message 1 is a check-in with **no ask and no link**, "just checking in to make sure everything looks good with the work we did today." Message 2 an hour or two later carries the request and the direct Google link. Message 3 after 24–48 hours only if the link went unclicked. Message 4 an email fallback. The sequence halts the instant the link is clicked or they reply STOP.

**No review gating, ever.** Every customer gets the identical request and the identical direct Google link regardless of sentiment. Filtering happy customers to Google while routing unhappy ones to a private form breaks Google's terms and FTC rules, and risks the reviews being wiped or the profile suspended. Message 1 is what catches a complaint before the public request goes out, a quality gate that happens to be compliant, rather than a gate on who gets asked.

Harvard Business School: a one-star rating increase moves revenue 5–9% `[G]`.

## 8. Metrics and thresholds

### Red / Yellow / Green
**Red**, below breakeven after the learning phase. Pause immediately, read the funnel metrics, fix the leak, relaunch.
**Yellow**, breakeven to 1.5x. Keep running, optimise, and do **not** scale. Raising budget on a breakeven ad spikes CPM and turns it red. This is the rule he says people break most.
**Green**, 3x+ ROAS. Scale, duplicate, double down.

### Benchmarks
Magic metric: $1 in, $1 out. Break even on the front end and volume becomes unlimited while backend lifetime value is the profit.

Landing page opt-in 20–30%, up to 33% optimised. Direct sales pages 2–5%. Discovery call close 25% for a beginner, 33–45%+ with pre-call framing. Cold outreach 1% bulk email, 10–15% personalised video audits. Link CTR 1–3%+, he ignores overall CTR as performance theatre. Judge cost per lead against earnings per lead, never alone.

### Timing
The 72-hour rule: launch and do not touch it for 3 to 4 days. Kill anything still below breakeven after that. Scale green lights 10–20% every few days. Ugly beats polished, raw graphics and casual iPhone footage break the feed pattern and cost less per click.

### Budget
Daily ad-set budget at 3 to 5x target CPA, so the algorithm gets 3–5 conversions a day. A $5 lead goal means $15–$25/day.

## 9. Constraints

### Never
Charge hourly, it punishes efficiency and caps income. Do custom work or custom proposals. Offer a menu of services. Sell untrackable vanity services. Do manually what software automates. Prioritise non-revenue activity. Keep toxic clients, rank them and trim the bottom 10%. Rely on long high-pressure calls. Build the solution before selling it.

**Only promise what you control.** Never a #1 ranking, never a revenue figure. Promise profile optimisation, requests sent, 100% of missed calls answered. The sharpest constraint in the corpus and the one most at odds with how this category usually sells.

### Named anti-patterns
**Random Acts of Marketing**, disconnected activity with no overarching strategy. "You can't scale chaos."
**The Baskin-Robbins Agency**, 31 flavours, which triggers the paradox of choice.
**Productive procrastination**, logos, colours, decks, course after course. Fear of rejection dressed as preparation.
**Hope marketing**, word of mouth and referrals only, producing a feast-and-famine cycle run out of fear.
**The Squirrel Maze**, a funnel where the audience isn't. "Building a squirrel maze in a yard full of rabbits and deer."
**The Offer-First Mentality**, picking Facebook ads, then hunting for someone to sell them to.
**The Vanity Metrics Trap**, likes and views instead of conversion, CAC, ROAS and earnings per lead.
**Build It and They Will Come**, "it is not the best business that wins, it is the business with the best marketing."

### Churn
76% of clients cancel a traditional agency inside 90 days. The average agency client lasts 2 months. Four causes, in his order: no quantifiable ROI, under-communication (taking the money and working in silence, so the client assumes nothing is happening), single-service dependence, and keeping toxic clients.

Under-communication is the one that costs nothing to fix, which is why his retention system is built almost entirely out of reporting. Clients don't leave because of bad results; they leave because they can't see the results.

## 10. What he says fails

He prices his own failures, which is rare in this category.

A $25,000 print campaign for an industrial drilling client that produced zero traceable calls and wiped out their annual budget. A first client charged $100 for a full campaign, working out under $5 an hour. Five thousand verified dental leads at $1–$2 each that the dentist never contacted, all 5,000 went cold, which is the strongest argument for speed-to-lead anywhere in the corpus and an argument against his own delivery. A $1M/year agency at 20% margin where he felt like an overworked HR mediator. The $1M course business shut down after doctor-diagnosed stress burnout. A 48-hour ChatGPT poetry business: 141 page views, 1 sale. Nine hours building a sample website for a roofer, then silence. Losing a client who was half of agency revenue overnight.

### Timelines, honestly
Beginners expect linear progress or a 90-day exit. Reality is flat for a long stretch then parabolic, and most people quit in the flat part and conclude the model doesn't work.

His first agency took 3 years to reach $100K/month. His second reached it in 30 days, on systems and a productized offer.

First client: 1 to 3 weeks of daily action. Niche mastery: staying 18 to 24 months.

**Buyer timing splits 3 / 10 / 87.** Three percent buy now (0–30 days), 10% soon (30–90), 87% later (90 days to 18 months). Nearly nine in ten of the market is not buying in the window most outreach is measured over. That split is the justification for everything he does with nurture and reactivation.

### Survivorship bias
He teaches it through Abraham Wald and the WWII bombers. The military wanted to armour where the returning planes were hit, until Wald pointed out they were only looking at the ones that came back. Beginners copy a guru's current behaviour, post daily, follow your passion, without seeing that the guru already has cash flow, an offer and an audience.

A marketing educator naming the bias that makes marketing education unreliable, including his own.

## 11. Where he has changed his mind

The notebook exposes no dates, so this is nearly the only place the corpus has a timeline.

He resisted niching down for fear of leaving money on the table, ran vets, realtors, fitness coaches and restaurants at once, burned out, and changed. He taught SMART goals, then concluded standards and identity drive change and goals do not. He mocked visualisation, then changed after reading on the reticular activating system. He believed professionalism meant a 20-slide deck, until he ran out of time to build one, stated his price flat, and got an instant yes. He built the 11-service site, made $0, and deleted ten services.

And the largest: he shut a $1M/year course business for a lean agency, arguing the course model is a content treadmill and local owners will pay month after month for real results because they do not care about follower counts.
