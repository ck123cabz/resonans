## Content-Led Growth

Michel's operating thesis is that the best way to acquire B2B clients is to give away the exact methodology you would execute for them. The logic is straightforward: target buyers at companies with high average contract values have the budget to hire an agency but lack the time to implement complex workflows themselves. By publishing the complete playbook, you prove competence rather than claiming it. The only people who will take your free advice and run with it are competitors or companies without budget. Neither of those were going to pay you anyway.

This philosophy sits at the foundation of everything ColdIQ does. It shapes which content Michel publishes, how he structures cold emails, and why he embeds functional tools in blog articles. Michel calls this the "trust moat" strategy: unlike cold email where every new message starts at zero trust, content compounds over time. A prospect might follow Michel's LinkedIn for six months without ever engaging (no likes, no comments) and then sign as a client the moment they actually need outbound services. Michel cites a specific client who did exactly that.

**LinkedIn as the primary engine.** LinkedIn generates over 70% of ColdIQ's leads and books 100+ qualified B2B meetings per month. Michel committed to posting daily, every working day, starting in February 2023. He gave himself a hard rule: post for three months straight before allowing himself to give up. It took 31 consecutive posts to generate a single lead. Today, LinkedIn engagement generates 8 out of 13 new deals in a given month and drives roughly 10,000 of 40,000+ monthly website visits.

The content that performs best is highly practical and solves an immediate problem. Michel avoids theoretical thought leadership. His most-engaged posts are tech stack breakdowns (the "every tool we use to run our $Xm agency" format) which he has published five or more times, each hitting 100K+ impressions. He writes 100% of his own content, spending roughly three hours on weekends drafting material. His team handles posting, comment management, and engagement mechanics. He doesn't log into LinkedIn himself anymore. The platform is "incredibly draining" and managing comments is "almost a full-time job" handled by a specialized team.

Michel's ideation process uses Taplio to find outlier posts in his niche (filtering for 300+ likes), then reverse-engineers the structure, hooks, and CTAs. Scripe adds author-level analysis, spotting when the same creator hits viral engagement on multiple angles of the same subject. He spends about 30 minutes per week on ideation, producing enough material for daily posting. For the actual writing, he uses Claude Code (Opus model) through Conductor to generate hook variations, repurpose past content, and brainstorm fresh angles. Most posts take 15-20 minutes from idea to final draft.

Michel strongly advocates mastering a single channel before adding others. He argues that focusing heavily on one platform yields a thousand-fold better results than poorly repurposing content across 15 different channels. His specific advice: completely dominate LinkedIn, then perhaps add highly complementary formats like a newsletter or YouTube, rather than diluting quality by posting simultaneously to X, TikTok, and Instagram. He admits his LinkedIn posts "take hours of his weekend" and the quality standard he maintains requires massive focus and energy.

**Visual leverage.** Pairing a text post with a custom infographic or schema increases impressions 3-10x. The visual occupies the entire screen on mobile, acting as a "second hook" that stops the scroll. Michel hired an in-house design team (Ada, Pilar, Maria Luisa) and tells employees explicitly: "don't even try to do your own design, just ask her to do whatever you need done." The investment pays for itself in engagement multiples. Carousel posts consistently outperform every other format in their data: more saves, more shares, more profile visits. Michel says he spends more time on visuals than on hooks. The hook is two lines occupying minimal feed real estate. A great visual takes up the entire phone screen and, if done well, functions as a standalone post.

**Algorithmic tactics.** Comment on other creators' posts for 30 minutes before publishing your own. Reply to every comment on your post within the first 30 minutes. Both behaviors signal to the LinkedIn algorithm that you are an active participant, not a broadcaster. An experiment Michel references found that commenting alone earned more followers than posting alone. Posting plus commenting together drove the most growth. GIFs outperform equivalent short video posts for reasons Michel finds unclear but treats as a reliable pattern.

**Content repurposing.** Most viewers forget your posts. New followers never saw them. Michel's top posts (tech stack breakdowns, milestone updates) get republished every couple of months with minor updates. They perform nearly as well each time. Michel points to the biggest LinkedIn creators and notes they've published their most viral posts "over and over" and they keep performing. This is the simplest way to maintain consistency with material already proven to convert.

**Anti-AI-slop stance.** Michel rejects fully autonomous AI content generation. Founders who use AI to scrape news and produce 100 LinkedIn posts in 15 minutes get zero engagement. He draws a clear line between using AI as a drafting assistant (human direction, AI execution) and using AI to replace the thinking entirely. The distinction matters because the practical value of his content comes from documenting actual problems his team solved that week. You cannot automate that.

He anticipates a future where text content will be indistinguishable from AI output, and is shifting toward formats that cannot be faked: live events, video walkthroughs, interactive tools embedded in web pages. He is actively planning a large in-person event in Barcelona and believes that "interactive in-person live content" will become a premium lead generation channel as buyers lose trust in text.

**YouTube.** Michel uses YouTube for long-form, highly technical content that won't fit in a LinkedIn post. Content includes deep-dive podcast interviews and extensive screen-share tutorials (including an hour-long walkthrough of Claude Code for outbound). ## Marketing Psychology

Michel approaches cold email and content through a psychological lens rather than a purely tactical one. Four principles form his framework.

**The defensive inbox-zero mindset.** Prospects opening their inbox are trying to delete emails, not discover new solutions. Their default assumption is that any unsolicited email is trying to scam them. Cold outreach starts with zero trust. This is why Michel's content strategy matters so much to his outbound results: when a prospect receives a cold email and looks up the sender, seeing months of practical content validates the sender's expertise immediately. The content is the trust credential.

**The starving crowd principle.** From Gary Halbert: having a "starving crowd" is infinitely more powerful than having the best product. In cold email terms, finding a prospect with an active pain point overrides the need for clever copy. A great offer with zero personalization beats a bad offer with deep personalization every time. ColdIQ's co-founder Alex cites a campaign where an executive sent completely unpersonalized emails offering to pay prospects to act as product advisors. No first name variable, no company mention, nothing custom. 20 out of 20 replies were positive. The offer was valuable enough to make personalization irrelevant.

**The anti-gimmick radar.** Buyers are educated on sales tactics and instantly detect fake personalization. Mentioning which college someone attended, using "Sent from my iPhone" signatures, or fabricating personal connections are recognized as AI-generated gimmicks. Instead of faking familiarity, Michel uses AI to surface real business signals: a company missing DMARC records, hiring in specific departments, raising money, or experiencing leadership changes. The rule is that the message should make the prospect feel it could only have been sent to them because it addresses their actual current business situation. If the message is genuinely relevant, the prospect doesn't care whether an AI or a human wrote it.

**The time vs. money asymmetry.** Founders fear that giving away solutions means prospects will just implement them internally and never hire an agency. Michel calls this a cognitive distortion. His ideal buyers (CEOs and VPs at companies with high average contract values) have budget but are starved for time. They will read the free guide, see that Michel's team knows exactly how to fix their problem, and pay ColdIQ to implement it because they don't have the hours to do it themselves. The free advice is the sales pitch.

These four principles are interconnected. The defensive inbox-zero mindset explains why cold email alone has diminishing returns and why content matters. The starving crowd principle tells you to invest time in targeting rather than copywriting. The anti-gimmick radar constrains how you use AI in personalization. And the time/money asymmetry tells you to lead with value rather than asks. Together they produce Michel's fundamental cold email philosophy: find someone with a bleeding problem, demonstrate you can fix it for free, and make it easy for them to pay you to do it because they don't have the time.

This is the direct opposite of the typical agency approach, which is to hide methodology behind sales calls and pitch decks. Michel's position is that the methodology should be the marketing. Every ColdIQ blog article, every LinkedIn post, every YouTube tutorial is simultaneously content and a sales pitch because it proves competence through demonstration rather than claims.

## Cold Email Methodology

**Framework 1: Hyper-Relevant Free Value.** Identify a specific technical pain point using intent data (example: companies missing DMARC records + using a legacy sales platform + large SDR team). Don't pitch a paid service. Offer a free resource that solves the exact problem. Email structure: state the observation ("noticed you're using [platform] and missing DMARC records"), add social proof ("spoke with 5 companies with the same setup, all struggling at 10% open rates"), give a soft CTA ("I compiled a guide on how we helped them go from 10% to 60%. Do you want this guide?"). This is a "no-brainer" for the prospect to accept.

**Framework 2: Relevance at Scale.** Never use AI to fake personalization (mentioning college, fabricating personal details). Use AI to surface real business signals: 4-5 data points per prospect (sustainable initiatives, specific department hiring, funding events, leadership changes). The message should make the prospect feel it could only have been sent to them.

**Framework 3: Irresistible Offer.** When the offer itself is valuable enough, personalization becomes optional. Example: executive sent completely templated cold email offering to pay prospects for their time as product advisors. No first name, no company name, no customization. 20/20 positive replies. Great offer always beats deep personalization with a weak offer.

**Framework 4: Inbound-Led Outbound.** Target people who already interacted with your brand (liked posts, viewed profile, visited website). The opening context references the recent interaction. Friction is greatly reduced because the prospect already has some trust.

**Waterfall personalization (Clay workflow).** A 65-step automation: (1) Check if prospect posted in last 2 weeks, write icebreaker referencing their post. (2) If not, check for new hires in their department, mention the new hire to congratulate growth. (3) If not, check tenure for milestones. (4) Fallback: personalize on the fact they own a niche business. Generated 152 leads in one campaign.

**Sequence structure.** Industry is shifting from 7-step to ~4-step sequences. Shorter sequences protect deliverability and prevent prospects from marking emails as spam. If a sequence finishes without reply, wait 60 days before re-engaging the same account by targeting different people at the same company. Trigger-based follow-ups: if a prospect re-opens an old email 3-4 months later, follow up immediately.

**Reply handling SOPs.** Speed is everything. Monitor the Slack channel and reply as fast as possible. Never send a Calendly link. Prospects who reply positively almost always have clarifying questions before booking. Answer those questions to keep the conversation alive. Be prepared to manually follow up multiple times. Most interested prospects get distracted and forget to reply again.

**The manual-first rule.** Before buying any automation tools, write 50 to 150 highly researched emails by hand. This tedious process uncovers the exact business initiatives, buying signals, and patterns that make a prospect an ideal fit. Only after discovering these human patterns do you buy tools to replicate the logic at scale. Skipping the manual phase means guessing at scale, which burns domains, wastes data credits, and produces sub-1% reply rates. The beauty of manual writing is that patterns emerge organically. You discover that 15 out of 20 prospects share the same pain point. You notice a specific phrase gets replies while another falls flat. You realize the job title you assumed was your buyer is not the one who actually cares about your offer. Those patterns become Clay variables and scale across thousands of prospects with personalization that still feels one-to-one.

**The 15-Minute SDR onboarding hack.** The most important question when onboarding a new agency client: "If you had 15 minutes to research a prospect, what would you research about them?" The client's answer becomes the exact blueprint for what ColdIQ programs into Clay to automate. Whatever manual research steps the client describes (checking hiring, looking at tech stack, checking funding) are precisely what gets replicated at scale.

**Deliverability as engineering.** Michel stresses that deliverability is currently the biggest challenge in B2B outbound. The days of blasting a list and getting 70-80% open rates are over. Required technical setup: SPF, DKIM, and DMARC records properly configured before sending. Never use your company's main domain for cold outreach. Purchase secondary domains and set up multiple inboxes. Use platforms with inbox rotation and warm-up pools (Smartlead, Instantly). Use spintax to vary each message and avoid triggering spam filters. Send fewer than 50 emails per day per mailbox. Never track open rates. Michel frequently encounters teams on legacy platforms (Outreach, SalesLoft) struggling to get even 10% open rates because the tools aren't built for modern deliverability. His direct advice: "just change tool."

## AI and Automation

**Claude Code as GTM infrastructure.** ColdIQ uses Claude Code to run complete outbound campaigns from a single terminal session, eliminating the tool-to-tool handoffs that make campaign building slow and error-prone. A typical outbound campaign involves five distinct phases: list building, ICP scoring, email enrichment, copy personalization, and campaign upload. Each phase traditionally requires a different tool, a different interface, and a different skill set. The transitions between tools introduce friction: exporting CSVs, reformatting columns, cleaning data, pasting into new platforms, and repeating. A single campaign for 500 leads takes 3-5 hours of manual work before a single email goes out.

Claude Code collapses all of that. Kenny drops a CSV of target companies into a folder, writes a natural language prompt with scoring criteria, and Claude Code takes over: reads every company in the CSV, scores them against ICP criteria (industry fit, company size, growth signals, tech stack indicators), accesses Apollo's API to find verified email addresses, automatically cascades through additional enrichment providers (Prospeo, FullEnrich, Wiza) for contacts Apollo misses, filters out low-confidence results, writes personalized copy based on historical winning templates (under 100 words, subject lines that look like they came from a colleague, yes/no CTAs, first line that answers "why you, why now"), and pushes the complete campaign (leads, sequences, copy) directly into Instantly. What took an SDR hours across multiple siloed tools took 57 seconds in the original demo. A later live demonstration with 1,400 viewers took under 20 minutes for a complete campaign.

The architecture behind this: 32 skills, 12 agents, 2,108 files. The scoring criteria can be updated by changing the prompt in plain English. No formula rebuilds, no column reconfiguration. For teams running multiple campaigns per week, this cuts the list qualification step from 45 minutes to under 3 minutes.

**The 19-API stack.** For Claude Code GTM automation, organized by layer: Signal (PredictLeads, Common Room, Attention, Midbound/RB2B/Vector), Data (Prospeo, FullEnrich, Openmart, Apollo, Wiza, Apify), Action (Instantly, lemlist, LinkedIn Ads), Automation (Relevance AI, n8n), System of Record (Attio), Conversion (Cal.com), Revenue (Hyperline, Dreamdata).

**AI SDR position.** Michel holds a nuanced view that sounds contradictory but is internally consistent. He warns founders against buying AI SDRs without first proving a sales motion manually. If you haven't figured out what messaging works by writing emails by hand, an AI tool will just automate failure at scale. At the same time, he is bullish on vertical-specific AI SDRs. In unscripted podcast conversations, he admits this is a "controversial take" but believes AI models already outcompete the average seller at email prospecting. Top-tier humans will always win on creativity. But the average SDR is already being surpassed.

**Agent taxonomy.** Michel distinguishes three tiers: (A) Non-agentic workflows where humans do all thinking and AI just helps with content. (B) Agentic workflows with triggers and logic that are semi-autonomous but cannot adapt to unexpected scenarios. (C) True AI agents with goal-oriented objectives that monitor multiple systems, personalize based on context, coordinate tools, and follow up autonomously. Most of what companies call "AI agents" are actually type B agentic workflows.

**Clay formula trick.** When using AI to generate personalized first lines, a common failure mode is the AI inserting conversational filler ("The final answer is: [Personalized Line]"). To prevent these hallucinations from reaching prospects, use Clay's formula feature to set a strict rule: only pass the output to the email sequencer if it starts with the exact formatting requested. This catches junk data before any email is sent.

**RB2B as agency trust builder.** Beyond its core function of identifying anonymous website visitors, RB2B has a secondary use that Michel values highly: instant client trust. When ColdIQ installs the tracking script for a new agency client, the client suddenly starts getting Slack notifications showing the exact names, job titles, and LinkedIn profiles of people browsing their website. This creates an immediate "wow effect" that surprises the client and validates the agency's technical capability before a single email is even sent.

**Lookalike generation.** When an outbound campaign starts succeeding, ColdIQ uses tools like Ocean.io to keep momentum going. They feed the successful, converting companies into the tool, and it generates a "lookalike" list of similar companies. This continuously feeds fresh, high-probability leads into the winning sequence without starting from scratch. Some workflows automate this: detecting when a lead converts and automatically generating 10 lookalike leads to feed back into the sequence.

**Warnings about automation.** Michel is emphatic on one point: the biggest mistake founders make with outbound automation is buying tools before proving the motion manually. If you haven't hand-written 50+ emails and discovered which messaging, which signals, and which buyer personas actually produce replies, buying Instantly or Clay or any AI SDR product will just automate failure at scale. The tools exist to replicate proven human patterns, not to skip the discovery phase. Similarly, Michel warns against the temptation to immediately scrape massive LinkedIn lists and blast generic messages to see who replies. This destroys domain reputation, burns through your TAM, and teaches you nothing about what works.

## AI Coding Stack

Michel runs ColdIQ's entire GTM engineering operation through a specific AI coding stack, replacing traditional tooling with natural language-driven workflows that collapse hours of multi-tool campaign building into minutes.

**Conductor** is Michel's primary interface. It wraps both Claude Code and OpenAI Codex into a single UI, providing a much smoother experience than the raw terminal. The key advantage is the ability to toggle effortlessly between different AI models and run multiple agents concurrently on separate branches. For non-technical team members, Ivan built custom interfaces on top of Conductor's outputs so people can manage campaigns without touching a terminal.

**Claude Code** is the central engine. Michel pays for the $200/month Max plan and uses it for everything: API interactions with Apollo, Prospeo, Exa, and FullEnrich for list building; automated ICP scoring using Python scripts against raw CSV data; AI-generated copy pulled from historically successful templates; and direct campaign uploads to Instantly via API. The workflow that demonstrates this best: Kenny drops a CSV of target companies, types natural language scoring criteria, and Claude Code reads the file, evaluates every company, extracts decision-maker contacts via Apollo, cascades through secondary enrichment providers for missed emails, writes personalized copy, and pushes the complete campaign into the sequencer. What an SDR does across five tools in three hours, Claude Code handles in under 20 minutes.

**OpenAI Codex** serves as a $20/month backup. Michel keeps a subscription because certain professionals report that Codex handles complex debugging tasks better than Claude models. When Claude Code fails to complete a build after a few attempts, Michel feeds the entire conversation into Codex, which typically solves the problem. He does not use Codex as his primary tool because Claude Code's unlimited plan makes it more cost-effective for sustained, concurrent agent usage.

**Cursor** was Michel's first AI coding platform but was abandoned when Claude Code launched its nearly unlimited $200/month plan. The models running natively within Claude Code produced consistently better outputs for GTM workflows than the same models accessed through Cursor's interface. Michel did have resistance to switching because he preferred Cursor's UI over the raw terminal, but Conductor resolved that.

**Lovable** was used to prototype ColdIQ's first interactive mini-tools (email finders, phone finders). It is the most user-friendly AI coding platform and Michel recommends it as a starting point for non-technical people. But the natural progression is to graduate from Lovable to Claude Code or Codex once you need more flexibility and modularity.

**Vercel** hosts all of ColdIQ's custom-built mini-apps and their entire website (migrated from Webflow). **GitHub** serves as the code repository for all AI-generated scripts and workflows.

**LinkedIn Ads API automation.** Ivan Falco automated their LinkedIn Ads workflow to bypass the platform's clunky native UI. The setup uses a Google Sheet as the command hub: campaign group, campaign name, targeting tags (bottom-of-funnel, ABM), ad copy, and a reference to a Google Drive folder containing the creative assets. Claude Code reads the spreadsheet, summarizes the proposed campaign for review, and builds it from scratch via the LinkedIn API. Ivan built this through Claude Code's "plan mode," iterating through trial and error until each action succeeded, then saving the successful code as a reusable "skill." The system also handles bulk updates to existing campaigns, bidding changes, and account list updates. Results: $15 CPMs on Thought Leader ads, $472K at 3x ROI for clients across six months.

## Operational Resilience

**The Dan/Fivos departure.** Dan Rosenthal and Fivos became two of ColdIQ's most important leaders. Dan was the first person trusted with service delivery, eventually becoming Head of GTM. Fivos joined as an SDR, rapidly mastered sales by listening to Michel's call recordings on Attention, and eventually closed over $2M in agency revenue. During a US trip, Michel noticed both seemed unmotivated and scattered. At dinner, they vaguely announced wanting to launch a new business. Michel offered to help fund their venture as a gesture of friendship.

What Michel later discovered was that they had secretly bought a domain and built a direct competitor at the exact moment they were pushing ColdIQ for more equity. On launch day, they copied ColdIQ's exact offers, messaging, and internal acquisition strategies. They blocked Michel and Alex on LinkedIn. The most painful aspect, in Michel's telling, was not the competition itself but the deception: they never stated openly what they were building, despite Michel explicitly offering to fund whatever business they wanted to create.

The outcome was instructive. ColdIQ had its best revenue month in company history immediately after the departure. Michel's takeaway: founders must make their business "bulletproof" by building systems robust enough that no single employee's departure threatens operations. If something important relies on one person and that person leaves, the business can collapse. At any time, you need to ask yourself: would we survive if that specific person left?

**Client retention as a growth lever.** Michel treats retention as more important than new client acquisition. Going from keeping a client 6 months on average to 12 months literally doubles the business because revenue is recurring. The most dangerous clients are pre-revenue startups and unproven products -- they blame the agency when outbound fails to sell a product the market doesn't want. Michel's philosophy: "You are not in the business of saving businesses, you're in the business of scaling businesses." This one-sentence filter eliminates the clients most likely to churn unhappily and drain delivery resources.

**Lessons from Lempire.** Michel's interview with Charles Tenot (CEO of Lempire, parent of Lemlist) provided several operational lessons he internalized. Lempire grew to $29M ARR entirely bootstrapped, primarily through content and personal branding. Multiple team members (Guillaume, Luca, Kevin, Roxanne) each carved out specific LinkedIn niches, creating a compounding audience effect that ColdIQ explicitly replicated with its own employee advocacy program. But Lempire also demonstrated the danger of over-diversification: running five SaaS products simultaneously (Lemlist, Lemwarm, Lemcal, Taplio, TweetHunter) caused execution limits that forced a painful company split. Michel recognized the same pattern in his own business and internalized the lesson that "the more revenue you make, the more women in the red dress appear" -- referring to attractive but distracting opportunities that pull focus from what actually makes money.

Another Lempire lesson: branded organic search was 95% of Lemlist's web traffic, creating massive dependency on founder reputation. When team members posted less and the founder got banned from LinkedIn repeatedly, the traffic model was exposed as fragile. Lempire had to diversify into SEO, affiliation, and paid social to de-risk. Michel applied this directly, building ColdIQ's five-source traffic model (LinkedIn, SEO, paid, cold outreach, other channels) rather than depending entirely on his personal LinkedIn audience.
