## Procrastination: the approach-avoidance mechanism

**What it is** `[D]` His model of what procrastination actually is. Procrastination is not a symptom
of laziness or a lack of motivation. It is "high motivation combined with inaction", which creates a
painful "I want to do it but I can't bring myself to do it" effect.

**When to use it** As the diagnosis that selects which fix applies.

**The two competing systems** `[D]`

| | Approach System | Avoidance System |
|---|---|---|
| Linked to | Pleasure and reward | Fear and anxiety |
| Pushes toward | Action | Inaction |
| Brain regions | Striatum, ventral tegmental area (VTA) | Amygdala, hippocampus |
| Driven by | Dopamine, which floods the brain to stimulate wanting and craving | Cortisol, which stimulates fear and avoidance |

**The tug-of-war** `[D]` The conflict produces a state where you experience the positive and negative
aspects of a task simultaneously. Physically away from the work, the positives are more salient and
approach tendency rises. The moment you sit down to start, the negatives become prominent and
avoidance tendency heavily increases. What the brain is ultimately trying to avoid is energy
expenditure, an evolutionary adaptation to preserve precious resources.

**Where it sits in the flow cycle** `[D]` He inserts an "engage" phase before struggle. The
approach-avoidance conflict occurs during engage and actively blocks entry to struggle. Because
struggle is an absolute prerequisite for flow, failing to engage means the flow cycle never initiates
at all. This addition is the join connecting his procrastination model to his flow model.

**The scaling rule** `[D]` Effort-to-engage scales avoidance directly. The more effort a task requires
to engage with, the harder the avoidance system fights back. Modifying a task so it requires less
effort makes it easier to step into struggle and cycle into flow. Every fix below is an application of
this one rule.

**Science under it** `[M:Kotler]` the four-phase flow cycle, struggle, release, flow, recovery, cited
flat in q29 with no attribution and upgraded per the profile rule that organisational proximity does
not make a borrowed construct his.

**Sources:** q29 (authoritative on mechanism), q30.

## Procrastination fixes, all seven

**What it is** `[D]` The complete prescription list from q30. Fixes 1 through 6 force engagement by
lowering the effort required. Fix 7 says to stop.

**When to use them** After the approach-avoidance diagnosis. The corpus supplies no test for choosing
between the push fixes and the stop fix, and that missing test is stated below.

**Fix 1, Wake Up and Flow.** Prepare the workspace the night before: laptop open, document loaded,
chair positioned. In the morning, roll out of bed and start typing within 60 to 90 seconds of opening
your eyes. Still half-asleep, the brain operates in Delta and Theta waves, which quiet the prefrontal
cortex, silencing the inner critic, and sit very close to the Alpha/Theta waves of flow. You leverage
the residual sleep state to start working before the conscious mind can mount resistance.

> Title note: the source video is "Give Me 56 Secs… I'll DELETE Your Procrastination". No 56-second
> step, threshold or measurement appears anywhere in the content. The only figure in the body is 60 to
> 90 seconds. Do not prescribe 56 seconds.

**Fix 2, Attention Recovery targets.** Train yourself to detect that your mind has wandered within 3
seconds, and to fully recover focus within 30 seconds. q30 states the baseline it replaces as "research
shows it takes the average worker exactly 23 minutes and 15 seconds to refocus after a single
interruption", naming no researcher and no study. q59 credits Gloria Mark (UC Irvine, 2004).

**Fix 3, the 3-2-10 Reset.** When distracted and wanting to procrastinate, execute a Flow State Breath
to calm the amygdala: inhale for 3 seconds, hold for 2 seconds, exhale slowly for 10 seconds. Then
immediately physically rewrite your clear goal to re-engage the prefrontal cortex.

**Fix 4, Task Decomposition.** Break the dreaded task into microscopic goals so tiny they require almost
zero energy to execute. The complete stated sequence: 1. Open the laptop. 2. Pull up the Google Doc.
3. Write the intro. Focusing on a massive outcome, like winning a game, requires too much cognitive
energy and triggers avoidance, so focus on specific targets for your attention instead. Microscopic
goals provide a steady drip of dopamine from bite-sized wins, making the work feel as easy as reactive
scrolling.

**Fix 5, Challenge-Skills Tuning.** Tune the task so its difficulty sits roughly 4% above your current
skill level. Three tuning methods: lower the hurdle, time-bound it, define the scope. The full method
list is under its own heading below.

**Fix 6, Bypassing.** Do not sit and deliberate, act immediately. Hit the "dial" button on a sales call
before you have a second to think about the rejection, the way you jump into a cold river before you
can talk yourself out of it. This works through response inhibition, the ability to override the
emotional brain, the amygdala, and execute a goal-directed behaviour before fear takes over.

**Fix 7, Reinterpreting Ambivalence.** Ambivalence often masquerades as procrastination. Continually
putting off a specific contract or project may be intuition signalling a bad deal, a misalignment with
your values, or a path leading to burnout. In those cases the fix is not to force the work but to heed
the signal and abandon the task altogether.

**The unresolved question** Fixes 1 through 6 and fix 7 are mutually exclusive treatments, and nothing
in the corpus tells you which case you are in. Pass 2 calls this the highest-value unresolved item in
the corpus, because "should I push or stop?" is the question a reader actually brings. A lens should
state the boundary aloud rather than pick.

**Every number in this section** `[D]` 60 to 90 seconds to start after waking · 3 seconds to detect
drift · 30 seconds to recover · 23 minutes 15 seconds as the untrained baseline · breath 3 seconds in,
2 seconds hold, 10 seconds out · challenge tuned to 4% above current skill · up to 70% of meetings
unnecessary (fix inside Schedule Repair, below) · a Swiss cheese calendar of 15-to-30-minute meetings.

**Sources:** q30 (authoritative protocol list), q29 (mechanism), q33, q24, q59.

## Challenge-Skills Tuning for a dreaded task

**What it is** `[M:Kotler]` `[M:Csikszentmihalyi]` Adjusting a task's difficulty until it sits in the
flow sweet spot, stated as roughly 4% above your current skill level. q30 states this without
attribution; the challenge-skills balance is Csikszentmihalyi's and the 4% figure is the Kotler and
Flow Research Collective popularization.

**When to use it** When the task is either so hard it triggers anxiety or so dull it triggers boredom.

**The three tuning methods, complete** `[D]`

1. **Lower the Hurdle.** If a task requires too much effort to start, for example making 250 cold
   calls, lower the initial barrier. Start the morning with a few easy warm calls to build confidence
   and momentum before tackling the harder work.
2. **Time-Bounding.** Use time to artificially manipulate difficulty. A painfully boring task such as
   tax returns gets its time shrunk to 1 hour instead of a week, to spike the challenge and force flow.
   An overwhelmingly complex task gets way more time than you think you need, to lower anxiety.
3. **Defining the Scope.** Uncertainty breeds anxiety, so clarify exactly what needs to be done and why
   it matters, to boost perceived importance and make engaging easier.

**Every number** `[D]` 4% above current skill level. 250 cold calls as the worked example of a task with
too high a hurdle. Tax returns compressed from a week to 1 hour.

**Evidence flag** The 4% figure arrives with no source, no measurement method, and no account of how
anyone detects a 4% gap. Pass 2 lists it among the seven residual unsourced claims that carry a protocol.

**Sources:** q30, q16, q40, q08, q59.

## The 3-2-10 Reset (the Flow State Breath)

**What it is** `[D]` A three-breath pattern used to break a distraction, calm the amygdala, and re-enter
the task. Neither source file is complete alone, so this is the union of both.

**When to use it** On catching yourself distracted, when attention slips inside a work block, and as the
closing step of the Cognitive Load Dump.

**The complete protocol** `[D]`

1. Inhale through your nose for 3 seconds.
2. Hold for 2 seconds.
3. Exhale slowly through your mouth for 10 seconds.
4. Repeat the cycle three times.
5. Immediately physically rewrite your clear goal, for example "write three sentences explaining the
   proposal", to manually re-engage the prefrontal cortex.

**Every number** `[D]` 3 seconds inhale, 2 seconds hold, 10 seconds exhale, 3 repetitions.

**Where each part comes from** q33 supplies "through your nose", "through your mouth" and "repeat this
three times". q30 supplies the follow-on step of rewriting the clear goal. q22 uses the same three
breaths as the closing Reset of the Cognitive Load Dump. q24 names it the Flow State Breath Reset.

**Mechanism** `[D]` The breath calms the sympathetic nervous system, clears the neurochemical chaos, and
activates the parasympathetic system. q24 states it activates the vagus nerve and creates a clean
neurological break from the distraction.

**Do not switch tasks** `[D]` q33 is explicit: if attention slips inside a certainty window, run the
breath rather than switching tasks.

**Companion move, Positional Rotation** `[D]` If attention drops, physically change your state. Stand up,
move to a different chair, or walk around. Changing physical position resets the brain's perceived
exertion, which refreshes attention span so you can finish the block.

**Science under it** `[M:Benson]` Herbert Benson (Harvard, 1970s) discovered the relaxation response,
proving we can voluntarily drop blood pressure, heart rate and metabolic rate. `[M:Peifer]` Karina Peifer
(Journal of Happiness Studies) showed flow occurs at a perfect balance of sympathetic and parasympathetic
activation.

**Ambiguity to carry** `[A]` A separate file states the breath as "3 rounds (or 9 total breaths)" and
never defines a round. The most-used protocol in the corpus has an ambiguous dosage.

**Sources:** q30, q33, q22, q24, q46, q55, q59.

## Schedule Repair (for procrastination)

**What it is** `[D]` Fixing the calendar so the brain's flow-payoff calculation comes out in favour of
starting.

**When to use it** When you procrastinate on days that look fragmented, regardless of the task.

**Mechanism** `[D]` The brain subconsciously computes whether the struggle of starting is worth the
reward. If the calendar is a Swiss cheese mess of 15-to-30-minute meetings, the brain knows you will be
interrupted before reaching the bliss of flow, so it makes you procrastinate.

**The complete fix** `[D]`

1. Collapse unnecessary meetings down the ladder: in-person → Zoom → phone → text → cancelled. A longer
   version of the same ladder in q13 inserts email between phone and text, giving five rungs: in-person →
   Zoom → phone → email → text → cancelled.
2. Batch all remaining communication and meetings outside your peak productive hours, so you hold long
   uninterrupted blocks that justify the effort of starting.

**Every number** `[D]` Up to 70% of meetings are unnecessary. Choke-point meetings run 15 to 30 minutes.

**Sources:** q30, q13.

## Dopamine resensitization (why he rejects the dopamine detox)

**What it is** `[D]` A rejection and a replacement. The popular dopamine detox is scientifically flawed
because dopamine is an essential neurochemical you need to survive and function. His replacement: do not
detox from dopamine, change how your brain responds to it, through receptor resensitization.

**When to use it** When ordinary work feels unbearably dull next to your phone.

**Mechanism** `[D]` Dopamine acts as a primary focusing mechanism in the brain. Stimuli capture your
attention precisely because of the dopamine release they trigger, which is why he rejects the detox
outright rather than softening it. Bombarding the brain with highly stimulating activities, scrolling
social media, playing video games, eating junk food, overstimulates the system and desensitizes dopamine
receptors, so fewer receptors receive the signal and you require massive amounts of extreme stimulation
just to feel a normal level of engagement. Receptor resensitization reverses this by intentionally
increasing reward sensitivity: lowering the bar for what you find stimulating recalibrates the
dopaminergic system so that objectively boring or difficult tasks, reading a dense book or working six
hours straight, become highly rewarding, engaging and capable of holding attention.

**The delivery vehicle** Boring Breaks, under its own heading below.

**Cross-corpus note** This is the corpus's head-on disagreement with the dopamine detox the
self-development canon popularised. It arrives complete: the rejection, the reason, and the replacement.

**Science under it** `[M:Berridge]` Kent Berridge (University of Michigan) discovered that the brain's
liking and wanting systems are different, and that dopamine is the molecule of craving rather than
pleasure. Berridge appears nowhere in q29 through q37; the attribution comes from q59.

**Sources:** q31, q59.

## Boring Breaks

**What it is** `[D]` A work-and-break cycle in which the break must be strictly less stimulating than the
work, so the brain ends the break craving the hard task.

**When to use it** Inside a long work block, on the stated cycle or after finishing a subtask.

**The stated cycle** `[D]` Work for exactly 45 minutes, then take a strict break to do nothing for 5
minutes.

**Competing values on break length, all carried.** 5 minutes (q31, in the stated cycle) · 1 to 10 minutes
(q09, q60, where the break follows each finished subtask) · a permitted 20-minute nap (q31's own permitted
list). q31 states the 5-minute rule and the 20-minute nap in the same file.

**The qualifying rule** `[D]` A break qualifies only if it is strictly less stimulating than the work you
are taking a break from.

**Permitted, the full list as given** `[D]` Staring at a blank wall for 5 minutes · taking a 20-minute nap
· walking · stretching · doing breathwork · foam rolling.

**Prohibited, entirely** `[WRN][D]` Anything that floods the system with novelty and dopamine: checking
emails, reading the news, looking at your phone.

**Mechanism** `[D]` Spend the break swiping through social media and returning to a difficult spreadsheet
or research paper feels agonizingly dull by comparison. Spend 5 minutes staring at a blank wall and you
actively starve the brain's novelty-seeking circuits. That brief dopamine starvation resets the system so
that when the break ends the brain actually craves getting back to the hard work just to have something to
engage with.

> Title note: the source video is "Give Me 58 Secs… I'll REPROGRAM Your Brain". No 58-second step,
> duration or measurement appears anywhere in the content. The method resolves entirely to the 45/5 cycle
> above. Do not prescribe 58 seconds.

**Contradiction on when to break, carried.** He insists you must not interrupt the initial struggle phase,
warning that giving in to a break resets the 23-minute focus cycle. He also advises a boring break of 1 to
10 minutes after finishing every subtask. Both stand in the corpus.

**Unresolved scheduling collision.** Three protocols compete for the same block of the day with no
integration rule: the 45/5 Boring Breaks cycle, a 10-to-30-minute certainty window held unbroken, and the
3-Hour Rule that wants the first 3 hours before anything else. The corpus never says whether the 3-hour
block contains three Boring Break cycles or where the certainty windows sit inside it.

**Science under it** `[M:Berridge]` dopamine as the molecule of craving rather than pleasure, which is what
resensitization is aimed at. Not named in q31; the attribution comes from q59.

**Sources:** q31 (authoritative), q09, q39, q41, q60, q59.

## The 10-Minute Rule

**What it is** `[D]` A retroactive time-logging practice that takes exactly 10 minutes at the end of an
8-hour workday. Not a rule about starting a task for ten minutes. A lens will default to the wrong reading
unless it holds the disambiguation.

**When to use it** Daily, at the end of the workday, as the practice that makes you a Temporal Architect.

**The complete procedure** `[D]`

1. Open your pre-planned calendar.
2. Delete any events or tasks you did not end up doing.
3. Drag the time blocks up or down to reflect the exact amount of time each activity actually took,
   leaving zero gaps.
4. Assign each block a colour-coded category, so you can visualise execution time, flow time and junk time.

**Every number** `[D]` Exactly 10 minutes, at the end of an 8-hour workday. The wider practice logs in
15-minute increments, 24/7, including sleep.

**Mechanism** `[D]` Time tracking forces you to explicitly set clear goals, which gives attention a precise
target and drastically shortens the neurochemically uncomfortable struggle phase. As you execute and log
those targets the brain receives a steady drip of dopamine, pulling you into flow where effortless exertion
takes over.

**What it fights** `[WRN][D]` Time slippage: experiencing effortless exertion during unproductive
activities, which drains the neurochemical resources you need for your actual goals.

**Science under it** `[M:Kotler]` clear goals as a fundamental flow trigger, stated flat in q32 with no
attribution.

**The unreconciled divergence.** q31 and q32 both answer "how do you make hard work feel like TikTok" and
give completely different protocols. q31 says Boring Breaks, the 45/5 dopamine resensitization cycle. q32
says the 10-Minute Rule, retroactive time logging. Neither file mentions the other, and the corpus never
says whether they are two routes to one outcome or two videos that never reconciled.

**Sources:** q32, q12, q60.

## The five intrinsic motivators

**What it is** `[D]` A scored framework for permanently upgrading your motivational fuel source from
extrinsic motivation, which he calls fossil fuels (money, status, praise), to intrinsic motivation, which
he calls fusion energy, completely self-sustaining.

**When to use it** As a standing yearly measurement rather than an in-the-moment fix.

**The five, complete with his definitions** `[D]`

1. **Curiosity, what you explore.** Ensure your work has immediate feedback and leverage so you see direct
   tangible results from the new things you learn.
2. **Mastery, what you improve.** Use deliberate practice on the edge of your comfort zone to endlessly
   improve the specific skills your craft requires.
3. **Autotelicity, what you love.** Build your career around tasks you are naturally good at, because
   strengths trigger flow, which eventually trains the brain to love the activity purely for its own sake.
4. **Purpose, what you impact.** Distil your life's mission into one laser-focused sentence and explicitly
   link daily work tasks to it.
5. **Autonomy, what you choose.** Cultivate "FU skills" and an "FU network" to create a backdrop of
   absolute choice and optionality in your career.

**The scoring rule** `[D]` Measure each of the five on a scale of 1 to 10. Goal: boost each motivator by 5
points per year until all are maxed at a total score of 50.

**Every number** `[D]` 5 motivators · scale 1 to 10 each · +5 points per motivator per year · total target
50.

**Mechanism** `[D]` Extrinsic motivation wanes quickly because dopamine surges during the anticipation of a
reward and plummets once the reward is attained. Maxing out the intrinsic motivators causes continuous
release of a performance-enhancing cocktail of norepinephrine, dopamine and acetylcholine, lowering
cognitive load, enhancing focus and driving flow. Once in flow you receive an additional cascade of
endorphins, serotonin, anandamide and oxytocin.

**The compounding loop** `[D]` Because flow is the pinnacle of intrinsic motivation, high intrinsic
motivation triggers flow, and flow's neurochemical rewards further amplify intrinsic motivation. The loop
produces a monk-like detachment from external rewards while you become fully obsessed with the work itself.

**Attribution gap** `[A]` No attribution is offered for the framework. Three of the five, autonomy, mastery
and purpose, are Daniel Pink's *Drive* triad, and the corpus names neither Pink nor any other source for
them. q59 lists `[M:Pink]` only for the chronotype taxonomy.

**Sources:** q32, q37, q59.

## Craving hard work (effortless exertion)

**What it is** `[D]` His account of why scrolling feels frictionless and hard work does not, and what
makes hard work feel the same way.

**When to use it** As the framing behind Boring Breaks, the five intrinsic motivators, and the "wrong kind
of hard" diagnosis.

**Mechanism** `[D]` Scrolling social media and playing video games feel frictionless because they trigger
effortless exertion, a core characteristic of the flow state. Effortless exertion is driven by transient
hypofrontality, the temporary deactivation of the prefrontal cortex. When that region powers down, the
sense of self diminishes and time dilates, allowing hours to pass like minutes.

**The cost when it happens on the wrong activity** `[WRN][D]` Experiencing effortless exertion during
unproductive activities creates massive time slippage, draining the neurochemical resources you need for
your actual goals.

**The prescription** `[D]` Hard work should feel easy when it triggers effortless exertion. The "right kind
of hard" means aligning tasks with your natural strengths and fuelling them with intrinsic motivation,
curiosity, mastery, autotelicity, purpose and autonomy, rather than extrinsic rewards like money or status.
Intrinsically motivated work gives a steady drip of dopamine and endorphins, making complex problem-solving
feel as effortless and addictive as playing a video game or scrolling TikTok.

**Science under it** `[M:unnamed]` transient hypofrontality is stated flat with no attribution in q32 and
q37. Pass 1 identifies it as Arne Dietrich's construct from outside knowledge; q59 credits Dietrich only for
the neurochemical shifts of flow, so the corpus does not itself connect him to this term.

**Sources:** q32, q37, q59.

## Attention Deficit Trait against ADHD

**What it is** `[M:Hallowell]` The term Attention Deficit Trait was coined in the early 2000s by
psychiatrist Ed Hallowell. It is one of only two named outside authorities in q29 through q37, and it is the
science underwriting Minimum Viable Certainty.

**When to use the distinction** Before prescribing a productivity protocol to someone who reports attention
problems.

**The distinction** `[M:Hallowell]`

| | ADHD | Attention Deficit Trait (ADT) |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | Genetic condition, typically caused by neurotransmitter imbalances | Artificial, non-genetic, develops later in life |
| Onset | From an early age | Often in successful adults dealing with demanding careers |
| Cause | Inherent difficulty with attention and impulse regulation | Purely environmental: chronic stress, constant distractions, non-stop pressure |

**What crisis mode does to cognition** `[M:Hallowell]` Chronic stress and constant distraction throw the body
out of balance and keep the brain stuck in a prolonged crisis mode. Attention span violently shrinks, working
memory falters, and it becomes increasingly difficult to plan ahead or see the big picture.

**Why it responds to a protocol at all** `[D]` ADT thrives on ambiguity. Creating temporary islands of
certainty in a chaotic day activates the task positive network, filters out distractions, and forces ADT
symptoms to retreat.

**Science under it** `[M:Hallowell]` the construct itself. `[M:Sapolsky]` Robert Sapolsky (Stanford): when
chronic stress throws the body out of balance the brain stays stuck in crisis mode, shrinking attention span.
q59 supplies Sapolsky; q33 does not name him.

**Sources:** q33, q06, q59.

## Minimum Viable Certainty (the ADT reversal)

**What it is** `[D]` His protocol for reversing Attention Deficit Trait by creating temporary islands of
certainty inside a chaotic day. This is the q33 three-step form; q06 gives the same protocol under the
Certainty Windows framing.

**When to use it** On a day where chronic stress and constant interruption have shrunk your attention span.

**The complete step list** `[D]`

1. **Create Your Certainty Window,** a 2-minute setup run like a Navy SEAL micro mission.
   - *Assess (120 seconds):* scan your plate and write down all tasks vying for your attention.
   - *Decide:* ask "What is the one task that if complete would most unburden me?"
   - *Commit:* pick that single task and determine how long you can maintain absolute certainty about its
     importance, starting small, even just 10 to 30 minutes. Then explicitly define the window: "For the next
     30 minutes, I am writing the introduction, and nothing else matters".
2. **Protect Your Certainty Window,** building a technological and physical force field.
   - *Go Dark:* turn the phone completely off and physically remove it from the room.
   - *Tech and social barriers:* app blockers such as Freedom, noise-cancelling headphones, close all
     unnecessary browser tabs, and explicitly tell colleagues not to interrupt you for the duration.
3. **Sustain Your Certainty Window,** using the Flow State Breath Reset. If attention slips, do not switch
   tasks. Inhale through the nose for 3 seconds, hold for 2 seconds, exhale slowly through the mouth for 10
   seconds, and repeat three times, to calm the sympathetic nervous system and clear the neurochemical chaos.
   If attention drops again, run Positional Rotation: stand up, move to a different chair, or walk around,
   which resets the brain's perceived exertion and refreshes attention span.

**Every number** `[D]` 2-minute setup · exactly 120 seconds for the assessment · window 10 to 30 minutes ·
breath 3 seconds in, 2 seconds hold, 10 seconds out, 3 repetitions.

**Warning** `[WRN][D]` The hardest part is holding the window once the uncomfortable struggle phase begins.
You will feel an urge to check email or abandon the task, and breaking the window prematurely casts you back
into ADT-driven uncertainty.

**Scale ambiguity to carry** The term "Certainty Window" carries two incompatible scales in the corpus: 10 to
30 minutes here and in q06, and 6 to 18 months in q05 and q60. Both use the identical term and both describe
putting blinders on. State which scale you mean before prescribing.

**Science under it** `[M:Hallowell]` Attention Deficit Trait, the condition this reverses. `[M:unnamed]` q33
states that simply having your phone visible, even powered off, measurably drains working memory and cognitive
performance, with no researcher, study or sample named. That claim is the justification for the Go Dark step.
`[M:Huberman]` and `[M:Diviney]` the micro-mission framing, from q06 and q59.

**Sources:** q33, q06, q59.
