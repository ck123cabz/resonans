## The Leverage Trifecta

*Type: [PRO] · Provenance: [D] · Source: q17, q50, q56*

Three vectors that multiply output without increasing input: **Proficiency, Process and People**.
The point of all three is to break the linear relationship between hours worked and results
achieved.

Definition of the underlying quantity: productivity is the ratio of input cost to output value
(q17). Leverage untethers the number of hours you work from the results you get, so the model
shifts from time-bound productivity to leverage.

**[MEC][D]** Why leverage feels good to the brain: it raises "reward magnitude." When the brain
calculates a much larger payoff for a given exertion of effort, the dopaminergic system lights up,
which drives focus and makes flow accessible. `[M:Yeung-Sanfey]` per q59: Nick Yeung and Alan
Sanfey (Princeton) proved the brain tracks reward size relative to effort.

Brutal Truth 13 states the same three sources in one line: People (hiring someone to do it),
Process (building a system so a 15-hour task takes 15 minutes), Proficiency (acquiring the exact
knowledge and skill to execute flawlessly). Full truths list: `biography-and-business.md`.

**[NUM][M:McKinsey]** The trifecta is sold against the 500% productivity figure attributed to
McKinsey and the 490–500% learning-speed figure attributed to DARPA; `[M:Sydney]` supplies 430%
for creativity. q17 states the percentages with no source at all; q59 supplies the names but no
design, sample or link for any of the three.

## Proficiency Leverage

*Type: [PRO] · Provenance: [D] · Source: q17, q50, q51, q53*

Learn the right skill, never all the skills. Acquire the exact knowledge and skill that makes your
work easier or makes it unnecessary.

Selection tactic: identify the single skill gap acting as your biggest constraint. Worked examples
as given: great product but no clients means master sales; a creator with no reach means master
distribution.

## Five for Four

*Type: [PRO][NUM] · Provenance: [D] · Source: q50, q51, q53 (q50 authoritative)*

**5 hours of skill building per week, for 4 weeks straight, reaching the 20-hour mark.** 20 hours
is the stated threshold for real working proficiency in a new high-leverage skill.

Three permitted splits, all reaching the same 20 hours:

1. 1 hour per day across a 5-day workweek.
2. A single 5-hour block every Saturday.
3. 5 hours a day for 4 days straight, to sprint through it.

## Process Leverage

*Type: [PRO][NUM] · Provenance: [D] · Source: q17, q50*

Turn one-off repetitive manual tasks into repeatable systems.

**The trigger is repetition: a task you have done at least twice, you will do again, so it needs a
system.** Build the SOP by defining the goal and detailing every instruction clearly enough that
anyone, regardless of experience, could follow it.

- **Build rate** — at least **1 new process every month**.
- **Claimed saving** — automating administrative or repetitive work saves **500 hours a year**.
- **Framing** — step back from the urgency and build a sprinkler system instead of putting out the
  fire by hand.

## The Hierarchy of Leverage

*Type: [PRO] · Provenance: [D] · Source: q50*

Strict order: **Eliminate → Automate → Delegate.**

1. **Eliminate.** Before building a system or hiring anyone, ask whether the task needs to happen
   at all. Deletion is the highest form of process leverage.
2. **Automate.** If it must be done and a computer can do it (data entry, scheduling, lead
   capture), automate it completely.
3. **Delegate via process.** If a human must do it, build the SOP first. If you cannot hand a task
   off, the reason is that you have not built the process with enough detail or clarity. People
   leverage runs on process leverage.

## People Leverage

*Type: [PRO] · Provenance: [D] · Source: q17, q50*

Remove yourself as the bottleneck. Doing everything yourself caps output at your own personal
bandwidth. Find the right "who" rather than learning the "how."

**Reader gate.** See the note at the top of this file. The corpus prescribes this vector from a
position of 60+ staff and a $500,000 monthly payroll (q52, q58), and never addresses an operator
with no one to hire.

## Finding your "Gotlieb"

*Type: [PRO][NUM] · Provenance: [D] · Source: q50*

A reference to Robert Caro's editor **Robert Gottlieb**, who stepped in and made a seemingly
impossible project succeed. q50 spells it "Gotlieb" and "Gottlieb" inside the same sentence; both
spellings are carried rather than normalised.

The move, in order: identify your biggest current goal, pinpoint the exact constraint capping your
output, and ask **who** could remove that obstacle completely. Then search proactively for that
person, employee, partner or mentor, rather than waiting to be found.

**[NUM][D]** The claimed payoff: finding the one right person can collapse **5 years of struggle
into 6 months** of acceleration.

## The Leverage Sprint

*Type: [PRO][NUM] · Provenance: [D] · Source: q17, q50, q51 (identical figures in all three)*

A defined, intense window that forces the transition out of the time-for-money trap, because
building leverage sporadically loses every time to the urgency of daily execution.

- **Duration:** 1 to 4 weeks.
- **Hours:** artificially increase total working hours by **30% to 50%**.
- **Allocation:** dedicate **at least 70%** of those hours exclusively to building people, process
  and proficiency leverage, ignoring all other non-essential work.

The heavy upfront effort pushes the car into motion, and future output compounds for a fraction of
the effort.

## The Output Dip

*Type: [WRN] · Provenance: [D] · Source: q51 (fullest), q50, q56*

The temporary drop in immediate results and momentum when you stop grinding out linear work and
start building leverage: automating a system, training a new hire, learning a new skill. Because
building takes time upfront, output stalls.

**What you have to tolerate is two things, not one:** the measurable decrease in output, and the
psychological panic of feeling like nothing is getting finished.

**[MEC][D]** The brain craves immediate dopamine from crossing tasks off a list, so you must
actively resist reverting to familiar, low-leverage work just to feel productive again.
`[M:Schultz]` per q59: Wolfram Schultz's monkey and juice experiments showed dopamine neurons fire
wildly for unpredictable rewards and stay quiet for predictable ones. Brutal Truth 11 states the
whole thing as a rule: the key to wild productivity is living in the output dip.

## High Skill Integration as a hiring filter

*Type: [PRO] · Provenance: [M:vandenHout] taxonomy, [D] engineering · Source: q38, q41, q52*

`[M:vandenHout]` per q59: Jef J.J. van den Hout (Eindhoven University) researched Team Flow Theory
and identified **7 triggers**, of which High Skill Integration and Collective Ambition are the two
the corpus names. The other five are absent from the whole corpus, flagged `[A]`.

Doris's use of it: to unlock team flow, aggressively recruit people with comparable, complementary
and uniquely high skill. It is named the single most vital team-flow trigger (q41).

**The hiring rule that falls out of it:** every new person must raise the average talent level of
the team. Mismatched skill levels drag the group down and break team flow.

- **Sourcing** — ignore job boards; use deep outbound headhunting to find proven A-players who need
  no training.
- **Firing** — fire B-players at the first sign of doubt, to keep raising talent density.

## Hiring rules: specialists, batteries included, fixed mindset

*Type: [PRO] · Provenance: [D] · Source: q52*

- **Recruit for specialised roles, never "unicorns."** Define roles with a singular, crystal-clear
  responsibility rather than blending skills that rarely go together, such as expecting a highly
  technical engineer to also be a charismatic people person.
- **Hire "batteries included."** The candidate must already have the exact skills to do the job
  successfully.
- **Adopt a fixed mindset for recruitment.** At the recruiting stage, assume the candidate's Day 1
  performance is as good as it will ever be, and hire only if that level already meets your
  standard.
- **Use the interview to sell.** Interviews sell the candidate on the company's vision rather than
  assess them.

## The Discovery Project

*Type: [PRO][NUM] · Provenance: [D] · Source: q52*

Pay the candidate a small stipend to do work that simulates the exact role, for **a day, two days,
or a week**.

Worked examples as given:

- **Salesperson** — running **3 mock sales calls** and drafting a follow-up email.
- **Marketing leader** — analysing your current marketing efforts and proposing a **30-day plan to
  halve customer acquisition costs**.

**[NUM][D]** For high-level six-figure roles, run the sequence: a **1-day Discovery Project**, then
a **1-month Discovery period**, and only then the full-time offer.

## Referral bonuses

*Type: [PRO][NUM][BIZ] · Provenance: [D], [G] as a business fact in q58 · Source: q52, q58*

The most talent-dense hiring channel is direct referrals, because A-players know and refer other
A-players.

- **Payout:** up to **$30,000 in cash** for a successful referral for a senior role; a
  proportionately lower cash bonus for smaller roles.
- **Conditions:** it must be a direct referral, and the candidate must stay successfully in the
  role for a minimum of **3 months** before the bonus is paid.

## Team-flow blockers a leader creates

*Type: [WRN] · Provenance: [D] · Source: q41*

Four named blockers, all of them leadership failures rather than team failures. The team-flow
preconditions themselves live in `flow-mechanics.md`.

1. **Mismatched skill levels, tolerating B-players.** Ruins skill integration, breaks the
   challenge-skills balance, drags the group dynamic down, breeds mediocrity, and drives A-players
   to quit.
2. **Failing to fire fast.** Leaders who ignore their intuition and keep people they have doubts
   about block the team's ability to synchronize.
3. **The Musician Trap.** See below.
4. **Poor role definition.** Packing too many unrelated responsibilities into one role makes it
   impossible to hire true specialists, and without specialists the team never reaches high skill
   integration.

## Musician to conductor

*Type: [PRO] · Provenance: [D] · Source: q08, q52 (fullest), q53, q56*

The graduation from individual contributor to orchestrator. q08 gives it as six moves:

1. **Prioritise team flow over individual flow.** Shift from seeking dopamine through your own task
   completion to seeking oxytocin through the team's success, flow states and completions.
2. **Obsess over leverage.** Treat your productivity as the sum of the collective's output and
   prioritise long-term systems building over short-term individual execution.
3. **Conduct, don't confiscate.** Stop stealing trumpets. Coach and clarify from the stage, because
   stepping in to do the work disempowers the team and destroys the autonomy they need for flow.
4. **Stay in your autotelic sweet spot.** Delegate everything outside your unique strengths and
   passions, and help your musicians do the same.
5. **Ask "Who," not "How."** When a new problem arises, find the musician who already knows how to
   play it instead of spending months learning a new instrument.
6. **If in doubt, delegate.** Relinquish paralysing control and empower the team from a distance
   rather than supervising every note.

**[MEC][D]** The transition is a neurochemical swap: dopamine from personal task completion traded
for oxytocin from the team's success. Explicit trust fosters respect, respect triggers oxytocin,
and oxytocin is what team flow runs on (q38). `[M:Zak]` per q59: Paul Zak researched oxytocin and
its implications for deep trust and business success.

## The Musician Trap

*Type: [WRN] · Provenance: [D] · Source: q08, q41, q53*

An entrepreneur or leader stuck playing their own instrument, doing the individual contributor
work, instead of graduating to the conductor who orchestrates and leads.

Four self-detection signs (q08):

1. Prioritising your own access to flow and the short-term dopamine hits of checking items off your
   personal to-do list over making sure the team has what it needs.
2. Everything in the business funnelling through you.
3. Taking tasks off your team's plates to do them yourself because you think it will be faster.
4. The business's overall output being strictly limited by your personal bandwidth.

## What you're good at is holding you back

*Type: [WRN] · Provenance: [D] · Source: q53*

The trap stated at its sharpest. You succeeded as an individual contributor because you are highly
skilled at your specific instrument: writing code, closing sales, crafting designs. As the team or
business grows, relying on the skills you are best at becomes a fatal bottleneck.

Because you are so good at the work, you resist relinquishing control and step in to do tasks
yourself, stealing trumpets from team members. Stay there and your output stays capped at your own
bandwidth while the team never reaches autonomy or team flow.

## Putting others' productivity above your own

*Type: [PRO] · Provenance: [D] · Source: q52*

**[MEC][D]** A musician is an individual contributor who gets dopamine from crossing personal tasks
off a list and protecting his own flow state. A conductor takes satisfaction from the success and
flow states of the team.

The instruction: stop measuring productivity by your daily individual output and think of your
productivity as the sum of the collective's output. Step back, relinquish control over the
specifics of *how* the work gets done, and orchestrate an environment that lets the team reach team
flow. You actively trade your individual flow for team flow.

**[WRN][D]** Stop "stealing trumpets," taking work off the team's plate to do it yourself because
you think you can do it faster. It stifles their growth, removes their autonomy, and creates
bottlenecks.

## Investing in yourself without spending money

*Type: [PRO] · Provenance: [D] · Source: q53, with q21 and q56*

The answer the corpus gives to "how do I invest in myself with no money" is minimalism, on the
grounds that it lowers cognitive load for free.

**[MEC][D]** Every physical item carries a cognitive cost of ownership: possessions capture
attention and drain working memory, because each one has to be managed, maintained, stored, cleaned
and worried about. `[M:Hook]` per q59: Joshua Hook (University of North Texas) ran a systematic
review finding minimalism strongly increases intrinsic motivation. `[M:Csikszentmihalyi]` noted the
cognitive cost of possessions draining psychic energy.

Filter questions, verbatim from q53: *"Is this a tool that advances my craft and professional
pursuit directly?"* and *"Is this worth the cost of ownership?"*

The tiered forms in q21, verbatim: aggressive minimalists use the craft question above; tempered
and mild minimalists use *"What am I assuming I'll get from this, and is it worth the cost of
ownership?"* or *"Do I love this more than the potential cost of my attention and professional
progress?"* Three tiers are named and only two questions are supplied, so tiers 2 and 3 are never
distinguished from each other `[A]`.

Full Possession Purge protocol, the one-day method and the two piles: `attention-and-load.md`.

## Skill selection

*Type: [PRN][PRO] · Provenance: [D] · Source: q53, q50*

Base the choice entirely on your biggest constraint. Look at the results you want but do not yet
have, and pinpoint the exact skill gap blocking them.

The guiding question, verbatim: *"What skill if mastered would make all of this work easier or
unnecessary and as a result boost what I can do in any given hour forever in the future?"*

Then run Five for Four against it.

## The Flow No

*Type: [PRO] · Provenance: [D] · Source: q13, q54, q55*

A strict personal policy that categorically rejects requests not aligned with your goals. First
appears in q13 as the fourth move for collapsing calendar choke points; q54 gives the full method.

**The mechanism: say no to categories of activity, never to individuals.** Look at the requests you
regularly have to decline and create a blanket rule for them in advance.

**[MEC][D]** Framing a refusal as a preset external rule depersonalises the rejection. Because the
refusal is dictated by policy rather than by your feelings about the person asking, it stops them
feeling slighted and stops you ruminating on the social consequences.

**[WRN][NUM][D]** The cost of not having one (q55): conflict avoidance produces calendar choke
points, 15 to 30-minute commitments that fracture the day, and raises cognitive load.

## The TSP Formula

*Type: [PRO] · Provenance: [D] · Source: q54*

Three parts, in order, for delivering a category policy to a specific request.

- **T, Thanks.** Start with genuine gratitude and personal acknowledgement, e.g. "Thank you for
  thinking of me."
- **S, Shortcoming.** Show humility by briefly explaining your constraint or deficit, e.g. "I'm
  behind on my book deadline" or "I'm overloaded at the moment."
- **P, Policy.** Assert the boundary by stating your pre-established category rule, e.g. "I've
  paused all speaking commitments until my book is done."

**[PRN][D]** Delivery rules: be highly specific so the other person can empathise with your
situation, and never backtrack or say "maybe later" unless you genuinely mean it. Hesitating or
apologising makes you look suspicious and invites pushback.

## TSP scripts, word for word

*Type: [PRO] · Provenance: [D] · Source: q54*

Declining a professional or speaking invitation:

> *"Thanks for the invite. It sounds amazing, I appreciate you thinking of me. I'm deep in a book
> deadline and a little bit overwhelmed with it, and I'm holding off on all commitments to speaking
> engagements until the book is complete. Appreciate you thinking of me. No doubt the event will go
> great."*

Declining a social invitation from a friend:

> *"Scott, I'd love to, but I'm writing a book, have a tight deadline, and I've banned myself from
> partying until it's complete."*

## The Trade-off No

*Type: [PRO] · Provenance: [D] · Source: q55*

The version for a superior, where a flat no is unavailable. If a boss asks you to do something,
identify the trade-offs, propose the options and their consequences, and let them choose.

Script as given: *"I can help with the presentation, but it will result in 15% fewer sales on our
current launch."*

## The Social Rumination Purge

*Type: [PRO][NUM] · Provenance: [D] · Source: q54 (fullest), q38, q55*

**[MEC][D]** Unresolved, unspoken conversations hijack the default mode network, which then replays
negative social scenarios endlessly and drains the mental resources flow needs.

Three steps:

1. **Identify.** Write down every uncomfortable conversation you have been avoiding, with a
   colleague or a family member, that keeps surfacing in your mind.
2. **Script it out.** Write exactly what you want to say word for word, **three to five sentences
   maximum**. Start with your main point, the uncomfortable truth. Add a simple acknowledgement of
   how it might affect the other person. End with your clear boundary.
3. **Deliver it.** After basic pleasantries, drop into an "almost unconscious mode" and read it off
   the page like ripping off a Band-Aid, to get the words directly into their ears. q55 gives the
   same instruction as getting into an ice bath: bluntly and immediately.

**[PRN][D]** Never use the feedback sandwich. Deliver blunt, honest truth.

In q38 the same protocol appears as the engineering method under the Open Communication social
trigger, with the same 3-to-5-sentence script length.

## Talking to people: the science under the purge

*Type: [CIT] · Provenance: [M:name] throughout · Source: q55, cross-checked against q59*

The most productive way to communicate, as the corpus frames it, is mastering conflict resolution
and learning to say no in order to protect focus. Four authorities are named in q55 itself, which
makes it one of the few files in the corpus that cites anyone.

- `[M:Miller]` **George Miller** (Harvard): we can hold about **seven items** in short-term memory.
  q59's Sweller entry says 4 to 7; both readings sit in the corpus and neither is reconciled.
- `[M:Sweller]` **John Sweller** expanded Miller into cognitive load theory. Avoiding a
  confrontation creates open mental loops that overload working memory.
- `[M:Hamilton]` **Dr. J. Paul Hamilton** (Laureate Institute for Brain Research): a meta-analysis
  showing high trait rumination is marked by strong connectivity between the default mode network
  and the subgenual prefrontal cortex, part of the reward system, which wires the brain to reward
  endless social worrying.
- `[M:Navratilova]` **Dr. Navratilova, 2012**: ending a stressor triggers a dopamine release in the
  nucleus accumbens while cortisol drops. q55 calls her "Ivita," q59 calls her "Edita"; both
  spellings carried.

**[MEC][D]** The relief reward: by being assertive and having the uncomfortable conversation you
rewire yourself toward assertiveness through that biological reward.

## Productive meditation

*Type: [PRO][NUM] · Provenance: [D] · Source: q55, q24*

Meditation is not framed as emptying the mind. It is high-intensity training for attention
recovery, and the reps are the product.

- **Session:** sit and meditate for **10 minutes**, using the breath as the anchor.
- **Expected wander count:** **30 to 50 times** per 10-minute session. Every time you notice the
  drift and return to the breath, you complete one **rep** of attention recovery.
- **[MEC][D]** Each rep strengthens the connection between the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and
  the anterior cingulate cortex.
- **[MEC][D]** Untrained, attention behaves like a pinball controlled by the salience network.
  Trained, it behaves like a boomerang: pulled away, it curves straight back.

**[NUM][M:Mark]** The figure this trains against: Gloria Mark (UC Irvine, 2004) found the average
office worker takes **23 minutes and 15 seconds** to refocus after an interruption. The target that
replaces it is detection within **3 seconds** and full recovery within **30 seconds** (q24, q30).

**[NUM][M:Barros]** A 2018 study by Marcelo Barros (Federal University of São Carlos) showed flow
states are connected to a massive mobilisation of attentional resources and blood flow to focus
regions. q55 spells the name "Baros."

## Work as meditation

*Type: [PRO] · Provenance: [D] · Source: q55, q24*

The transfer of the meditation reps to the desk.

1. **Set the anchor.** Before starting any task, physically write down a highly specific clear
   goal. The goal does at the desk exactly what the breath does in meditation: it gives attention a
   precise landing strip.
2. **Practise two speeds.** Detection speed, how fast you catch the mind wandering. Recovery speed,
   how fast you redirect back to the clear goal.
3. **Run the Flow State Breath Reset when you slip.** Inhale through the nose for 3 seconds, hold
   for 2, exhale slowly through the mouth for 10. The 3-2-10 breath stimulates the vagus nerve,
   activates the parasympathetic nervous system and reduces amygdala activity, which creates a
   clean break from the distraction.
4. **Rewrite the goal.** Immediately after the breath, physically write a fresh specific clear
   goal, e.g. "Write three sentences explaining the proposal timeline." The physical act of writing
   re-engages the prefrontal cortex.

Full breath protocol and its arousal account: `physiology-and-recovery.md`.

## Engineering the five social flow triggers

*Type: [PRO] · Provenance: [M:Kotler] taxonomy, [D] engineering · Source: q38*

The trigger list belongs to Kotler and Csikszentmihalyi per profile rule 2; the engineering under
each one is Doris's. The full 14-trigger inventory sits in `flow-mechanics.md`; the five social
triggers and their hiring and communication methods sit here.

1. **High Skill Integration** → outbound headhunting for A-players, fire B-players at the first
   sign of doubt.
2. **Collective Ambition** → turn job descriptions into compelling sales pages that lead with the
   mission, and use interviews to sell candidates on the vision.
3. **Open Communication** → the Social Rumination Purge, plus a saying-no-for-flow policy.
4. **Psychological Safety** → graduate from musician to conductor and explicitly trust the team to
   play their own instruments.
5. **Equal Participation and Shared Control** → "conduct, don't confiscate." Delegate outcomes
   rather than supervising processes, so team members have absolute autonomy over how they execute.
