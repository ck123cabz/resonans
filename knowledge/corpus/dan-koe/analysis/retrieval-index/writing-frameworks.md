# Dan Koe Retrieval Index — Writing Frameworks

Layer 2 retrieval index. One framework per `##` heading, each section self-contained: the part
list, the instructions and the verbatim example lines all sit inside the same section. Context is
repeated rather than cross-referenced, so a single grep returns something usable.

**The verbatim rule.** Every line inside a `>` blockquote is Koe's own wording, reproduced exactly
as the corpus renders it, transcription defects included. His voice is the product being taught,
so nothing here is paraphrased, cleaned or tidied. Where the corpus garbles a figure, both the
garbled and the clean rendering are given.

**Provenance key.** `[D]` his own, with no source underneath · `[D+M:name]` his name over someone
else's architecture · `[M:name]` a straight relay under someone else's name · `[G]` backed by his
own reported outcome · `[A]` a flagged gap.

**Source files.** q18–q24 in `knowledge/corpus/dan-koe/notebooklm/`. q22 holds 41 of this lane's
verbatim lines and wins wherever its rendering differs from q21's.

**Standing caution.** This corpus teaches persuasive patterns the repo's own prose filter bans:
em dashes as the default sentence-breaker, three-item lists for rhythm, absolutes, the exclusion
hook. Quote him as quotation. The full list is in the last section of this file.

## APAG — Attention, Perspective, Advantage, Gamify

**Provenance** `[FRM][D+M:copywriting-tradition]`. The corpus (q18) presents APAG as a proprietary
persuasive writing structure created by Dan Koe. q68, his own coinage audit, gives its
psychological foundations as PAS, AIDA and PASTOR. The acronym and the packaging are his; the
four-beat architecture is the direct-response tradition's. Never collapse to either half.

**Where it applies** `[NUM][D]` Ten surfaces, the full list as given: newsletters, podcast or
YouTube scripts, Twitter threads, medium-form LinkedIn posts, sales pages, landing pages, book or
ebook chapters, course modules, lead magnets, short-form tweets. Any part of the framework can be
condensed into a single line or expanded into an entire section of content.

### Stage 1 · Attention (A)

`[FRM][D]` The art of hooks and headlines. Its goal is to capture the reader's focus so they do
not scroll past your hard work. Constructed by balancing three elements: **relevance** (what is in
it for the reader, via resolved pains or potential benefits), **awareness** (simple or complex
enough for your target audience), **effort** (how fast and easily they receive the result). Built
from "building blocks": a big problem, a big benefit, a big idea, a transformation process, a
timeframe. q18 lists five blocks; q22 gives the complete six by adding Negative Personal
Experience, and q22 wins.

Verbatim example line (q18):

> "you won't make it without Obsession but nobody starts out obsessed they start out curious they experiment they build things they break things they fail and eventually they can't pull themselves away curiosity turns into obsession with time and persistence"

q22 repeats the same line with an initial capital: *"You won't make it without Obsession but nobody starts out obsessed…"*

### Stage 2 · Perspective (P)

`[FRM][D]` His own words for what this stage does:

> "painting a picture of the enemy or why a certain perspective is wrong"

You relate to and deeply amplify the problems the reader is currently experiencing. You paint a
common, mediocre or faulty perspective to establish common ground before presenting a better way
of living or thinking.

Verbatim example line (q18, and the only APAG line that appears nowhere else in the corpus):

> "wake up hit snooze four times stare at your phone roll out of bed make coffee sit in traffic 8 hours of unfulfilling work... repeat this should scare the shit out of you"

### Stage 3 · Advantage (A)

`[FRM][D]` Painting a picture of the hero, the new vision, or why your new perspective is right.
You present a credible argument to sell your ideas and propose a better way of doing things,
backed by novel ideas, concepts, social proof, quotes and personal experiences.

Verbatim example line, short rendering (q18):

> "writing is a great skill to learn because it pairs with any other skill or interest if you can write you can do whatever you want"

Verbatim example line, full rendering (q22, which continues past where q18 stops):

> "Writing is a great skill to learn because it pairs with any other skill or interest if you can write you can do whatever you want and distribute the value you discover from those Pursuits writing allows you to make a living from living the problem is that most people aren't"

### Stage 4 · Gamify (G)

`[FRM][D]` His own words for what this stage does:

> "to create a hierarchy of goals or a game for people to follow"

You give step-by-step advice on achieving the transformation from the old, problem-ridden
perspective to the new, advantageous one, wrapping the piece into a clear, concise, actionable
path: a list of steps, key points to follow, or resources such as books to read.

Verbatim example line (q18):

> "you need four habits one that builds your mind one that builds your body one that builds your business one that builds your relationships the good life is the process of becoming everything you could be"

q22 repeats it capitalised: *"You need four habits one that builds your mind…"*

## PPP — Pull, Perspective, Punchline

**Provenance** `[FRM][D]`. **One of only two pure `[D]` items in the entire corpus**, alongside the
2-Hour Content Ecosystem. q68 states outright that no source sits underneath it. Everything else
in his named vocabulary either credits a source, disclaims itself, or has one found for it in
Pass 2.

**What it is for** `[TAC][D]` His personal structure for writing short-form, punchy content like
tweets.

**The three stages** `[TAC][D]`

- **Pull.** Pull the reader in with a hook that catches attention. He recommends studying
  headlines, and using statistics, shocking facts, the word "you", or a bit of exaggeration.
- **Perspective.** Offer a unique perspective, or a completely different way of framing the topic.
- **Punchline.** Deliver a punchline that ties the entire thought together.

**Example 1, verbatim** `[TPL][D]` a single-sentence PPP structure:

> "people will spend 10 hours worrying about a task that takes 10 minutes to complete"

His breakdown of it: the **Pull** starts with the word "people" because readers relate to it,
combined with the specific numbers "10" and "10" to grab attention. The **Perspective** is his
unique framing around "worrying about stupid things". Because it is a single-sentence post, the
ending realisation of wasting 10 hours on a 10-minute task serves as the **Punchline** itself.

**Example 2, verbatim** `[TPL][D]` a longer, list-style PPP structure:

> "most people will spend eight hours on someone else's dreams four hours hiding from their own two hours going through the motions 16 hours awake 24 hours of sleep it doesn't have to take 40 years in a retirement plan to realize that a participation trophy isn't fulfilling"

His breakdown of it: the **Pull** is the opening phrase *"most people will spend"*, which catches
attention by calling out the masses. The **Perspective** is his unique view of exactly what people
do with their time, heavily using numbers (8, 4, 2, 16, 24) to hold the reader through the body.

The **Punchline** of example 2, isolated verbatim:

> "it doesn't have to take 40 years in a retirement plan to realize that a participation trophy isn't fulfilling"

**Defect carried, not corrected** `[NUM][A]` Example 2's number series does not close: 8 + 4 + 2 is
14 against a stated "16 hours awake", and "24 hours of sleep" is impossible. This is either his own
rhetorical looseness or a transcription artifact. It is extracted as written, per the verbatim
rule, and a lens quoting the line should quote it whole rather than repair the arithmetic.

## PAS — Problem, Amplify, Solution

**Provenance** `[FRM][M:copywriting-tradition]`. PAS is the direct-response tradition's own
framework, Problem-Agitate-Solution. Koe substitutes "Amplify" for the tradition's "Agitate" and
presents the whole thing without attribution. One swapped word is not enough packaging to earn a
compound marker, so this is a relay.

**When to use it** `[TAC][D]` His stated "go-to" framework when he feels stuck and does not know
what to write. Versatile across short social posts, YouTube scripts, emails and promotions, where
you capture attention, agitate a problem, and position your offer as the fix.

**Verbatim example** `[TPL][D]`:

> "what's a problem you don't know how to articulate yourself Okay how do you amplify that what does that lead to down the road you won't get the girl You won't land the job You won't be able to start your own business You're going to live in your mom's basement for the rest of your life Okay what's the solution use the PAS framework"

**The problem-opener he names** `[TPL][D]` a great way to introduce the problem is simply to start
with the phrase:

> "if you struggle with"

## PAO — expanded in the corpus as "Problem, Amplify, Solution, Offer" (acronym defect)

**Provenance** `[FRM][D+M:copywriting-tradition]`.

**The defect, carried and flagged.** The corpus (q20) expands PAO as **Problem, Amplify, Solution,
Offer**. Those four words spell **PASO**, not PAO. Either the label or the expansion is wrong in
the source, and Pass 2 lists it as a re-query candidate. It is recorded here as written rather than
silently fixed, because a lens that teaches a framework under a repaired name has invented the
repair. **A lens asked for PAO should state the mismatch and quote both the label and the
expansion.**

**When to use it** `[TAC][D]` When you are actively selling or promoting. It functions exactly like
PAS, but ends by linking a free download, product, service, website or blog as the vehicle for the
solution.

**No single-sentence example exists** `[WRN][A]` He gives no one-line PAO hook anywhere in the
corpus. What he does give verbatim is the amplify passage:

> "illustrate how that pain point is impacting someone's life like how being tired can make your husband or wife think something is wrong and little problems start springing up out of nowhere or how you aren't able to perform when trying to build your side hustle will it ever work or how when you get home from work all you want to do is lie down and eat junk food"

## PASTOR — Problem, Amplify, Story, Testimony, Offer, Response

**Provenance** `[FRM][M:copywriting-tradition]`. PASTOR is Ray Edwards' framework, near-identically
ordered, and the corpus presents it with no attribution. q68 names PASTOR as one of APAG's
psychological foundations, which confirms he knows the source exists without crediting it here.

**When to use it** `[TAC][D]` Because of its length, specifically for sales copywriting: a
promotional newsletter or a product landing page.

**The structural progression, as he gives it** `[TAC][D]`

1. State and amplify the problem.
2. Tell a personal or client story.
3. Provide "testimony": proof, facts, or testimonials showing the goal is possible.
4. Break down the "offer", listing what is inside each module and the benefits of those features.
5. End with a "response", the call to action.

**No verbatim example exists** `[WRN][A]` PASTOR is given only as a macro-structure. A lens asked to
write a sales page from PASTOR has the skeleton and no voice sample. Pass 1 flags this as a cheap
Layer 3 gap to close with a targeted re-query.

## bPAS — Big Idea, Problem, Amplify, Solution

**Provenance** `[FRM][D+M:copywriting-tradition]`. Unlike bare PAS, this one earns the compound
marker: the tradition owns PAS, and the Big Idea prefix plus the anomaly-post trigger is his
packaging on top.

**When to use it** `[TAC][D]` Specifically for writing a newsletter that expands on a social media
post which performed exceptionally well, what he calls an "anomaly post".

**The formatting spec, verbatim** `[TPL][D]` (the closest thing to an example the corpus supplies):

> "one sentence big idea a few sentences problem a few sentences amplify the problem and how it impacts someone's life that's the intro and then you have a headline that states what the solution is"

followed by the steps to solve it.

**Newsletter application** `[TAC][D]` q16 adds the detail that the Solution is broken apart by
sub-headlines, and q27 builds the whole newsletter around this sequence.

**No verbatim example exists** `[WRN][A]` Like PAO and PASTOR, bPAS is given only as a macro-
structure with no worked one-line sample.

## The 10 Commandments of Engagement — complete, with definitions and every verbatim example

**Provenance** `[FRM][D]` for the numbered set and its packaging. Several individual entries are
the tradition's or psychology's own terms used unchanged, marked per item.

**The complete named set, in corpus order** (q21): 1 Specific Numbers · 2 Pattern Interrupts ·
3 Negativity Bias · 4 Target Call Out (or Group Call Out) · 5 Problem Call Out · 6 Potential
Benefit · 7 Social Proof · 8 Confidence and Conviction · 9 Punch, Rhythm, and Readability ·
10 Warnings or Cautionary Advice.

### 1 · Specific Numbers `[TAC][D]`

**Definition:** using precise numbers, statistics or dollar amounts in headlines, hooks or tweets.
**Purpose:** they act as a pattern interrupt that makes people stop scrolling to see what the
numbers relate to, so you capture attention and stand out from the noise.

> "Apple just released a $1,175 iPhone"

The second example, as q21 garbles it:

> "how I turn one piece of content per week into a $ 45,2 se5 a month creative income and 340,000 followers in 2 hours a day in five simple steps"

The same line as q22 renders it cleanly, which is the one to use:

> "How I turn one piece of content per week into a $45,275 a month creative income and 340,000 followers in 2 hours a day in five simple steps"

### 2 · Pattern Interrupts `[TAC][M:copywriting-tradition]`

"Pattern interrupt" is the persuasion tradition's own term, used unchanged and uncredited here.
**Definition:** a structural or visual element that breaks people out of their normal, conditioned
patterns of scrolling. **Purpose:** force the reader to pause and stop the scroll, achieved through
visual breaks on the screen, specific numbers, or uniquely formatted text.

He gives no singular phrase for this one. He explains it contextually:

> "if someone is scrolling on social media and is used to a lot of political commentary a well-formatted list tweet will make them stop their scroll"

> "visual pattern interrupt so you can have something on the screen that makes them be like okay what the heck just went on"

### 3 · Negativity Bias `[TAC][M:cognitive-psychology]`

Negativity bias is an established psychology term, used unchanged and uncredited here.
**Definition:** spinning your writing, hooks or the promised outcome in a negative light.
**Purpose:** leverage the brain's wiring to pay closer attention to and remember negativity and
threats.

The example is a rewrite pair. Instead of:

> "you are going to achieve great things"

you change the framing to:

> "you will never hit rock bottom again"

### 4 · Target Call Out (or Group Call Out) `[TAC][D]`

**Definition:** explicitly calling out the specific group or demographic you are talking to.
**Purpose:** make the content more potent and relatable to that audience, so they immediately know
the message or product is for them.

> "calling all coaches that sell a $10,000 offer or more I have a lead generation system for you that will bring you to $50,000 a month"

> "fathers are a gift to mankind"

### 5 · Problem Call Out `[TAC][D]`

**Definition:** stating, implying and agitating a painful problem people face. **Purpose:** capture
attention by highlighting a burning problem the reader is experiencing before introducing your
solution.

The two openers he gives:

> "most people suck at..."

> "if you struggle with"

q22 completes both into full hooks: *"Most people suck at writing"*, *"If you struggle to focus for
more than 5 minutes"*, and adds two more Problem Call Outs q21 does not carry: *"The worst skill you
can learn is web design"* and *"You feel terrible because your subconscious knows that you could be
doing better"*.

### 6 · Potential Benefit `[TAC][D]`

**Definition:** the direct opposite of the problem call out. Explicitly illustrating the positive
outcome, transformation or benefit of overcoming a problem. **Purpose:** answer the subconscious
question in the reader's mind so they understand the exact value of reading.

**This is the only one of the ten with no standalone example line anywhere in the corpus**
`[WRN][A]`. What he supplies instead is the subconscious question the content must answer:

> "what's in it for me?"

### 7 · Social Proof `[TAC][M:Cialdini]`

"Social proof" is Cialdini's term, used unchanged and uncredited in this file. **Definition:**
flexing your results, experience, tangible progress or credentials. **Purpose:** build authority
and bridge the information gap. When people see your results they assume you know something they do
not, which builds trust and convinces them to read on.

He names a move inside it, the "humble flex": share massive milestones as relatable learning
moments.

> "I made 468k in a month"

The beginner versions:

> "I just closed my first client"

> "I just finished the first chapter of my book"

The 468k figure is `[G]`, and it carries the corpus-wide confound: his revenue comes from selling
the teaching of this system, and no figure anywhere in this corpus comes from a student.

### 8 · Confidence and Conviction `[TAC][D]`

**Definition:** eliminating words that imply uncertainty, speaking in absolutes, and exaggerating
your point to add energy. **Purpose:** make the argument clear, credible and polarising, because
people do not want to follow someone who sounds unsure or who stays in the safe middle ground.

The before-and-after pair. Rather than:

> "it may be wise if some people develop their skill set"

you say:

> "it is crucial that everyone on planet Earth develops their skill set"

### 9 · Punch, Rhythm, and Readability `[TAC][D]`

**Definition:** structuring the visual flow of your writing by starting short, getting straight to
the point, and heavily using line breaks and bullet points. **Purpose:** guide the reader's eyes
smoothly down the page, prevent boredom from large blocks of text, keep them engaged through the
whole post.

The example demonstrates itself:

> "start short into the point then just like this continue on with your idea use LINE breaks people love bullet points you can get as creative as you'd like"

### 10 · Warnings or Cautionary Advice `[TAC][D]`

**Definition:** starting your content by warning the reader about a trap, pitfall or common
mistake. **Purpose:** warning people of a trap and explaining why it is dangerous immediately
positions you as an authority who has navigated the path.

> "be careful telling people your goals it releases dopamine similar to actually achieving them skip the instant gratification go quiet and build"

## Hook construction — the three balance elements and the six building blocks

**Provenance** `[FRM][D]` for the six blocks and the triad as stated. The relevance / awareness /
effort triad tracks Hormozi's dream-outcome, likelihood, time-delay and effort variables closely,
and Pass 1 flags that as the highest-stakes unsettled provenance call in the lane.

**The three things a hook balances** `[FRM][D]`

- **Relevance.** What is in it for the reader, hinted through a resolved pain or a potential
  benefit.
- **Awareness.** Language simple or complex enough for the specific audience you are writing to.
- **Effort.** How fast and easily they receive the promised result.

**The six building blocks of a hook, complete** `[FRM][D]` (q22; q18 lists only the first five)

1. **Big Problem.** Summarise all the problems you have listed into one burning pain point.
2. **Big Benefit.** Summarise the benefits into one massive advantage or desirable outcome.
3. **Big Idea.** Condense the most impactful parts of your content into a single sentence.
4. **Transformation Process.** Use numbers or a unique mechanism or name to hint at the process,
   which creates a curiosity gap.
5. **Time Frame.** Quantify how long it will take the reader to read the content or achieve the
   result.
6. **Negative Personal Experience.** Imply a personal low point and the negative emotion attached
   to it, to build a relatable connection.

You piece together the most potent combination of the six. The same six blocks are restated in q28
as the thread hook's ingredients.

## The Value Equation — macronutrients and micronutrients of value

**Provenance** `[FRM][D+M:Hormozi]`. "The Value Equation" is Hormozi's name from *$100M Offers*.
Koe's contents share almost nothing with Hormozi's variables, but the name is exact and it is
uncredited in this file. The nutrition metaphor and the specific part-lists are his.

**The frame** `[FRM][D]` A hook must be framed around human psychology and value creation. Value
splits into overarching **macronutrients** (narrative structure, perspective) and detailed
**micronutrients** (perception shapers).

**The three Macronutrients of Value (Perspective), complete** `[FRM][D]` The overarching
architecture of human behaviour and storytelling.

1. **Problem.** A limitation or challenge that creates pain in a person's life when left unsolved.
2. **Goal.** An impactful end result or desirable outcome that allows the recipient to evolve
   beyond the problem.
3. **Process.** A creative system that breeds the knowledge, skill and awareness necessary to
   bridge the gap between the problem and the goal.

**The six Micronutrients of Value (Perception), complete** `[FRM][D]` The elements you sprinkle
into hooks and writing to shape how your value is perceived.

1. **Concept.** Packaging and naming your value, his own example being "The 1-Person Business", so
   it captures attention and sticks in people's heads.
2. **Proof.** Giving people the belief that the goal is possible, by sharing personal or peer
   results.
3. **Risk Reversal.** Reducing the perceived uncertainty, friction and difficulty of getting
   results. `[M:copywriting-tradition]`, the tradition's own term used unchanged.
4. **Pain Points.** Amplifying the initial problem to illustrate how deeply it is negatively
   impacting their life beneath the surface.
5. **Benefits.** Increasing the desire to reach the solution, which makes their motivation to act
   sustainable.
6. **Awareness.** Recognising the number of people who suffer from the problem, can benefit from
   the solution, and are ready to receive the clarity to act.

## The Five Levels of Awareness

**Provenance** `[FRM][M:copywriting-tradition]`, specifically **Eugene Schwartz's** ladder from
*Breakthrough Advertising*. The stage names are unchanged and no attribution appears anywhere in
q22 or q29. q33 records Koe uploading *Breakthrough Advertising* to Gemini 2.5 Pro to build a
copywriting prompt, so the source is one he has worked from directly. `[M:]` rather than
`[D+M:]`, because nothing was renamed. What is his own is the **mapping of content format to
awareness level**, which stays `[D]`.

**Why it matters to a hook** `[TAC][D]` To write an effective hook you must know what level of
awareness your target reader is currently in.

**The five rungs, complete, with his instruction for each** `[TAC][D]`

1. **Unaware.** They are unaware of their problem and how it is hurting their quality of life.
   *Focus on pain points.*
2. **Problem Aware.** They know they have a problem but do not know a solution exists. *Focus on
   amplifying the impact of the problem.*
3. **Solution Aware.** They know a solution exists but are comparing options. *Focus on giving
   actionable advice and the process.*
4. **Product Aware.** They know about your specific streamlined path or system but need proof or
   objection handling. *Focus on your unique way of doing things.*
5. **Most Aware.** They are ready to change and just need a nudge or reason to act. *Focus on
   covering different angles to say the same thing 1,000 different ways.*

**Example coverage is 3 rungs of 5** `[WRN][A]` He supplies hooks for Levels 1, 2 and 4 only.
**Levels 3 (Solution Aware) and 5 (Most Aware) have instructions and no example hook anywhere in
the corpus.**

**Level 1 (Unaware), verbatim** `[TPL][D]`:

> "You're always tired because you stuff your face with hot pockets ubereats and foods that lead to nutrient deficiency"

**Level 2 (Problem Aware), verbatim** `[TPL][D]`:

> "If you're tired all the time weigh yourself every morning eat your body weight and protein drink water until you pee clear walk 10 000 steps a day thank me later when you feel so good that you hate the thought of ruining that feeling with junk food"

**Level 4 (Product/Solution Aware), verbatim** `[TPL][D]`:

> "Why you should start intermittent fasting morning focus is insane you don't have to plan morning food lunch and dinner can be massive meals"

**Format-to-level mapping** `[TAC][D]` (from q29, restated here so the section stands alone):
short-form targets Levels 1 to 3; medium-form bridges Levels 1 to 3 toward dedicated readers;
long-form targets Levels 2 to 5.

## Sentence and word rules

All from q23. `[TAC][D]` unless marked.

**Reading level.** Write for a 5th-grade reading level. Do not water down the ideas, but use
straightforward, simple language rather than overcomplicating your wording to sound smart.

**The golden rule, verbatim** `[TPL][M:copywriting-tradition]` (a long-standing copywriting maxim,
uncredited here):

> "clear not clever"

**Openings.** Start short and concise. Begin with short, punchy statements to capture attention
immediately. Once the reader is engaged, verbatim:

> "let loose and get creative with how your sentences flow"

**Line breaks.** Avoid large academic paragraphs, which destroy readability on social media.
Frequent line breaks guide the reader's eyes down the page and add emphasis.

**Bullet points.** Order the points visually from shortest length to longest.

**Punctuation.** Break apart sentences with em dashes and parentheses instead of commas. Opening a
parenthesis and extending a thought builds anticipation, because the reader wants to find out how
the phrase closes.

**Certainty.** Eliminate words that imply uncertainty. Speak in absolutes and exaggerate to add
energy. The before-and-after pair, verbatim, instead of:

> "it may be wise if some people develop their skill set"

write:

> "it is crucial that everyone on planet Earth develops their skill set"

**Voice.** Cut passive voice: it is boring and ruins the anticipation of a story. Look up articles
on active voice and practice editing until active voice is a habit, because it makes you sound far
more confident.

**Grammar** `[WRN][D]` Ignore traditional grammar rules. Do not obsess over grammatically correct
writing or worry about run-on sentences, because trying too hard to sound clever or polished makes
your articulation suffer.

**Drafting** `[PRN][D]` Accept that the first draft will suck. Get the idea down, expect the first
iteration to be bad, then edit specifically to make the body more impactful and attention-grabbing.

**Editing with training wheels.** Paste a proven post format next to your draft and edit your own
ideas to match that proven rhythm and structure.

**Editing after publishing.** The final editing pass happens after you publish. Use market feedback
(engagement) to see which ideas resonate, then continuously rewrite and refine those concepts from
different angles.

**The organising claim of the file** `[PRN][D]` Prioritise visual readability over traditional
grammar. This puts him in direct conflict with the repo's own prose rules, and a lens should state
the conflict rather than smooth it over.

## Cross-Domain Synthesis

**Provenance** `[FRM][D]` for the named framework and its three stages.

**What it is** `[TAC][D]` An advanced writing framework for structuring long-form content
(newsletters, articles, video scripts) by combining your primary topic with concepts, patterns or
metaphors from entirely different disciplines to support your argument.

**The three stages** `[TAC][D]`

1. **Introduce and amplify a problem.** State a relatable problem your audience faces and
   illustrate what happens if it is left unsolved.
2. **Cross-domain synthesis.** Instead of giving the answer, teach a pattern or concept from a
   completely different discipline (your secondary interests) to explain why the problem exists, or
   how the overarching principle works.
3. **Unique process.** Wrap up with a step-by-step solution that stems from your own contemplation
   rather than someone else's recycled advice.

**Sourcing the second domain** `[TAC][D]` Pull concepts by actively studying outside your main
niche. Become a "specialized generalist" who consumes across psychology, philosophy, science and
health, and actively looks for overlapping principles.

**The four worked examples he gives** `[TPL][D]`

- **Physics + productivity.** Writing about distraction and deep work, he pulls in **entropy** from
  physics to illustrate how distractions naturally pull the mind toward disorder.
- **Spirituality + marketing.** To teach the marketing concept of "levels of awareness", he pulled
  insights from the spiritual book *Awareness* by **Anthony De Mello** `[CIT][M:Anthony-De-Mello]`.
  This is the single explicit outside-source credit anywhere in q13–q24, and he gives it for the
  inspiration to write about the awareness ladder while the ladder itself, Eugene Schwartz's, goes
  uncredited in q22. That asymmetry is the sharpest coinage-ceiling evidence in the lane.
- **Metaphysics + focus.** Writing his book on how to focus, he merged the topic with philosophy,
  psychology, metaphysics and spirituality instead of giving productivity hacks. The book is *The
  Art of Focus* `[BIO][G]`.
- **Neuroscience + philosophy.** Study both and you can synthesise the connections to create an
  entirely new category to write about, such as **"neuro-philosophy"**.

**Why he says it works** `[PRN][D]` True originality is rarely inventing entirely new concepts; it
is exploring existing ideas in a fresh way. Ten people might write a generic post about how to
focus, but only you have your specific combination of interests, experiences and studies.
Synthesising across disciplines gives the audience a novel perspective that triggers a dopamine hit
of understanding, which makes the writing memorable and produces content that AI or "commodity
creators" cannot replicate.

**Newsletter application** `[FRM][D]` q16 names Cross-Domain Synthesis as an alternative newsletter
structure to bPAS: start with the problem, teach a concept from a completely different discipline
(his example: using physics to explain deep work), then give your unique step-by-step process.

## Patterns he teaches as craft that this repo's own prose filter bans

Recorded so a lens can quote him accurately without writing like him. Every item below is
something he instructs the reader to do.

- **Em dashes as the default sentence-breaker.** q23: break apart sentences with em dashes and
  parentheses instead of commas. The repo caps em dashes at 3 per 1000 words of prose.
- **Lists of exactly three for rhythm.** The Gamify line, the macronutrients, the Training Wheels
  expansion all run in threes.
- **Absolutes and exaggeration as conviction.** Commandment 8 instructs you to eliminate hedges and
  overstate. "it is crucial that everyone on planet Earth develops their skill set" is the model.
- **The exclusion hook, a denial followed by a substitution.** "This isn't for people who want to
  try to get in shape this is for people tired of their own excuses". The repo's filter calls this
  the reframe and bans it.
- **The significance tag.** His punchline stage exists to add a closing claim of importance to the
  lines above it.
- **Ignoring grammar for visual rhythm.** q23 tells you not to worry about run-on sentences.

The irony is worth stating once and then working around: this material is a manual for the exact
voice the repo's filter exists to catch. His lines belong in blockquotes. Our prose stays ours.
