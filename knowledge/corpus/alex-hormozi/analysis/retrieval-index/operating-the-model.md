## Hiring: the 40-to-1 screening bottleneck and group interviews

**Type:** CLM, FRM, PRN · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** a business with a cold outreach sales team and an HR screen; frontline hiring in volume · **Signals:** S-97.3, S-97.4, S-97.5 · **Files:** q97

- **CLM, the diagnosis:** cold outreach sales hit a ceiling because reps kept leaving. HR screened 10 candidates to find 1 qualified applicant, and sales hired 1 of every 4 applicants HR passed on. That took 40 one-on-one screening interviews to hire one frontline worker. The company is not named.
- **Group interview rule:** replace one-on-one screening with group interviews. Screen out the obvious red flags ("crazies") as a group. Send everyone else with a good work ethic and basic social skills straight to sales or role training, since the rest can be taught.
- **Fancy versus ground-level employees:** do not be picky when hiring ground-level, frontline lead getters. Hire anyone willing with a basic work ethic, train them, keep the winners. Save extreme pickiness for "fancy employees", the C-suite executives paid multi-six-figures.

## Training: 3Ds, Document, Demonstrate, Duplicate

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-97.8 · **Files:** q97

1. **Document:** make a checklist. Write the exact steps and record yourself doing the task across several shifts. Test it by doing the work yourself from your written directions only. If that gives an A+ result, you have Draft 1.
2. **Demonstrate:** sit with the employee and go through the checklist one step at a time. Each time they pause or ask a question, clarify and update the checklist. That is Draft 2.
3. **Duplicate:** the employee does the task from the checklist while you watch. Refine the checklist until they do it correctly.

Layer two adds the self-test and the draft numbering. Layer one's version and the discretionary budget: `closing-vetting-and-operating.md` § Delegation: 3 Ds and authority to match responsibility.

## Training rules: think slow, then smooth, then fast

**Type:** PRN · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-97.9 · **Files:** q97

1. **Responsibility:** if an employee gets confused or makes a mistake in training, you wrote bad or confusing directions.
2. **"Think slow, then smooth, then fast."** If they know what to do, the instructions are fine and they just need reps.
3. **Praise following the checklist over raw results.** If they follow it exactly and get a bad result, the checklist is wrong: praise them and fix the checklist on the spot.
4. **No punishment in training.** Praise good steps at once. Never punish errors, and do not take over when they mess up: pause, step back, let them try again.
5. **One step at a time:** if several steps are wrong, correct one step at a time.

## Performance Diamond: four levers when performance drops

**Type:** FRM, SCR · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-97.12, S-97.13 · **Files:** q97

Check the levers in this order:
1. **Communication problem, they don't know you want it done.** Clues: "I didn't know you wanted me to do that," "I didn't know you wanted that done first." Fix: have them describe their job, priorities, schedule and targets back to you in their own words.
2. **Training problem, they don't know how.** Clues: "I don't know how to do that," "I didn't know you wanted it done that way." Fix: do not ask "do you understand?"; have them demonstrate the checklist live in front of you.
3. **Motivation problem, they don't want to.** Causes: wrong reward timing, frequency or source; friction or aversion at work; outside life events (divorce, illness, sleep). Fix: adjust the reward levers or remove the friction.
4. **Environment or systemic friction:** remove operational barriers that management failed to prevent.

Friction shows up both as a cause in lever 3 and as lever 4; the answer may have split one lever in two.

**Hard feedback question ("Layla's Diamond"):** "Over the last [X period of time], your [X performance] has changed from the norm. What do you think has gotten in the way and how can I help?" The answer credits the diamond to Layla (Layla Hormozi, going by the name); the attribution is not checked against the audio.

## Cost Per Engaged Lead: payroll as the acquisition cost

**Type:** FRM, CLM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** a business with paid lead-getting staff; Acquisition.com · **Signals:** S-97.14, S-97.15 · **Files:** q97, q102

**Cost Per Engaged Lead (CPEL) = Total Payroll ÷ Total Engaged Leads Generated.** q102 words the numerator "Total Marketing & Sales Employee Payroll" ($100M Lost Chapters, Section D, chapter 24).

Worked example:
- $100,000 payroll ÷ 1,000 engaged leads = $100 CPEL.
- If 1 in 10 engaged leads becomes a customer, CAC = $1,000 ($100 x 10).
- With LTGP of $4,000, LTGP:CAC = 4:1.

**CLM, the Acquisition.com benchmark:** a content team costing $100,000/month that produces 30,000 engaged leads a month comes to $3.33 per engaged lead in payroll.

This is the only formula in layer two that makes labour the acquisition cost. Pass 2 L2.2: nothing says how the money model's 30-day gross profit target changes when the cost is labour `[A]`. ## Maker versus manager: the manager's schedule

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-98.1 · **Files:** q98, q102

- **Who:** executives, department heads, operational supervisors whose work is directing, deciding, collecting and reporting data, leading, training and communicating across functions.
- **Time unit:** blocks of 30 to 60 minutes, giving 10 to 20 work chunks a day.

## Maker versus manager: the maker's schedule

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-98.2 · **Files:** q98, q102

- **Who:** individual contributors who make creative or technical output: engineers, software developers, copywriters, builders, video and media editors, presenters, writers.

## Maker versus manager: what a meeting costs a maker

**Type:** MEC · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-98.3 · **Files:** q98

- A 30-minute meeting costs a maker 10x what it costs a manager.
- A meeting at 10:00 am splits the morning into a 2-hour block and a 1.5-hour block, and neither is long enough for deep, flow-dependent work.

## Growth: the seven levels of advertising and organisation

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** solo founder up to $100M+ · **Signals:** S-98.6 · **Files:** q98

1. **Solo founder, 0 employees:** 1 offer, 1 avatar, 1 platform. Warm outreach to everyone you know to get your first 5 customers.
2. **Personal capacity limit:** maximum warm outreach plus free content or basic ads until your own schedule is completely full.
3. **First staffing, 1 to 4 employees ("Stage 3: Stabilize"):** hire frontline employees (a videographer, a media buyer, a cold caller) to advertise and deliver for you. Problems at this stage: cash conversion cycle bottlenecks, time wasted on bad leads, customer confusion.
4. **Product quality and referral engine:** improve the product until 25%+ of new customers come from word-of-mouth referrals. Referrals remove cash as a growth bottleneck.
5. **Multi-channel and multi-platform:** new ad placements, media formats and platforms, plus higher-leverage channels such as affiliate networks.
6. **Hire department leaders ("Killers"):** experienced C-suite and department heads who run Sales, Marketing and Finance without the founder.
7. **The $100M+ machine:** executive leadership drives growth across all 8 functions on its own.

Looks blended: "Stage 3: Stabilize" is a label from a separate stage-of-growth model. Level 1 is close to the 1-1-1 rule, which q103 runs to $1,000,000 (`leads-and-outreach.md` § 1-1-1 rule: one offer, one avatar, one channel). Level 4's 25%+ is the only referral-share threshold in layer two. Layer one's channel order: `leads-and-outreach.md` § Channel roadmap for a new business, seven steps.

## Levers: CAC, LTGP and payback period

**Type:** FRM, NUM, PRN · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-99.1, S-99.2, S-99.4 · **Files:** q99

The three levers of Customer Financed Acquisition:
1. **Lower CAC.** CAC = total marketing, sales and support spend ÷ customers acquired, counting ad spend, sales commissions and salaries, marketing software, media buyers and creative production. This version puts "support" spend in CAC; q102's formula (advertising + sales payroll + software + commissions) does not.
2. **Raise LTGP.** LTGP = gross profit per transaction x average number of transactions, where gross profit = price paid minus direct fulfilment costs.
3. **Shorten the payback period (PPD):** the time until a customer's gross profit covers their acquisition cost (GP ≥ CAC).

**Baseline:** 30-day gross profit > CAC at minimum; ideally 30-day gross profit ≥ 2x CAC, so customer payments fund the next acquisition without outside capital.

**Asymmetry:** CAC can only approach zero, while LTGP can go to infinity.

Lane 15 (q74 to q76) holds the same formulas with worked examples and the three CFA levels. Layer one's ratio targets: `leads-and-outreach.md` § LTGP to CAC: the ratio targets.

## What to change first: the 3x industry CAC test

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** a business with an industry CAC benchmark · **Signals:** S-99.3 · **Files:** q99, q103

Compare your CAC with the industry average.
- **CAC more than 3x the industry average:** an advertising or sales problem. Ask "Do my engaged leads have the problem I solve and the money to spend?"
- **CAC at or below 3x the industry average:** advertising is working well enough and the problem is the business model. Go straight to raising LTGP: higher prices, upsells, better retention and continuity.

q74 (lane 15) holds the same test. A business with no published industry CAC cannot run the test as stated.

## What to change first: generate flow, monetize flow, then add friction

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-99.5 · **Files:** q99

Build or fix the money model one stage at a time, in this order:
1. **Stage 1, Get Cash (attraction offers):** turn strangers into customers reliably and cover the basic acquisition cost, starting with a low-friction hook (free, win-your-money-back, or deep discount promotions).
2. **Stage 2, Get More Cash (upsells and downsells):** push 30-day gross profit to ≥ 2x CAC with immediate upsells (classic, menu, anchor, rollover) and downsells (payment plans, feature downsells) for those who say no.
3. **Stage 3, Get the Most Cash (continuity):** maximise LTGP and recurring revenue with memberships, subscriptions and waived-fee commitments.

## Warning: lowering price to cut churn

**Type:** WRN, CLM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** high-ticket licensing back end, $42,000/year · **Signals:** S-100.1, S-101.15 · **Files:** q100, q101

Lowering prices on an existing offer to cut churn or lift sales rarely works. It cuts top-line revenue and net profit without fixing the retention or value problem underneath.

**His case:** he cut the GymLords / high-ticket program from $42,000/year (about $800/week) to about $31,000/year ($600/week), hoping to halve churn, and added $100,000/month in extra fulfilment overhead. Churn did not change at all, top-line revenue fell 25%, and he lost $5,000,000 a year in profit, cutting his net income by a third.

Arithmetic: $800 to $600 a week is −25%; $600 x 52 = $31,200. A third of net income at $5M means net income was about $15M a year. q101 labels the $5M a "Claim / Calculation". Layer one tells the same story: `hormozi-record.md` § Gym Launch price cut: $800 to $600 a week and $5 million lost. Pass 2 §4 row 9: the price cut is the failure; pricing to the worst month is the lesson.

## Warning: discounting the same product, the car insurance example

**Type:** WRN · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general, a car dealership · **Signals:** S-100.4 · **Files:** q100

Do not negotiate or discount the price of the exact same product when the buyer objects. Remove features or offer other payment structures instead. Example: a car salesman offered add-on insurance at $5,000, then kept dropping the price for the same coverage after each refusal, down to $400. Buyer trust was gone: the buyer refused even $400 and began to wonder whether the car itself was a rip-off. This is the reason behind the feature downsell and payment-plan downsell: `upsell-and-downsell.md` § Downsell rules: when to present and how to frame it.

## Warning: changing a product customers love

**Type:** WRN, CLM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** one gym, 3,500 sq. ft. · **Signals:** S-100.11, S-101.6 · **Files:** q100, q101

Do not change a product or service customers love to fix profit margins. Fix the pricing structure. "If they love it, fix your pricing, don't fix the product!" (punctuation rewritten; the answer has a dash). Cost: damaged customer culture, lower satisfaction, lost goodwill.

**His case:** when the gym was packed with 40 to 50 members in 3,500 sq. ft., he cut session lengths in half to fit more people in, and lost one-third of his customer base (q101 only). q100 tells it as changing workout session formats after members were already raving about them.
