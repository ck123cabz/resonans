## CLOSER: the six-step sales conversation

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** phone sales · **Signals:** S-50.1, S-45.6 · **Files:** q50, q45, q65

1. **C, Clarify why they are here.** Ask what made them respond to the ad, email or outreach;
   find their intent and expectations at the start.
2. **L, Label them with the problem.** Define the gap between current and desired state, name
   the main obstacle (the "monster"), position the service as the sword that slays it.
3. **O, Overview past pain (the pain cycle).** Ask what they tried and why it failed; tie what
   worked to your solution and what failed to why it didn't stick. Poking past frustration
   stretches the "rubber band" of deprivation.
4. **S, Sell the vacation.** A 3-pillar pitch on the dream outcome, not the process; no long
   monologues; each pillar brief and followed by an analogy or metaphor.
5. **E, Explain away concerns.** Objections fall in 6 categories (q50): time, money,
   decision-makers, fit or preference, past experiences or other products, stalls or avoidance.
   q45 lists four: time, money, authority, fit.
6. **R, Reinforce the decision.** Validate the purchase at once to stop buyer's remorse; set
   expectations for the first 24 to 48 hours; keep every onboarding promise; BAMFAM (Book A
   Meeting From A Meeting: every interaction ends with a scheduled next step); pass detailed
   sales notes to onboarding so the customer never repeats themselves.

q65 cites $100M Leads part 10 for CLOSER and part 8 for BAMFAM.

## 3A: Acknowledge, Associate, Ask

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** phone sales · **Signals:** S-50.2, S-45.5 · **Files:** q50, q45, q55, q65

For objections and trap questions:
- **Acknowledge:** restate what they said; it shows listening and buys two seconds.
- **Associate:** link the concern to a trait of a successful customer. q50 script: "That's a
  great question, that actually shows you're a really rational person, which makes you just like
  Sarah who crushed it in our program." q45: "That's a great question. In fact, our best clients
  always ask about that..."
- **Ask:** answer with a question about their question. The one asking questions
  controls the conversation; answering puts you in the position of being judged. q45 examples:
  "Which specific features are you looking for?" or "What are you most afraid of having happen?"

The third A is "Ask" in q45 and q50, "Attack" in q65, with the same meaning. q65 cites the video
"After Closing 4000+ Sales, I Discovered a New Method to Close Deals Faster".

## Objections: never argue, use a third party, probe the fear

**Type:** SCR · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** phone sales · **Signals:** S-50.3, S-50.4, S-50.5, S-50.12 · **Files:** q50

- **Never disagree.** You never win a sale by winning an argument. "Be like smoke": side-step
  statements with deeper questions. Prospects believe what they say, so do not tell them they are
  a fit; ask until they say it.
- **Hard truths through a third party.** "It's funny you ask that, I had another client who asked
  that exact question earlier..." and the authority foil "Alex actually pointed this out to me
  earlier today..."
- **Universal probe** when a prospect hesitates late: "What are you most afraid of having happen?"
  or "What would make this a no?"
- **Ask more times.** The salespeople who ask for the sale most close the most. With reframing and
  rapport you can ask several times without alienating the buyer.

## Objections about price: add a bonus, never discount

**Type:** SCR · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** phone sales · **Signals:** S-50.6, S-50.7, S-62.14, S-05.7 · **Files:** q50, q51, q62

- Never discount the core offer. Discounting signals the price was negotiable, erodes authority
  and comes from weakness. Never compete on price or lower prices to win clients.
- Match a bonus to the objection and keep the price fixed: "I completely understand [obstacle].
  Tell you what, if we get you started today, I'll throw in our [Named Bonus] for free to make
  sure you succeed. Sound fair enough?"
- Never let an unaddressed objection kill a sale; build a bonus asset that removes the real-world
  friction. His case: an executive who ate lunch out every day refused his weight-loss programme;
  he wrote her an Eating Out Guide, closed the sale, and kept it as a permanent bonus.

## Jump anchor: raising price without losing sales

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** sales team selling $2,000 to $12,000 · **Signals:** S-50.9, S-68.2 · **Files:** q50, q53, q68, q71

1. Find the home-base price the team sells with conviction (e.g. $2,000).
2. To raise to $3,000, do not pitch $3,000; anchor at $4,000.
3. Offer $4,000 as $2,000 today and $2,000 in 30 days (the rep's home-base ask), or $3,000 in full
   today as a prepay discount.
4. The prospect sees $3,000 as $1,000 off; the rep can always fall back on the $2,000 ask.

Second example, $4,000 to $5,000: anchor at $6,000; uneven split $4,000 today + $2,000 in 30 days
(keeps more cash up front); or $5,000 today.

Third example (q68), $10,000 to $12,000: anchor with terms like $6,000 + $3,000 + $3,000 (or 3 x
$4,000) to push up-front prepayment at a discount. Here the installment total equals the target
itself, unlike the first two, and no prepay figure is given.

## Speed to lead and close-rate numbers

**Type:** NUM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** inbound lead businesses · **Signals:** S-50.10, S-50.11, S-50.13, S-66.7, S-72.9, S-41.7 · **Files:** q41, q45, q50, q66, q72

- Contacting a lead within 60 seconds of opt-in gives a 391% increase in sales (4.91x). Raising
  close rate from 50% to 80% is a 1.6x lift; cutting response time under a minute can give 5x
  throughput. Speed often moves revenue more than the script. The 391% looks like an industry
  statistic; no study is named.
- **The maniac:** one team member whose only job is to stop whatever they are doing and call leads
  the second they arrive. Needs lead volume to justify the hire.
- **Close-rate target:** 1 in 3 (33%) on qualified calls (q66).
- **Conversations per close:** expect 2 to 3 before closing higher-ticket sales (q41); local B2C
  businesses have local trust and close in 1 to 2 conversations or a single call (q72).
- **Speed of closing inside a call:** not covered. q50 was asked and answered with speed to lead
  instead; no rule on call length, number of calls or time from first contact to close (S-50.13,
  `[A]`).

## Raising prices: the virtuous cycle and the wine study

**Type:** MEC · **Prov:** [M*]; wine study wording [D] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-53.1, S-53.2, S-53.3, S-12.7 · **Files:** q51, q53, q54, q57, Offers p2

**Virtuous cycle of price:** higher price, higher emotional investment, better client results
(adherence), less demanding clients, excess gross profit, better talent and service, which
supports the higher price. "Those who pay the most pay the most attention." Low prices attract
bargain hunters who take the most time, complain most and churn fastest.

**Wine study:** testers got three identical wines labelled at low, medium and high prices and
rated the "expensive" one best. q53 calls it blind; Pass 2 §2 found in Offers part 2 that the
prices were visible, so "blind" is NotebookLM's error. An outside study, not named.

**Conviction:** premium rates need conviction in delivery; outwork self-doubt with reps and real
proof until your conviction beats the prospect's scepticism.

## Raising prices: anchoring and the pipeline notice

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** business with a sales pipeline · **Signals:** S-53.4, S-53.5, S-61.4 · **Files:** q53, q61

- **Anchoring:** set a high reference price early in the pitch; stacking bonuses or offering a
  lower prepay option against it widens the gap until the prospect's wallet resistance "snaps".
- **Pipeline notice:** before any price increase, announce it to all active leads and
  fence-sitters with a 2-week deadline to lock the old rate. It creates real urgency, a cash surge
  and a clean pipeline. He used price-increase deadlines at Gym Launch (q61).
- A price-rise letter to existing customers is question-shaped: not in the sources (Pass 2 §3).

## Raising prices: tripling on existing members and pricing to the worst month

**Type:** CLM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** local gym; B2B recurring services · **Signals:** S-49.10, S-49.11, S-62.2 · **Files:** q49, q53, q59, q62, q71

- **Tripling:** gym members moved from $99/month to $299/month. 33% cancelled, 67% stayed at 3x the
  rate. Revenue doubled (0.67 x 3 = 2.01), profits more than doubled, and fulfilment costs fell 33%
  as the most demanding clients left. Which gym and when is not given.
- **Worst month:** price recurring offers to the client's worst month, not their best. Gym Launch
  charged $3,500/month ($800/week), and clients cancelled in bad months despite making an extra
  $120,000 a year; competitors at $800 to $1,200/month kept clients 36 to 40 months (q49) or 36 to
  40+ months (q53, q59).
- Lowering prices almost never reduces churn. Fix fulfilment or pricing structure instead (q62).
  Pass 2 §4 row 9: his failed price cut is the failure; the worst-month rule is the lesson.

## Van Westendorp: four price questions

**Type:** SCR · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** new offer, price unknown · **Signals:** S-53.7, S-65.15 · **Files:** q53, q65

A standard market-research method he teaches, not his own. Ask prospects:
1. "At what price would this be so cheap that you would question its quality or doubt it would
   work?"
2. "At what price would this be so high that you wouldn't even consider buying it?"
3. "At what price would this be a bargain or a great deal?"
4. "At what price would it feel expensive, but you would still buy it anyway?" (the
   profit-maximising price point)

q65 names the video "How I Price Every New Offer".

## Tiers: no three-tier menu, enter at the top

**Type:** PRN · **Prov:** [A] for the menu; rest [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-68.1, S-68.4, S-68.6, S-53.8, S-51.2 · **Files:** q51, q53, q68

- Question-shaped: not in the sources. q68, a fresh query, found no three-tier structure
  (premium anchor, middle, entry). He sells one differentiated offer and ascends buyers. A
  "magnetic middle" tier is not in the sources either (S-53.8). Pass 2 §3.
- **Ascension instead:** customers enter on a front end and climb to higher-ticket back ends.
  Front-end examples in q68 mix his and competitors': gyms $21 for 20 days (the competitor offer he
  argues against), $99/month boot camp, $299/month membership, $500 6-week challenge deposit,
  $16,000 front-end intensive; agencies moving from $1,000 setup + $1,000/month to a $3,997 /
  $4,000 one-time setup with pay-for-performance.
- **Enter at the top:** higher prices, fewer clients, then move down-market as capacity grows.
  Tesla: Roadster first, then Model S ($120,000), Model X, Model 3.
- **Start high-touch:** $1,000,000 is easier as 100 clients at $10,000 (e.g. $200/month over 4
  years) than 10,000 buyers at $100. $200 x 48 = $9,600.

## Trust: proof beats promise

**Type:** PRN · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-54.1, S-54.3 to S-54.7, S-55.4 · **Files:** q54, q55

- "The hardest thing in all of marketing is getting someone to believe you." Trust and belief are
  the main currencies of business.
- Trust is the perceived likelihood term of the value equation. Of two surgeons doing the same
  procedure, the client pays far more for the one on their 10,000th operation.
- **Proof beats promise.** Business A promises 4 results with 1 review; Business B promises 1 result
  with 11,000 5-star reviews (q55: "11,000+"). The customer buys from B every time. q55 dates the
  ranking to a video, *How to Grow Your Business SO Fast in 2026*: although he wrote a book on
  offers, proof is the single highest-leverage priority.
- Bigger claims are easy and lazy; the hard work is proof, removing risk and shortening time to
  result.
- When sales stall, owners rush to a new offer, bigger promises or lower prices. If an offer fails
  despite good ad volume, the cause is often negative word of mouth or a lack of trust.
- "You don't need a new offer, you need trust": used only as a heading in q54 and q55, never
  quoted as his words (Pass 2 §3).

## Trust: damaging admissions, the flash roll and proof walls

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** local business ads · **Signals:** S-54.9 to S-54.14, S-72.16 · **Files:** q54, q55, q72

- **Damaging admission:** "State the facts and tell the truth" ("Nothing sells like the truth").
  Formula: [Bad Thing] + But + [Good Thing]. q54: "We don't have the biggest gym or fancy juice
  bar, but when you walk in, our trainers guarantee you get results". q55: "We have bad parking and
  a small facility, BUT when you walk in, you're going to get the best workout of your life".
- **Flash roll:** describe problems the prospect has not met yet and how you already solved them;
  naming their hidden pain makes them hand you authority.
- **Proof up to your eyeballs:** case studies, before-and-after photos, video interviews, reviews.
  When starting out, work free for beta users in exchange for reviews. Screenshot reviews, put
  them in $1 dollar-store frames, and cover sales materials and office walls.
- **Risk** is the largest friction point and the number one objection. A guarantee ("If you don't
  achieve X in Y time, we will Z") moves risk to the seller.
- **Branding** is the accumulation of kept promises. Once deep trust exists, prospects say "I
  missed the presentation, but just charge my card, I know I'll get my money's worth."

## Vetting: when to add qualification

**Type:** PRN · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-57.1, S-57.7, S-72.1 · **Files:** q57, q72

- Qualification protects capacity, raises results and maximises LTV. Add fences only when
  capacity is maxed, the sales team is swamped with unqualified prospects, or you will give up 33%
  to 50% of raw lead volume to raise ticket size and attract a higher-tier avatar.
- Selling to someone who will not get a good result is deceptive selling.
- Commitment requires investment; a prospect who puts nothing on the line has no buy-in.

## Vetting: five levers for lead quality

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** ad-funded with a sales team · **Signals:** S-57.2, S-72.3 · **Files:** q57, q72

Higher quality usually means lower volume.
1. **Targeting:** wealthy zip codes, income, profession; strict operating requirements for the
   avatar.
2. **Number of steps:** 10 steps to reach a call, not 1, filters tire-kickers.
3. **Nature of the offer:** a paid or straight-to-call offer, not a broad giveaway.
4. **Application:** open-ended paragraph questions, not checkboxes; goals, acknowledging the
   problem, a financial baseline, a minimum-spend question.
5. **Value first:** require a 5-minute tactical video or mini-training before booking; viewers
   arrive seeing you as an authority.

## Vetting inside the funnel and at events

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** paid lead gen, events, communities · **Signals:** S-72.5, S-72.6, S-72.8, S-38.22, S-57.5 · **Files:** q38, q57, q72

- Remove any bonus that low-quality leads love, so the offer stops attracting them.
- Disclose price or a price range early to filter price-sensitive prospects.
- Put qualification dropdowns on lead-magnet opt-in pages; route qualified leads to calls and
  others elsewhere.
- Segment live-event attendees by revenue or income and pitch only the qualified, for 80%+ close
  rates in the vetted room. In communities, require a milestone (e.g. 1,000 subscribers) before a
  higher-tier sales call.
- A free item with a gate: a custom "CEO" hat or shirt at a conference only for verified CEOs.
- A deposit is a vetting tool: the $500 challenge deposit produced 78% to 80% goal achievement.

## Vetting: four market qualifications

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-51.10, S-72.2, S-72.7 · **Files:** q51, q72

1. **Massive pain**, acute and urgent.
2. **Purchasing power:** money plus decision authority. A problem without money is a non-profit; a
   resume service for unemployed workers failed because they could not pay a $300 fee.
3. **Easy to target:** gathered in identifiable places (trade associations, email directories,
   hyper-local submarkets, online communities).
4. **Growing market:** flat or expanding (med spas, ageing boomers), not contracting.

**What vetting earned him** (q72): a competing gym consultant made 70x less profit on equal sales
volume because he ran generic ads ("if you're in fitness and want to make money, come to me") and
got $5,000 customer LTV, while he vetted for established gym owners and got $42,000 LTV. $42,000 /
$5,000 = 8.4x; the 70x is not derivable, and Gym Launch LTV elsewhere is $126,000+.

**Not covered** (S-72.19, `[A]`): scripts for firing an existing client; legal guidance on
protected classes when rejecting prospects; qualification forms outside fitness, accounting and
digital agencies.

## Service business: 1-1-1 and the two ways to grow

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** up to $1M · **Signals:** S-51.1, S-51.5, S-65.14, S-62.9 · **Files:** q51, q62, q65

- **1-1-1:** 1 product, 1 avatar, 1 acquisition channel until $1,000,000 in revenue (q51). q62:
  until $1 million to $10 million. More too early dilutes focus. His own case: while building Gym
  Launch he ran 6 gyms, a chiropractor agency, a dental agency and a supplement line, and revenue
  slowed across all of them.
- **Two ways to grow:** more customers, or higher customer lifetime value (higher prices, more
  purchases, lower fulfilment cost). q65's three ways: more customers, higher average purchase
  value, more purchases per customer.

## Service business: do it by hand, pre-sell, then productise

**Type:** PRN · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** early stage; coaching · **Signals:** S-51.3, S-51.4, S-48.9 · **Files:** q48, q51

- Do the service by hand first to learn client friction; turn the manual fixes into templates,
  calculators or recorded assets later.
- Pre-sell and run grand openings: market and sell before delivery exists. Pre-selling for 1 to 3
  months builds cash, validates demand and avoids fixed costs on an unproven idea.
- **Gatorade or shot glass:** hiring cheap sub-coaches dilutes the founder's value (diluted
  Gatorade in big glasses). Keep the founder's concentrated X-factor and serve it in smaller "shot
  glasses" to group cohorts. As demand grows, move from 1-on-1 custom work to small groups,
  done-with-you and productised assets.

## Service business: the SMB dead zone

**Type:** WRN · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** agencies and software selling to SMBs · **Signals:** S-51.15, S-68.7, S-66.5 · **Files:** q51, q53, q66, q68

Services to small and medium businesses at $1,500 to $3,000/month ($450/week) churn fast, sticking
4 to 6 months on average, because SMB volatility becomes your volatility. Two exits:
- **Down-market:** an automated offer at $300 to $400/month (q53: under $400/month; q66: $400 or
  less) with near-zero fulfilment, sticking 29 to 38 months (q51) or 30 to 40 months (q66).
- **Up-market:** high-ticket clients ($10,000+ or performance-based, per q53) with established
  operations and proven sales metrics.

$450/week x 52 / 12 = $1,950/month, inside the range. US figures. Pass 2 §2: the dead zone was not
found in the transcribed audio, so book or video origin is `[A]`.

## Service business: no unconditional refunds on heavy fulfilment

**Type:** WRN · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** high-touch services · **Signals:** S-51.17, S-55.7 · **Files:** q51, q56

Do not offer unconditional, no-questions refunds on high-touch services with heavy labour or hard
fulfilment costs: you pay both the fulfilment and the refund. Use conditional service guarantees
("we keep working for free until you hit the goal") or anti-guarantees ("all sales final"). q56
places the warning in $100M Offers. In videos he describes an unconditional 5-week money-back
guarantee in his early gym challenge sales; Pass 2 §4 row 15 reads this as consistent, since the
boot camp was low cost per head and high volume.

## Service business: picking a niche and staying in it

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general; info products · **Signals:** S-51.11 to S-51.14, S-20.7 · **Files:** q51, q65

- **3Ps:** a Pain you overcame, a Passion, a Profession you practised.
- **Niching pays:** up to 100x pricing power for the same deliverable. A generic time-management
  course sells for $19; "Time Management for B2B Outbound Power Tools & Gardening Sales Reps" can
  sell for $1,997 ($1,997 / $19 is about 105x). Pass 2 §2: not found in the transcribed audio.
- **No niche-hopping** ("niche slapping"): leaving a market after one failed offer resets all
  learned data to zero.
- **Never sell into a shrinking industry:** nothing overcomes a market contracting 25% a year
  (printed newspapers; Lloyd's newspaper software, which Pass 2 did not find in the audio).

## Service business: offer fatigue, change the lightest thing first

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** businesses running ads; local radius · **Signals:** S-51.16, S-72.11 · **Files:** q51, q72

When campaigns slow, change elements in this order: Creative, then Body Copy, then Offer Wrapper
(name), then Duration, then Enhancers, then Core Monetisation Model (last resort). Small local
radiuses fatigue fast; rotate the wrapper, headline, season or creative, not the core service.
Examples: "6-Week Lean by Halloween Challenge", "21-Day Mommy Makeover", "Back to School Free
Braces Giveaway", "Valentine's Day Lover Special".

## Service business: 70/20/10 and don't break what works

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-62.21, S-62.22, S-42.3 · **Files:** q42, q44, q62, q66

Entrepreneurs tire of their offers and ads long before the market does. When a gym session format
or ad was working, he got bored and changed it, which dropped satisfaction and broke funnels. The
rule: 70% of resources to copying what works, 20% to adjacent tweaks, 10% to new experiments. Ad
creative: 70% proven hooks, 20% adjacent variations, 10% moonshots.

## Delegation: 3 Ds and authority to match responsibility

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** business with staff · **Signals:** S-51.7, S-51.8, S-72.18, S-66.10 · **Files:** q51, q66, q72

- **3 Ds:** Document every step as a checklist; Demonstrate it in front of the employee;
  Duplicate: they do it in front of you with the checklist until their output matches yours.
- **Discretionary budget:** spend up to 100% of a customer's monthly payment, up to $500, to fix a
  complaint without sign-off (q51); up to $500 or $1,000 (q72); up to $1,000 (q66).
- **Operator pay** (q72): on-target earnings e.g. $150k/year for a location manager = 50% base
  ($75k) + a profit share of 10% to 12.5% of location profit.

## Partners: TEAM and never carry 100% of the risk

**Type:** WRN · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** partnerships · **Signals:** S-51.18, S-62.4, S-62.20 · **Files:** q51, q62

- Never partner with someone who has your exact skillset because you are friends. A partner must
  bring what you lack: Time, Expertise, Assets or Money.
- Never take on 100% of the financial exposure in a partnership, and do not partner with people
  who lack a financial track record.
- Separate operating labour from equity: operating partners get a market-rate salary before
  profits go to equity holders.

## Local business: location rules and the pre-sale test

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** local B2C, multi-location · **Signals:** S-51.6, S-72.10, S-72.12 to S-72.17, S-72.20 · **Files:** q51, q72

- **Nail it before you scale it:** no second location until location #1 runs completely without
  you and shows 6 consecutive months of growth without your intervention. Vacation test: 30 days
  (q51) or 1 month (q72) away.
- **Pre-sale market test:** before a 5-year lease or a $500k buildout, run $500 to $1,000 in ads
  across 5 to 10 candidate zip codes; open where cost per lead is lowest.
- **Barter** with limited capital: memberships for IT equipment and printers, janitorial, food
  prep, videography.
- **Shadow** non-competing local owners 1 to 2 steps ahead and ask detailed questions.
- **Upsell physical products** with no extra labour (supplements, gear, mats) at once, to cover
  acquisition cost on day one.
- **Small share needed:** a gym needs about 200 members in a city of 1,000,000, stated as 0.002%;
  it is 0.02%.
- **Not covered** (`[A]`): permits, licensing, insurance, tax, lease negotiation, and blueprints
  for local businesses other than micro-gyms, studios and chiropractic or med-spa clinics.

## Offer as the main lever: the $10K versus $10M offer

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*]; TJ quote [D] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-48.1, S-48.2, S-48.5, S-48.7, S-48.10 · **Files:** q48, q57, Offers p1 and p4

- The offer is the strongest lever; changing it changes cash flow fastest without changing the
  back end. From his mentor TJ, matched to $100M Offers parts 1 and 4: "Make people an offer so
  good they feel stupid saying no."
- Ads, cold outreach and content grow linearly; a remarkable product unlocks word-of-mouth growth
  (q48 calls it quadratic, also "exponential").
- **Six differences** (q48; its diagram shows four, so the six may be NotebookLM's grouping):
  1. Lifetime gross profit per customer: one service and no ascension, against LTV-first so you
     can outspend on acquisition and talent.
  2. Scalable delivery: 1-on-1 founder labour capped at 25 to 60 clients, against small group,
     done-with-you or productised.
  3. Avatar quality: beginners, against buyers for whom the same deliverable returns a lot.
  4. Value equation denominator: large businesses win on time delay and effort.
  5. Need to believe: several hurdles and long calls, against zero or one obvious belief.
  6. Ask to give: high-friction pitches, against a 3.5:1 give-to-ask ratio.
- **Same advice, different value:** about $100 to a beginner and $2 million to a founder doing $5
  million a year (q48: added value; q57: added gross profit).

## Offer as the main lever: the agency split test

**Type:** NUM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** ad agency, $10,000 ad spend · **Signals:** S-48.3, S-48.4, S-64.4 · **Files:** q48, q56, q64

$10,000 on ads, 300,000 impressions, only the offer changed:
- **Commodity, $1,000 down + $1,000/month:** 0.13% response, 40 booked calls, 30 shows, 5 closes
  (16.7%), $5,000 up-front cash, 0.5:1 ROAS (lost $5,000 up front).
- **Grand slam, $3,997 pay-on-show / pay-for-performance:** 0.33% response (2.5x), 100 booked, 75
  shows, 28 closes (37.3%), $112,000 up-front cash (28 x $3,997 = $111,916), 11.2:1 ROAS.
- 22.4x more cash up front on the same spend and back end.

q48's general multipliers (ad response 2.5x, close rate 2.3x to 2.5x, price often 4x, ROAS from
negative to profitable on day one) match this one example and are probably drawn from it. Whether
it was a real test or an illustration is not stated.
