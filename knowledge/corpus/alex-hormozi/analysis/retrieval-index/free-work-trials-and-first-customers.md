## Free work: when he recommends it

**Type:** PRN · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** beginner with zero proof · **Signals:** S-56.1, S-56.2, S-52.5 · **Files:** q56, q52, q39 (re-run)

He recommends free work when you have zero social proof, zero skill, or are launching a new
offer. "You suck at first": a beginner has no track record, and charging full price for a weak
service makes angry buyers and negative word of mouth.

Why start free, the four reasons in the re-run q39:
1. It gets essential sales and delivery reps.
2. You will lack skill at first, and free clients are far more forgiving.
3. It helps you find and remove **hidden costs** (friction, effort, time).
4. It produces initial proof, case studies and testimonials.

q56 adds that free work finds product defects and builds conviction.

Who gets it: only qualified prospects who fit the avatar, never broke freebie seekers (q56). Yet
the free five come from warm personal contacts, not a screen (S-69.1 against S-70.3); the
sources do not reconcile the two.

Pass 2 §4 row 14: this advice is for a beginner with no proof, with three terms attached. His
opposition to free trials (see "Free trials: the warning against unconditioned trials") is
about unconditioned trials for businesses buying traffic. Not a contradiction.

## Price step-up: raise 20% every five sales

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [D] · **Scale:** beginner unsure of price · **Signals:** S-53.6, S-65.16, S-52.7 · **Files:** q53, q65, q66, Leads p3

His words, matched to $100M Leads part 3: "raise it by 20% every five until you find your sweet
spot".

The 5-to-5-to-5 rule as q53 gives it `[M*]`: start lower; every time you sell 5 units at the
current price, raise the price 20% for the next 5; keep stepping 20% until conversion drops,
which shows the price ceiling.

points of discount per batch of five until full price; once at full price, the price keeps

## Free to paid: the transition script

**Type:** SCR · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** free client at capacity · **Signals:** S-69.4, S-52.8 · **Files:** q69, q52, q39 (re-run)

At capacity, he goes back to the free clients. Three renderings:

- q69, closest to speech: "I am accepting somebody who's paying full vote in order to do this if
  you would like me to keep doing this for you you would have to start paying to match them are
  you comfortable with that". If yes, they become paying. If no: "no sweat because all you did
  is you help them for free and you say no worries can I get the the killer killer review you
  asked me for or I asked you for". "Full vote" is likely a transcription error.
- q52 paraphrase: "I am accepting paying clients now to fill my remaining slots. If you'd like

## Hidden costs: "your free stuff is too expensive"

**Type:** PRN · **Prov:** [D] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** Pass 2 §2 (new), S-56.5 · **Files:** Leads p3, q39 (re-run), q56

sacrifice. "Your free stuff is too expensive." His instruction: "figure out the hidden costs".

The re-run q39 table puts it as the line between working and failing: free works when the
friction, time and effort asked of the recipient are kept minimal, and fails when the hidden

His example as the buyer `[M*]` (q56): a video editor offered to make and post short videos for
him free, with zero effort from him, and he said yes at once. The only case in the set of free
work offered unsolicited to a business.

## Free content quality: the 99% rule and give until they ask

**Type:** PRN · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-39.2, S-63.4, S-54.8, q39 re-run · **Files:** q39 (re-run), q54, q63

- **Quality rule.** Free content and free offers must be as good as or better than competitors'
  paid products. "Give away your secrets, sell the implementation." If prospects see your free
  content beats competitors' paid courses, they assume your paid service is far better.
- **The 99% rule** (re-run q39). 99% of the market will never buy from you, but they make or
  break your reputation on the quality of your free stuff. Shallow "sucky fluff" makes prospects
  assume the paid offer is terrible.
- **Give until they ask.** Deposit goodwill by giving value until prospects reach out to ask how
  to pay you. Reciprocity makes people feel unreasonable receiving value without returning it.
- **The prospect's thought** (q54): "If their free stuff is this good, imagine what their paid
  stuff is like." Example given: a 5-minute video that solves a real problem now.
- **Legal drug dealer** (re-run q39, q38). Give the highest-potency value up front, the "first
  hit", so prospects get an immediate success and come back to pay for the ongoing or full
  solution.

## Seven free-offer models

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** mostly local gym and fitness, one info-product example · **Signals:** S-49.4, S-39.3, S-39.4 · **Files:** q39 (re-run), q49, q56, q57, q58, q59

The answers call these seven "attraction" or "ethical free" offer structures:

1. **Free Bribe.** A high-value program given free as a bonus for joining a lower-cost recurring
   membership. Frank Kern gave $12,000 worth of courses free to anyone joining his $397/month
   newsletter. Re-run q39 adds a free $1,000 challenge when signing up for a $200/month gym
   membership.
2. **Limited Free Offer (the A/B close).** A restricted trial (q49: 28-day trial, 1 workout a
   week, no nutrition plan). At onboarding, upsell 3 out of 4 (75%) to a paid 28-day challenge at
   $199 to $299 with full workouts, meal plans and accountability (q58 adds 3x/week coaching).
3. **Free Trial + Penalty (the downsell).** If they refuse the paid upsell, a free trial with a
   credit card down and a $50 penalty for missed sessions or no-shows to accountability calls.
4. **Free with Deposit (challenge model).** A $500 deposit up front for a 6-week challenge. Hit
   the goal (lose 20 lbs, or 10% of body weight) and the deposit is 100% refunded or credited
   toward a long-term membership.
5. **Free Forever (secondary monetisation).** The core service is free forever; the business
   monetises a required high-margin product. Weight-loss clinics give consultations and coaching
   free and require their own supplements, which make 95% of clinic revenue.
6. **Free Giveaway.** A high-value contest: a $25,000 transformation challenge (q49 gives a $300
   entry fee) or a month of free meals (re-run q39). Every entrant self-identifies as a qualified
   lead; non-winners get runner-up discounts on core programs.
7. **Free with Commitment.** First month or first 28 days free in exchange for a 9-month or
   12-month contract.

Notes:
- q58 prices types 1 and 7 as "$0 entry attached to a $199/month to $397/month recurring
  contract". The $199 lower bound appears only there.
- Every model attaches money or a commitment to the free thing: a recurring fee, an upsell, a
  card on file, a deposit, a required product, a contract. None is a free service with no
  condition.
- Origin: these names match the $100M Money Models attraction-offer list, which is not in this
  layer. The q39 citations for the block run [204, 208–216] in the first run and [207–221] in the
  re-run, out of range for the rest of the answer. Pass 2 §7 lists Money Models as the top gap.

## When free works and when it fails

**Type:** WRN · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-39.5, Pass 2 §4 row 12 · **Files:** q39 (re-run)

The re-run q39 table, which recovered the part the first q39 answer lost (S-39.5 was `[A]`):

| Area | Works when | Fails when |
|---|---|---|
| Monetisation | Backed by a proven backend: upsells, continuity, high-ticket back end | Run with no backend process, so lead flow turns into effort spent on non-buyers |
| Problem scope | It solves a narrow first problem that uncovers the next one, which the core product solves | It gives away the entire core solution, so the client no longer needs to buy |
| Lead qualification | Filters, dropdowns or applications separate qualified prospects from tire-kickers | It is open to unvetted leads, filling operations with freebie seekers |
| Hidden costs | The friction, time and effort asked of the recipient stay minimal | The hidden costs (time to learn, onboarding friction, effort) exceed the value of the free offer |
| Pricing context | It is a front-end lead magnet while the core offer keeps high, premium prices | It is confused with lowering core prices, which cuts margins, leaves churn the same and ruins positioning |

The last row ties to his price cut from $800/week to $600/week, which left churn unchanged and
cost $5,000,000 a year in profit (S-62.1).

## Free trials: the warning against unconditioned trials

**Type:** WRN · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** local gym, B2C, buying traffic · **Signals:** S-56.7, S-56.8, S-49.8, S-71.2 · **Files:** q56, q49, q71

Against generic, unconditioned free trials and low-barrier offers for service businesses (q56):
1. They attract unqualified freebie seekers with no budget or intent.
2. Zero skin in the game means no-shows, poor adherence and no transformation.
3. Cash-flow math: competitor gyms ran $21 for 20 days, collected almost no cash, waited 3 weeks
   to pitch a $99 to $199/month membership and took 3 months to break even on ad costs.

Free trials work only with friction or a built-in monetisation path (q56):
1. Free Trial + Penalty ($50).
2. Limited Free Offer / A/B close (75% upsold to $199 to $299).
3. Deprivation trials (4 months free, see "Deprivation").
4. "Try before you buy": a 7-day trial before billing, like Amazon Prime. q56 labels this item
   "Layaway", which means paying before delivery, the opposite. Mislabel or a blend.

Caution: q56 adds "a 22.4x cash collected advantage and an 11.2:1 ROAS vs a 0.5:1 ROAS" to the
gym comparison. Those figures belong to the ad agency example (S-48.4), so the answer blends two
sources. It also counts $21 for 20 days, a paid offer, as a free trial.

## Lead magnet: definition and the three types

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-38.1, S-38.2, S-38.3, S-38.20 · **Files:** q38, q39 (re-run), q65

A lead magnet is a complete solution to a narrow problem. Its job is to get strangers to raise
their hands, give contact information and become engaged leads. q65 cites $100M Leads part 2
and the video "Watch This To Generate 1000s of Leads (In Any Niche)".

1. **Reveal a problem (diagnosis).** Show a hidden problem, or quantify how bad it is, so it
   needs fixing now; the best ones get worse with time. Examples: a free website speed test
   (re-run q39: a 3-second delay that loses 18% of conversions), posture analysis, a free termite
   inspection.
2. **Samples and trials.** Short access to the core offer, limited by time or usage, to build
   habit. Examples: Costco food samplers, software trials (q38: 7-day; re-run q39: 14-day), a
   free chiropractic adjustment.
3. **One step of a multi-step process.** Solve the first step so the next steps are needed.
   Examples: 1 free coat of garage sealant out of 3 coats needed for weather protection, the
   other 2 sold as a bundle; 1 free laser hair-removal session of a 6 to 8 session plan; a single
   free alignment or posture chart that shows long-term relief needs a care plan; a free colour
   analysis for wardrobe styling (re-run q39 only).

The clothing-store dressing room does all three at once (q38): you try clothes on (trial), find a
size does not fit (problem revealed), and are asked about matching pants (step 1 of an outfit).

## Lead magnet: never solve the core problem

**Type:** WRN · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-38.5, S-38.6, S-38.7, S-37.2 · **Files:** q37, q38, q39

A lead magnet must never solve the core problem the paid offer solves. After a large entree,
offering another entree fails because the diner is not hungry; you offer dessert. The lead
magnet solves the "entree" so the prospect is at maximum deprivation for the paid "dessert".

Three analogies:
- **Salty pretzel.** Bars give free salty pretzels; they solve hunger and create thirst, which
  the paid drinks solve.
- **Costco sampler.** A bite of teriyaki chicken lets customers taste the quality and want the
  whole box.
- **Legal drug dealer.** Give the "first hit free"; when the relief is taken away, deprivation
  makes them pay for the core offer. The stronger the free sample, the more they get hooked.

Price: free or very low cost (at cost), so the lead invests time before money.

Problem-solution cycle: each solved problem reveals the next, which the core or a later upsell
solves (q65 cites $100M Leads part 2).

Pass 2 §4 row 13: this rule and the four-months-free agency example (see "Deprivation") both
stand. The four-month case works by the leads stopping, and is a reported example, not a rule.

## Audit or problem reveal: the CAC math

**Type:** NUM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** local service businesses · **Signals:** S-56.3, S-70.2, S-70.5, S-70.6, S-70.7 · **Files:** q56, q70, q39 (re-run)

An audit, assessment or diagnostic is a lead magnet of the "reveal a problem" type. It works best
when it reveals a problem that gets worse while the prospect waits; quantifying a compounding
problem creates deprivation and urgency.

**CAC math.** q56: if 1 hour of labour costs you $25 on a service worth $250, and 1 in 4
recipients buys the core offer, your CAC is $100. q70: a diagnostic with $25 hard labour cost and
$250+ perceived retail value, given free to 4 qualified leads, costs $100; if 1 of 4 becomes a
client, CAC is $100, profitable if the core offer is worth much more.

**Website speed audit.** q70: a site loads in 9 seconds against 3; every second costs 3% in
conversions, an 18% loss, which makes the web service look cheap next to lost revenue. The
re-run q39 words it as a 3-second delay losing 18%. Whether the 3% per second is his figure or an
industry statistic is not stated.

**Other diagnostics** (q70): financial and tax audits exposing back taxes or cash-flow leaks;
posture and termite inspections; chiropractic wrappers "Free X-ray and treatment" and "$2,000
off / free initial examination". Neil Patel's URL tool: an instant SEO report for contact
details (q38).

**Who gets one** (q70, q72): never everyone. Only highly qualified leads with budget,
decision-making authority, a clear need and intent to act now.

**The line he gives prospects** (q70): it is unethical to give a solution or price without an
inspection. "If anyone gives you an answer on the first call without looking under the hood or
doing an assessment first, run the other way because they're just trying to tell you whatever
they can to get you to buy."

q56 places "free audits" over the reveal-a-problem category; "audit" is the question's word, and
financial or tax audits are the only literal audit examples.

## Free with deposit: the $500 challenge and refund versus credit

  membership. Pass 2 §4 row 17: refund in the book, credit in a later video; ordering needs

## First payment: large down payment and layaway

**Type:** NUM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** high-ticket custom services · **Signals:** S-57.6, S-71.3, S-68.5 · **Files:** q57, q58, q68, q71

## Front end: create flow, monetise flow, then add friction

**Type:** PRN · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general; ad-funded for the cash rule · **Signals:** S-49.1, S-49.2, S-49.3, S-59.1 · **Files:** q49, q51, q57, q58, q59

- "Create flow, monetize flow, then add friction." The worst position in business is zero flow.
  The attraction offer casts the widest net; monetisation is built on the back end once flow
  exists. Do whatever manual labour it takes to over-deliver for early clients before
  automating.
- A first offer should either collect high cash on day 1 or lock in long-term recurring
  continuity from the start. A strong front end collects enough on day one to cover ad spend and
  commissions ("liquidating acquisition costs"). This only fits a business buying traffic.
- Attraction offers sell a specific dream outcome with a short time delay ("Lose 20 lbs in 6
  weeks", "Fill your gym in 30 days"). Commoditised offers sell flat access ("$29/month gym
  pass", "$1,000/month ad management").
- The free or low-barrier first step exists to maximise volume, cover acquisition cost and build
  trust. The wealth is made on the back end through continuity and ascension.

## Free or cheap to recurring: activation points and the continuity pitch

**Type:** SCR · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** local gym; online community · **Signals:** S-49.5, S-49.7, S-59.2, S-59.3, S-49.9 · **Files:** q49, q58, q59

**Activation points.** Set milestones where a client proves readiness for the next level (losing
15 lbs, reaching 1,000 subscribers, completing week 4). Do not pitch everyone an upsell at once;
invite them to continuity at the milestone or near the end of the front end.

**Upsell at the point of greatest need, not greatest satisfaction.** Pitching right after the
client is fully satisfied (after a "steak dinner", or at the end of a 3-day event) gets rejected.
Open a loop on the next problem they will hit.

**The continuity pitch**, three renderings, none confirmed verbatim:
- q49: "You've lost your first 15 lbs during this 6-week challenge; now let's get you enrolled
  for the full year so you keep it off forever."
- q59: "You've lost 15 lbs in 6 weeks; now let me get you enrolled for the full year so you keep
  it off for good."
- q58 (uncited, adds "preferred client rate"): "You've lost 15 lbs in these first 4 weeks, which
  is amazing. To make sure you don't lose these results and put the weight back on when the
  challenge ends, let's lock in your full-year membership today at our preferred client rate."

Timing: q58 says around week 4 or 5 of 6; q59 says midway.

**Ascension examples.** A client's hairdresser-education business sold a $2,000 course on one
cutting style (30:1 LTV:CAC), then ascended graduates to a $15,000/year coaching programme (12:1
LTV:CAC); his advice was to put all marketing into $2,000 buyers and operational energy into
ascending them. A low-ticket community at $99/month or $999/year ascends members who hit
milestones into a $10,000+ programme (q59).
