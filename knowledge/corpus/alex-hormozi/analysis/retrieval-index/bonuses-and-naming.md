## Bonus: why bonuses work, "but wait, there's more"

**Type:** MEC · **Prov:** [D] for the knife and the stack · **Scale:** general; infomercial example · **Signals:** S-26.1 · **Files:** q26, q27, q29

- A single offer presented as one lump is perceived as less valuable than the same deliverable split into components and stacked as distinct bonuses.
- "But wait, there's more" (1990s infomercials): anchor the core price first, then lay out bonuses one by one. The price-to-value gap widens until the buyer's resistance "snaps" (the wallet's "rubber band").
- Example: selling 1 knife for $38.95 and layering 37 additional tools. Pass 2 matched the $38.95 knife and the stack to Offers part 6; the 37 appears only in q29.
- A large bonus stack makes the buyer assume the core offer must be worth even more than the bonuses.

## Bonus: what a bonus should be

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-26.3 · **Files:** q26, q27, q29, q31

- **Low-cost, high-value assets:** tools, checklists, templates, swipe files, scripts, pre-made calculators, Excel or software tools, recorded trainings or pre-recorded workshops. High one-time effort to build; near-zero cost to fulfil again (1-to-many).
- **Tools over trainings:** checklists and software beat extra video training because they need far less time and effort from the buyer, so perceived value is higher.
- **High-access or VIP assets,** used selectively: virtual event tickets, 1-on-1 onboarding calls, short-term VIP group access.

Example of a one-to-many asset: his Excel macro sheet, 100 hours to build, then 15 minutes per personalised meal plan.

## Bonus: one objection per bonus, "the one thing"

**Type:** PRN · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general; weight-loss coaching · **Signals:** S-26.7, S-35.5, S-34.5 · **Files:** q26, q27, q34, q35

- Every bonus must address and dismantle a specific objection, fear or limiting belief about why the prospect thinks they will fail.
- A bonus should also solve the next logical problem the customer will hit right after starting, removing friction before it happens.
- **"The One Thing":** often a single unresolved friction point ("I travel too much", "I eat out for business lunches") causes the no. A bonus matched to it removes the reason to refuse.

Source story: an executive refused his weight-loss program because she ate out for lunch every day. He agreed on the spot to write her a custom restaurant guide, closed the sale, and turned the document into a permanent bonus ("Live It Up While Slimming Down Eating Out System", $349 in the stack) that removed the objection for all later prospects.

Warning form (q35): insisting clients follow your process strictly without solving their real-world friction means a single unresolved objection kills the sale. He lost numerous weight-loss sales by insisting clients cook every meal at home.

## Bonus: the six-step reveal checklist

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-30.1 · **Files:** q30

For revealing each bonus:
1. **Benefit title:** a distinct name with the outcome in it.
2. **Relate and explain:** how it relates to their issue, what it is, what you had to do or discover to create it.
3. **Value equation impact:** how it makes the process faster, easier, or lowers effort and sacrifice.
4. **Social proof:** a stat, case study or personal result showing it works.
5. **Paint the future picture** of life after using it.
6. **Price tag and justification** by the time or money it saves.

## Bonus: presenting bonuses, 1-on-1 against group

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-26.9, S-15.2 · **Files:** q15, q17, q27, q28, q29

- **1-on-1:** ask for the sale at full price before mentioning bonuses. If they say yes, reveal the bonuses after they sign up to create a "wow experience" that reinforces the decision and removes buyer's remorse. If they hesitate, use the matching close (next section).
- **Group or webinar:** pitch the core offer, anchor the price, then reveal each bonus one by one to keep widening the value gap.

## Bonus script: the matching close

**Type:** SCR · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** 1-on-1 sales, weight-loss coaching · **Signals:** S-26.8, S-15.3, S-15.4 · **Files:** q15, q16, q17, q26, q27, q29, q30

Steps: (1) ask for the sale at full price first without mentioning bonuses; (2) if they hesitate or object with a specific obstacle, agree with the concern, introduce the bonus that solves that exact obstacle, and ask again.

> "I completely understand [states obstacle, e.g. 'I travel too much']. Tell you what, if we get you started today, I'll throw in our Ultimate Tone-Up While You Travel Blueprint for free to make sure you succeed on the road. Sound fair enough?"

q30's obstacle example: "I travel too much for work". q15's ending: "would that be fair enough for us to get started today?" The answers put a dash after "Tell you what"; exact words need the audio.

Why it works: people find it hard to reject reciprocity; a bonus matched to the obstacle creates goodwill while the core price holds.

## Bonus: never discount the core instead

**Type:** PRN · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-26.2 · **Files:** q26, q27, q28, q29, q35

Never discount the core offer to close a sale. Discounting teaches prospects your prices are negotiable, signals weakness, erodes authority and destroys margin. Keep the core price fixed and stack bonuses (q35 adds "or change payment terms") instead; this closes from strength and goodwill and solves the underlying hesitation.

## Bonus: how bonuses change the value equation

**Type:** MEC · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-27.1 · **Files:** q27, q29

Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood of Achievement) / (Time Delay × Effort and Sacrifice). Bonuses move three of the four:
1. **Cut effort and sacrifice toward 0:** pre-made grocery lists, calculators, swipe files, meal-prep guides, fill-in templates remove the need to figure things out.
2. **Cut time delay toward 0:** ready-to-use tools, quick-start guides, instant-access assets give fast wins.
3. **Raise perceived likelihood:** proof-backed tools, scripts, accountability systems, expert orientation.

The mapping may be Gemini's synthesis from the value-equation chapter and the trim-and-stack example.

## Bonus: other people's products and services (OPS)

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** local business · **Signals:** S-26.4, S-28.2 · **Files:** q26, q27, q28, q29

Partner with non-competing adjacent businesses to include their products or services as free bonuses in exchange for exposure to your clients. They agree because they get free access to high-intent customers (their ideal market) without ad spend. Examples: a local chiropractor donating free adjustments, a pharmacy offering discounts. It makes the offer more valuable at zero cost to you.

**The "Jedi" strategy:** beyond free service bonuses, negotiate a group discount for your clients AND a referral commission for yourself, so a bonus becomes a profit stream.

## MAGIC naming: steps to build a name

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-29.2, S-33.2 · **Files:** q29, q30, q31, q33, q34

1. Select 3 to 5 components; you do not need all 5. Balance brevity and specificity.
2. Combine and order for flow, not strict M-A-G-I-C order.
3. Optional polish with rhyme or alliteration.
4. Apply the formula to every bonus in the stack.
5. Draft several wrappers and run 2 to 3 top contenders in ad campaigns to find the winning control.

The five-step numbering is Gemini's in q29; q30 gives three execution rules. Step 5 assumes paid ads.

## MAGIC naming: result plus time is not a rule

**Type:** WRN · **Prov:** [M*], with the rule question `[A]` · **Scale:** anyone running Meta or Google ads · **Signals:** S-31.1, S-31.2 · **Files:** q29, q30, q31

Question-shaped: no rule says every name must state result and time. Goal and Interval are two of five optional components (pick 3 to 5); Pass 2 confirms from Offers part 7.

What the sources do say: Goal (result) plus Interval (time) tells the prospect how fast the transformation comes and makes the offer more compelling. Caveat: on ad platforms (Meta, Google), pairing a quantifiable claim (specific income, specific weight loss) with a time duration is often rejected as an implied guarantee.
- Non-compliant: "$10,000 in 10 days".
- Compliant: "Make your first sale in 10 days" or "6-Week Stress Release Challenge" (process or non-claim goal plus time).

Use result plus time freely on landing pages, sales calls, emails and anywhere without ad-platform compliance.

## Naming: the wrapper and the container

**Type:** MEC · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general; gym example · **Signals:** S-29.6, S-30.3, S-35.8 · **Files:** q29, q30, q31, q35

- Naming is the "wrapping paper" of the offer. It changes perception and response rate without changing backend fulfillment. It attracts the exact avatar, separates the core service from commodity competitors, and restores conversion when campaigns fatigue, so you can restimulate demand without changing delivery.
- The Container word names the core mechanism. It turns a standard service into a proprietary "category of one" system that cannot be price-compared to local competitors.
- Warning form (q35): selling the deliverable or feature (a 1-hour workout, a gym membership) instead of the destination. Selling access or time commoditises the offer and invites direct price comparison with local competitors.

## Enhancing: is it the offer, the traffic or the sales process

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-32.1, S-32.2, S-32.3 · **Files:** q32

Levers ranked: Starving Crowd (market) > Offer strength > Persuasion / sales skills.

| Problem | Symptoms |
|---|---|
| Traffic or marketing | people do not click ads or respond to outreach; wrapper, headline, creative or targeting fails to capture attention |
| Sales process | leads not followed up quickly, calls not booked, reps not showing up or not following scripts |
| Offer | low close rate despite qualified leads showing up; prospects compare you to cheaper alternatives ("Gym B is $29/month"); high persuasion needed to close in a normal, pain-ridden market |

The three-way diagnosis reads as Gemini's assembly from scattered points; treat it as synthesis.

> "If your offer doesn't work, it doesn't mean you suck. It means your offer sucks."

A true Grand Slam Offer sells even when presented by a mediocre salesperson.

## Enhancing: funnel metrics to watch

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** paid-traffic funnel · **Signals:** S-32.4, S-33.4 · **Files:** q32, q33, q35

1. **Response rate / click-through rate:** initial appeal; a strong wrapper raises it.
2. **Booking and show rate:** lead quality and commitment.
3. **Closing / conversion percentage:** switching from a commodity offer to a Grand Slam Offer typically raises close rate by "2x to 3x" (q32); q33 says "2.3x to 2.5x" and gives 16% to 37% (2.31x). The 2x to 3x range may be Gemini's generalisation of one case.
4. **Upfront revenue and return on ad spend:** for example ROAS from 0.5:1 to 11.2:1.

The one case behind the numbers (agency, $10,000 ad spend): commodity offer $1,000 down + $1,000/month retainer, 5 clients, $5,000 revenue, $5,000 lost upfront, 0.5:1 ROAS. Grand Slam Offer: response rate 0.13% to 0.33% (2.5x), close rate 16% to 37%, $112,000 cash upfront, 11.2:1 ROAS, 22.4x more upfront cash. Whose agency is not stated. Full table: pricing-and-tiers.md, "Agency offer math".

## Enhancing: order to vary a fatigued offer

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** paid-traffic business with campaigns · **Signals:** S-32.5 · **Files:** q26, q30, q32, q33, q34

Lightest (least operational drag) to heaviest:
1. **Creative:** ad images and videos.
2. **Body copy:** ad text, hooks, stories.
3. **Headline / wrapper (name):** rename, fulfillment identical; for example Free 6-Week Lean Down Challenge → Holiday Hangover to New Year, New You.
4. **Duration:** for example 6 weeks → 28 days or 8 weeks.
5. **Enhancers:** scarcity, urgency, bonuses, guarantee.
6. **Monetization / core model:** pricing or fulfillment, last resort.

Never change backend fulfillment or core pricing first. Consistent across five files. Steps 1 and 2 assume ads.

## Testing: local market reach cost, $20 per 1,000

**Type:** NUM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** local brick-and-mortar market of 200,000 people · **Signals:** S-33.3 · **Files:** q33

In local brick-and-mortar markets, reaching the target population costs roughly $20 per 1,000 people. A 200,000-person local market needs around $10,000 to reach once across platforms. Reaching an audience once does not mean an offer is fatigued; it takes repeated impressions with varied creative.

Arithmetic: at $20 per 1,000, 200,000 people is $4,000 for one impression each, not $10,000. The $10,000 may assume several impressions or several platforms ("across platforms"), or a figure is misheard. US ad costs.
