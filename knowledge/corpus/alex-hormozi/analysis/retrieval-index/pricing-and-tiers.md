## Premium pricing: profit, not customers

**Type:** PRN · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-12.1, S-12.2, S-12.3, S-11.4 · **Files:** q02, q11, q12, q13, q14, q16

- The objective of a business is not to acquire the most customers but to make the most profit.
- Competing on price is a race to the bottom: price can only go down to zero, while it can go up without limit. Lowering prices is "a one-way road to destruction." Stated the same way in four answers.
- Dan Kennedy's principle, cited by him: "There is no strategic benefit to being the second cheapest player in the marketplace, but there is for being the most expensive."
- Charging premium prices is a moral obligation if you care about delivering the best outcome to clients (q12 only).
- Charge premium prices so you can invest heavily in client success.

## Premium pricing: the vicious cycle of lowering prices

**Type:** MEC · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-12.4, S-35.2 · **Files:** q12, q13, q14, q16, q17, q35

Five steps, same order in q12, q13, q14 and q35:
1. **Decreases client emotional investment:** cheap means no skin in the game.
2. **Decreases perceived value:** clients assume a cheap or market-priced service cannot be good.
3. **Decreases client results:** uninvested clients do not act or follow instructions.
4. **Attracts the worst clients:** entitled, high-maintenance bargain hunters who take the most time, complain, are never satisfied until the service is free, cost the most to fulfill, leave negative reviews and demand refunds.
5. **Destroys margins:** no money for top talent, systems, pampering clients or growth, which forces the owner into burnout and an "army of average."

## Premium pricing: the virtuous cycle of raising prices

**Type:** MEC · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-12.5 · **Files:** q12, q13, q14

Mirror of the vicious cycle:
1. **Increases emotional investment:** a price that "stings a little when they buy" forces attention and commitment.
2. **Increases perceived value:** a high price signals exclusivity, luxury, authority and quality.
3. **Increases client results** through adherence and follow-through.
4. **Attracts the best clients:** easiest to satisfy, cost less to fulfill, perceive the most relative value.
5. **Multiplies gross margins:** the capital ("oxygen") for elite staff, scalable tools, world-class support and personal wealth.

## Premium pricing: those who pay the most pay the most attention

**Type:** MEC · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-12.6 · **Files:** q12, q13, q14

"Those who pay the most pay the most attention." In any service where the customer must do work to get the result (fitness, business, learning), their focus decides their success. A higher price forces adherence, which produces better results and better case studies, so raising price raises the value delivered without changing the product.

Limit noted in Pass 1: the mechanism holds where the client's own effort drives the result. A done-for-you service, where the provider does the work, gets less from it.

## Premium pricing: the 10x value-to-price gap

**Type:** PRN · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-13.1, S-02.3, S-17.5 · **Files:** q02, q13, q14, q17

- Warren Buffett, cited: "Price is what you pay, value is what you get."
- Raise prices only after you have raised value.
- Aim for a 10x value-to-price gap: $100,000 of perceived value for a $10,000 price.
- Never charge more than a product is worth, but charge up to 100x more than it costs to fulfill.
- People buy when the value is far above the price, so the premium price feels like "money at a discount."
- An irresistible price is not a discount or bargain; it is a premium price where perceived value far exceeds it (q02; its $10,000 for $100,000 example may be NotebookLM's).

**Quantifiable ROI rule (q17):** charge a fraction of the financial return you produce. $239,000 of extra revenue makes a $42,000 price "money at a discount." That is 17.6% of the gain, about 5.7:1, well under the 10x rule.

q13 offers $4,351 value for $599 as a 10x example; the ratio is 7.3x.

## Premium pricing: copycat pricing and the commodity trap

**Type:** WRN · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general; agency example · **Signals:** S-13.3, S-35.1 · **Files:** q13, q14, q35

Copycat pricing: look at competitors, average their rates and go slightly below. This enforces "market efficiency," where margins vanish and owners work 100-hour weeks to barely stay afloat.

q35's commodity trap: offering "a little more for a little less". Owners copy competitors who are themselves broke; a race to the bottom where winning means working more hours for less pay. Example: the agency at $1,000 down + $1,000/month; identical offers let clients switch the moment a cheaper option appears.

## Discounting: never discount the core offer

**Type:** PRN · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-15.1, S-26.2 · **Files:** q15, q16, q17, q26–q29, q35

"Whenever trying to close a deal, never discount the main offer." Why:
- it teaches prospects your prices are negotiable and erodes authority;
- it comes from weakness, not strength and goodwill;
- it squeezes margins.

"Any fool can sell a product by offering it for a discount. It takes great marketing to sell the same product for a premium." Not attributed to anyone else.

Keep the core price fixed and stack bonuses instead (q35 adds "or change payment terms"); this closes from strength and goodwill.

## Discounting: the 1-on-1 sequence used instead of a discount

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** 1-on-1 sales, his gym examples · **Signals:** S-15.2, S-15.4 · **Files:** q15, q17

1. Ask for the sale at full price without mentioning bonuses.
2. If yes, reveal the bonuses after sign-up to create a "wow experience" that reinforces the decision.
3. If they hesitate or object, agree with the concern, add a bonus that addresses that exact obstacle, and ask whether that resolves it.

Keep the core price fixed; the bonus widens the price-to-value gap until refusal feels illogical. It works because people find it hard to reject reciprocity; a bonus matched to the obstacle creates goodwill while the price holds.

## Raising prices: clean the pipeline first

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general; any business with a lead pipeline · **Signals:** S-16.4, S-23.5 · **Files:** q16, q17, q23, q24

Never raise prices silently. Before the increase takes effect, tell every lead, open proposal and fence-sitter in the pipeline that prices go up on a specific date (q16: for example end of the week or month). Anyone who signs before the cutoff locks in the current rate; after it, all new enrollments pay the new rate. The prospect can still buy later but loses the old price.

Why: it shows strength, uses real urgency, and brings an immediate influx of cash from prospects who were hesitating. q16's only outcome: "an immediate spike in closed deals and upfront cash." It needs a real planned price increase to stay honest.

This applies to prospects in the pipeline, not to existing paying customers.

## Tiers: 20% will pay 5x to 10x

**Type:** MEC · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general; workshop or coaching seller · **Signals:** S-14.1, S-22.15 · **Files:** q14, q22, q25

The one part of the tier material that may be his: demand follows a fractal 80/20 rule. 1/5 (20%) of prospects will pay 5x to 10x the standard price for more access, speed or done-for-you work.

Worked demand math (q22, q25):
- Scenario 1: sell 10 units at $500 each = $5,000 revenue. Satisfies all demand; zero scarcity; zero pent-up desire.
- Scenario 2: sell 2 1-on-1 workshops at $5,000 each = $10,000 revenue. Doubles revenue at lower fulfillment cost and leaves 8 prospects with pent-up demand for the next launch (skimming the top 20%).

It assumes 2 of 10 buyers will pay 10x; a premise, not a measured result.
