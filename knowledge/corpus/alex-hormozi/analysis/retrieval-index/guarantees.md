## Guarantee: why it works

**Type:** MEC · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-04.4, S-19.2, S-19.6 · **Files:** q02, q04, q18, q19, q21

- Risk (fear of failure and financial loss) is the #1 objection to any sale; q04 calls it the single biggest obstacle in sales.
- A guarantee moves the risk from buyer to seller. That raises Perceived Likelihood of Achievement on the top of the value equation, which lets you charge more while making refusal feel illogical.
- Choose it by asking: "What do they desperately NOT want to happen if they pay me?" Then reverse those exact fears.
- State it hard in the pitch and explain why you can afford to make it (q21 only).
- A guarantee adds perceived value by widening the price-to-value gap. Outsized conditional guarantees (buying back a business, double or triple money back, reimbursing hotel, airfare or hourly wages) put an explicit high dollar value on the guarantee itself. Implied guarantees build that value into the price mechanism.

Not covered: selling a guarantee as a separate paid item. The question asked how a guarantee "can be sold or given a price"; the last point above is the closest the answers come.

## Guarantee: the formula

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-18.1 · **Files:** q18, q19, q21

Three parts:

> "If you do not achieve [X result] in [Y timeframe], we will [Z remedy]."

Without the "or what" (the specific Z remedy) a guarantee has no teeth and sounds weak.

## Guarantee: the four types

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [D] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-18.4, S-18.7, S-18.10, S-18.12, S-55.6 · **Files:** q18, q19, q20, q21

Pass 2 matched the book's four types, with variations, to Offers part 6:

1. **Unconditional:** no questions asked; money back for any reason within a set period.
2. **Conditional:** the remedy depends on actions the client completes.
3. **Anti-guarantee:** all sales final, with a reason why.
4. **Implied:** performance-based pricing; the pay structure is the guarantee.

## Guarantee type: unconditional

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-18.4 · **Files:** q18, q19, q21

A no-questions-asked promise. The client pays up front and can get the money back for any reason within a set period, with no work required, like a trial.

- **Use for:** lower-ticket B2C, digital assets, or low-cost fulfillment with high volume, where buyers rarely take the time to refund.
- **Avoid on:** high-cost, high-touch services unless gross margins are very high, because you eat both the fulfillment cost and the refund.
- **Name it** boldly and creatively, not with generic "satisfaction" wording. Generic form for contrast: "30-Day No-Questions-Asked Money-Back Guarantee."

## Guarantee type: conditional

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-18.7 · **Files:** q18, q19

The refund or remedy depends on actions the client must complete. It ties the client to the behaviours that predict success.

- **Use for:** high-ticket, B2B or effort-heavy programs (fitness, business coaching).
- **Tie the conditions to activation metrics**, for example: attending all calls, logging food daily, spending $X on ads, doing 10 sales calls, setting up landing pages, tracking metrics, exercising 3x/week. (The condition list merges q18 and q19.)
- **Frame it as "better than money back":** an outcome or other remedy, not just a refund.

## Guarantee type: anti-guarantee

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general; info products · **Signals:** S-18.10, S-18.11, S-21.2 · **Files:** q18, q19, q21

State that all sales are final and no refunds are given.

- **Use for:** high-ticket, custom or vulnerable offers where the deliverable is consumable, one-time custom labour, or proprietary information or code that "cannot be unseen or returned."
- **It must carry a logical "reason why"** (a damaging admission).
- **Own it boldly in the pitch** instead of hiding it. Selling without a guarantee means using the anti-guarantee openly, not leaving the refund policy unstated. The answers give no case of him selling with no guarantee and no stated policy.

Scripts (wording differs a little across q18, q19, q21; none is clearly the source text):

> Proprietary IP: "All sales are final. We are giving you access to our proprietary checkout code and cold-outreach ad scripts. Once you see these assets, they cannot be unseen or returned. If you are looking for a way out before you start, do not buy."

> Selectivity: "All sales are final. We only work with serious, motivated self-starters who take full accountability and execute. If you need a safety net to try hard, this program is not for you."

> q19 variant: "If you're the type of customer who needs a guarantee before taking a leap, you're not the type of person we want to work with. We only work with motivated self-starters."

## Guarantee math: 100 sales against 130

**Type:** NUM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-18.2, S-18.3, S-19.1 · **Files:** q18, q19, q21

| | Without a strong guarantee | With a strong guarantee |
|---|---|---|
| Sales | 100 at $1,000 = $100,000 | up 30% to 130 at $1,000 = $130,000 |
| Refund rate | 5% (5 refunds) = $5,000 lost | doubles to 10% (13 refunds) = $13,000 lost |
| Net | 95 net sales, $95,000 | 117 net sales, $117,000 |

117/95 = 1.23x, stated as a 23% increase in net profit. A stronger guarantee almost always raises net revenue even when refunds rise. The $1,000 price comes from q19 only.

**Break-even rule (q21):** for a stronger guarantee to lose money, every new sale it brings would have to be fully offset by a matching refund (for example a 5% bump in sales wiped out by a 5% bump in refunds), which rarely happens.

**Conversion claim (q19, q21):** changing only the quality of a guarantee can raise offer conversion 2x to 4x.

Reading notes: the math shows net sales, not profit; it counts no fulfillment cost on the 13 refunded buyers, which a high-cost service must eat. The 30% lift is an assumption in the example, not a measured result, and it does not match the 2x to 4x claim.

## Performance pricing: the five structures

**Type:** FRM · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-20.1 · **Files:** q18, q19, q20, q21

1. **Pay-per-result:** a fixed fee per outcome ($X per sale, $Y per show, $Z per qualified lead, $XX per pound lost).
2. **Revenue share:** a percentage of topline revenue.
3. **Profit share:** a percentage of net or gross profit.
4. **Ratchets, triggers and cash bonuses:** tiered percentages by threshold, or "I get $X when Y occurs."
5. **Hybrid:** performance plus a guaranteed base fee to cover costs.

## Performance pricing: the risks

**Type:** WRN · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-20.8, S-35.9 · **Files:** q20, q21, q35

1. **Tracking and collection:** hard to track the metrics and enforce payment.
2. **Client dishonesty and sabotage:** clients hiding sales figures, telling new members to ask for refunds so the owner can keep them at half price, or taking the collected cash and filing for bankruptcy.
3. **Dying or contracting markets:** even a zero-risk offer fails in a shrinking market (Lloyd's newspapers, down 25% a year).
4. **Fulfillment burnout:** pure done-for-you performance work becomes a "logistical nightmare" that wears out the team unless you move to scalable assets.
5. **Control and counterparty risk (q21):** if the client has bad sales reps, poor operations or messy accounting, you can drive many leads and still be paid zero.

q35: pure pay-for-performance or revenue-share deals without independent tracking software, transparent metrics or control over collections let clients hide sales, misreport revenue or file for bankruptcy, leaving you with zero pay for completed work.

The dishonesty examples read like his own Gym Launch turnaround experience; the answers do not say so. Who the half-price poacher was (a client gym owner or a competitor) is open (Pass 2 row 11).

## Performance pricing: when to avoid or modify it

**Type:** WRN · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general · **Signals:** S-20.9 · **Files:** q20, q21

1. When you cannot verify the numbers yourself or lack direct access to tracking and reporting software.
2. When you do not trust the client to pay, or have no automated card authorisation or other control over collection.
3. When you do not control the close. If the client's staff cannot close, avoid pure performance unless you close yourself or tie payment to metrics you control (shows, booked appointments).
4. During a long setup or ramp-up with heavy early labour or ad spend: add a base retainer or minimum fee (for example $1,000/month).
5. In declining or bad markets.

## Guarantee: when not to use one

**Type:** WRN · **Prov:** [M*] · **Scale:** general; high-cost service; IP products · **Signals:** S-21.1, S-35.6 · **Files:** q18, q21, q35

1. **High-cost, high-fulfillment services** (flights, hotels, intensive 1-on-1 labour, custom software builds): an unconditional refund makes you eat both the fulfillment cost and the refund. Use a conditional service guarantee ("we keep working for free until you hit the goal") or an anti-guarantee.
2. **Easily stolen or consumable IP** (source code, cold outreach scripts, checkout code, ad templates, confidential material that can be copied or used in minutes): avoid refund guarantees. Without an anti-guarantee, buyers download, refund and keep the IP.
3. **Filtering buyers:** unconditional guarantees can attract low-accountability buyers looking for an exit before they put in effort. An anti-guarantee or strict conditions filter out tire-kickers.
4. **A poor product or weak team:** a guarantee cannot mask a bad product or a disorganised fulfillment team; it brings mass refund requests that destroy the business. This point may be NotebookLM's inference; q21's heading says "Poor Product or Weak Sales Team" but the text says fulfillment team.

His own unconditional boot-camp guarantee is consistent with point 1: low cost per head, high volume (Pass 2 row 15).
